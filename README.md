# Prime Factors

What information about two unknown primes can be recovered cheaply from their product alone? This project studies classical integer factorization in Lean 4. A motivating case is a 2,048-bit integer $N=pq$ with two 1,024-bit primes and no supplied relation between them. Progress can be a constructive formula, a proved reduction in the work needed to find a factor, or a proof that a proposed relation leaves too many candidates. [Factor-sum research](discoveries/searching-the-balanced-interval.md) · [General search bounds](discoveries/recursive-integer-valued-windows.md) · [Project progress](https://marius-bughiu.github.io/prime-factors/)

**Can I give you a 2,048-bit number and get its prime factors easily?** No. If you multiply two randomly chosen 1,024-bit primes and give us only the result, we have no practical way to find them in general. We have factored specially constructed 2,048-bit examples. In some we chose primes whose sum was easy to guess. In others we chose primes with a predictable pattern in their binary digits. For each successful example, Lean verified that the answers were prime and that multiplying them gave the original number. That check starts *after* the search has found the answers.

**Does a larger product imply a larger sum of its primes?** The product 32,881 has prime sum 382. The larger product 34,427 has prime sum 372. Both pairs use eight-bit primes. Among full-size products of distinct 1,024-bit primes, even the best rule whose prediction never decreases gets the sum exactly right on fewer than one in $2^{484}$ products. [Five examples and the full-size proof](discoveries/why-a-larger-product-can-have-a-smaller-prime-sum.md)

**Could a single polynomial equation give the prime sum?** Take five primes and form their ten different pairwise products. No quadratic equation in the product and sum can cover more than seven. An equation that reaches seven works precisely by selecting two of the five primes in advance. At full size, even a fixed equation of degree $2^{128}$ holds for fewer than one in $2^{879}$ eligible products. [Exact coverage, approximate predictions and the formulas this rules out](discoveries/how-often-can-a-polynomial-describe-the-prime-sum.md)

**What measurement would give the sum for every pair of 1,024-bit primes?** Count the ordered triples of remainders whose squares add to $-1$ modulo the product. At $N=15$ there are 360. Dividing by 15 and subtracting 16 gives **8**, the sum of 3 and 5. This rule works for all distinct odd primes. At target zero a different rule uses the count modulo $N^2$ and works for balanced primes. Getting either count cheaply from a large product remains open. [How the target changes the answer](discoveries/which-modular-counts-really-add-information.md) · [How accurate a count at zero would need to be](discoveries/what-changes-when-we-count-modulo-the-square-of-the-product.md)

**How does the recent RH work help?** On 697 the first two sphere counts tell us nothing new. A sign test using only 697 picks multiplier 10. The resulting count, 18,696, gives the prime **41**. The released Dirichlet theorem and an explicit least-prime bound show that a multiplier at most 4,030,330 suffices throughout the 2,048-bit range. Computing the large count cheaply remains open. [How to choose an informative count](discoveries/choosing-a-three-square-twist-from-the-product.md) · [What the ordinary sphere count reveals](discoveries/what-counting-points-on-a-sphere-reveals.md)

**Would billions of guesses help if we use the product’s last 161 bits?** Even in the most favorable class, the best list of 4,294,967,296 guesses contains the correct 161-bit sum ending only about once in **3.95 quintillion**. This counts distinct 1,024-bit prime pairs with a 2,048-bit product, using a published prime-count estimate. A five-guess example shows exactly how the count works on small numbers. [What the best list can cover](discoveries/how-many-factor-sums-can-a-list-of-guesses-cover.md)

**Can the product itself give us a sum worth testing?** On 601,049 a single prediction gave 1,590 and recovered **619 × 971**. For 842,713 the same prediction missed. The inputs share their lowest twelve bits and both have ten-bit prime factors. For two 1,024-bit primes, this rule works on fewer than one in $2^{500}$ eligible products. [What makes the same guess succeed or fail](discoveries/when-one-public-square-root-predicts-the-prime-sum.md)

**What if someone gives us the last 168 bits of the sum of the two primes?** For about **255 in every 256** eligible 2,048-bit products, that clue determines the last 160 bits of *both* primes, up to order. The decoder can tell from the product and the clue whether it has enough information. The frequency uses a published prime-count estimate under Dirichlet GRH. Finding those sum bits from an arbitrary product remains open. [How the decoder works and what the examples used](discoveries/how-many-sum-bits-identify-the-factor-endings.md)

**What does knowing part of one prime tell us about the other?** If someone supplies the lowest 160 bits of one prime, about 162 low bits of the sum are fixed on average across balanced 2,048-bit products, under the stated Dirichlet-GRH estimate. We derived a gcd formula for the exact precision on each input. On the independent comparison product it fixes 161 sum bits. On a deliberately constructed example it fixes 320. [Formula, full-size comparison and remaining search](discoveries/how-many-sum-bits-a-factor-clue-really-gives.md)

**Can the product tell us how useful those 160 supplied bits would be?** Read its last three bits. Endings `011` and `111` guarantee exactly 161 low sum bits. Ending `101` guarantees 162. For `001`, the clue fixes at least 163 and averages about 164 among full-size prime pairs with any one specified 161-bit product ending, under the stated Dirichlet-GRH estimate. Even with the clue and all binary tests, an ascending scan still meets at least $2^{577}$ wrong sums on all but fewer than one in $2^{35}$ of **all** eligible prime pairs. This search bound is unconditional. [What the product ending predicts](discoveries/can-the-product-predict-the-value-of-a-factor-clue.md) · [Which wrong sums survive](discoveries/when-more-binary-digits-can-reject-a-factor-sum.md)

**What can we learn from the product alone?** If its remainder modulo 12 is 1, we can list exactly four possible sum remainders modulo 144 or 288. For 527,077 they are 38, 70, 218 and 250 modulo 288. Knowing whether −1 and −3 are squares modulo the product selects the correct answer, 38 in this example. A cheap general way to obtain both answers remains open. [Four-entry formula and verified examples](discoveries/what-two-square-questions-tell-us-about-the-prime-sum.md)

**Is there a formula that actually returns both factors in a special case?** Yes. Two integer fourth roots of quantities derived from 151,847,041 give the prime factors **10,301 and 14,741**. Lean proves exactly which products this formula recognizes. Fewer than one in $2^{1129}$ eligible 2,048-bit prime products qualify. [Formula, example and coverage](discoveries/two-fourth-roots-reveal-a-special-factor-pair.md)

**Can the formula work on more products without a long search?** Multiplying a product by a known fourth power sometimes exposes its factors. On a chosen 58-bit input this gives **362,037,209 × 536,767,369**. For every eligible 2,048-bit product, trying just **1, 725 and 1,517** as denominators gives exactly the same answer as trying all 2,048 possibilities in order. Even the expanded formula covers fewer than one in $2^{1102}$ such products. [Proof, worked examples and scope](discoveries/three-denominators-for-the-quartic-search.md)

**Has the fourth-root formula found two 1,024-bit primes?** Yes. We constructed a 2,048-bit number of the form $1+4y^4$. Given only the product, the formula returned both primes, and Lean certified them. Ordinary Fermat also succeeds on its first midpoint for every positive $y$ in this family. [Full-size example and comparison](discoveries/a-full-size-quartic-example.md)

On a second constructed 2,048-bit input, the same fourth-root reading gives both primes, while ordinary Fermat would need **more than $2^{719}$ consecutive tests**. The construction also leaves a public remainder that lets a modified Fermat search succeed on its first test. [How the two shortcuts work](discoveries/when-a-quartic-reading-beats-consecutive-fermat.md)

**When can we skip the quartic search altogether?** Look at the product’s last four bits. Unless they are `0001` or `0101`, every denominator fails. This skips about three quarters of eligible prime products under a published unconditional prime-count estimate. For those that pass, knowing whether $-1$ has a square root modulo $N$ would select one of two remainders for the prime sum. Finding that answer cheaply from $N$ remains open. [Last-bit test and examples](discoveries/which-products-pass-every-quartic-congruence.md) · [What the answer would reveal about the prime sum](discoveries/one-modulus-and-the-low-bits-of-the-prime-sum.md)

**Would trying vastly more denominators help on an ordinary number?** Even a free choice from every denominator through $2^{512}$ gives these two formulas a success rate below **one in $2^{227}$** over eligible prime pairs. The successful full-size examples prove the family exists. The counting theorem shows how rarely this particular way of reading the product can apply. [Coverage proof](discoveries/how-far-a-rational-denominator-can-extend-the-quartic-formula.md)

**What can the product alone tell us?** For 419,636,024,497 and two 20-bit primes, small remainders reduced 7,273 possible sums to one. That gave 556,559 and 753,983 without a supplied clue. Checking the same primes in a different order saved 21.54% of the lookups. [Small example and proof](discoveries/how-far-public-remainders-narrow-the-sum.md)

**How much work does this save at 2,048 bits?** On our two published examples, seven small primes reduce the number of candidate sums by about 125 and 84 times. Checking the squares of those primes raises the reductions to about **802 and 1,169 times**, measured from the same starting list. Even the smaller search still contains at least $2^{1006}$ sums. With the ordinary prime tests, changing their order also saves about a quarter of the remainder lookups. [Candidate counts and limits](discoveries/when-a-prime-square-rules-out-a-sum.md) · [Lookup costs](discoveries/what-ordering-saves-on-the-full-width-interval.md)

**Can we prove a saving on ordinary 2,048-bit products?** Yes. Across all products of two distinct 1,024-bit primes with a 2,048-bit product, choosing sum guesses by both their location and their remainder saves **more than 2.59% of the exact square tests in total** against increasing order. This counts all slower inputs. The candidates can be generated with at most ten live sums. For all but fewer than one in $2^{35}$ eligible products, even this order needs at least $2^{893}$ tests. The savings proof uses a published unconditional prime-count estimate. The remaining-work bound uses a prime count proved in the repository. [Full-size work comparison and costs on small inputs](discoveries/using-both-the-location-and-remainder-of-a-sum.md#does-the-combined-order-help-across-all-full-size-products)

**Can we cut the square tests further using the product alone?** Six small remainder checks remove **over 99.3319%** of the exact square tests from that ordered search across the full-size population. They cost fewer than **1.5852 small lookups per original square test** in total. A shared 7,035-byte table payload also brings candidate generation below 6.494% of the earlier total. Almost all inputs still need at least $2^{853}$ exact tests. [Proof, costs and a complete small census](discoveries/six-small-checks-before-an-integer-square-test.md)

**Does the product predict which remainder the sum of its factors will have?** To a degree. For five prime squares, the product tells us which of 32 frequency patterns applies. Across all full-width 2,048-bit products of distinct 1,024-bit primes, ranking the sum remainders by frequency saves more than **29.7758% of the guesses** against random order, under a published Dirichlet-GRH prime-count estimate. An optimal list still averages about **25.98 million guesses for one remainder**. Recognizing the correct guess and turning it into an integer factor are additional problems. [What is being guessed, and how the estimate applies](discoveries/how-predictable-is-the-sum-remainder-for-all-prime-products.md)

**Does that saving carry over to guessing the remainder of an actual factor?** For the same five prime squares, ordering saves **less than one quarter of one guess** on average when a guess must name either factor’s remainder. Both orders already use the known product to avoid duplicate guesses. The optimal mean is still about **980 million guesses**, under the same published prime-count estimate. Modulo 25, for example, a single sum guess can cover three different unordered factor pairs. [Comparison and worked example](discoveries/guessing-a-factor-remainder-instead-of-the-sum.md)

**Can the leading and trailing digits of the public product predict that sum remainder?** Even with its lowest 128 bits and 64 more bits after the leading one, the best possible rule still gets one guess right on only about **one in 537,510** full-size prime pairs under the stated estimates. Choosing factor-remainder guesses using the same extra bits still costs about 980 million guesses on average. [Exact counting argument and small examples](discoveries/does-reading-more-of-the-product-predict-the-sum.md)

**What if someone already knows the correct sum remainder?** If they give it to us for free, the mean cost falls to **48.899 guesses for either factor remainder** on that same full-size population. The correct sum remainder tells us which list to search. Recovering that clue from the product, recognizing the correct pair and finding the integer primes are further steps. [Conditional formula and checked finite examples](discoveries/if-the-true-sum-remainder-were-given.md)

**How much extra information does that clue represent?** A helper who knows the factors can send 25 carefully chosen bits and bring the average below 31 guesses for the factor remainders. No fixed-width message of 24 bits or fewer can bring it below 58.9232 in the same model. These are bits the helper supplies. [Counting proof and a two-bit example](discoveries/how-many-supplied-bits-a-factor-remainder-guess-needs.md)

**Can we keep getting the same improvement by taking higher powers?** For any finite set of odd primes coprime to the product, once their squares have been tested, **more than a quarter of the surviving sums still pass** at any higher finite precisions, measured across a complete repeating pattern. We proved this for all such products. The actual factor-width interval can be shorter than a period. [General proof and an example close to the bound](discoveries/how-much-higher-prime-powers-can-save.md)

**Could the search finish before it reaches those wrong guesses?** In the current score order, more than $2^{765}$ wrong sums pass every power of the 117 primes through 643 **before the true sum** on all but fewer than one in $2^{35}$ eligible full-size products. [Population proof and a 28-bit example](discoveries/wrong-sums-before-the-search-can-stop.md)

**Can remainders single out the right sum at 2,048 bits?** Yes. For two 1,024-bit primes, only the correct sum passes every odd-prime check through $2^{940}$ in the public range. This follows from three published estimates without assuming RH. There are more than $2^{930}$ tests. We still need a way to locate the sum cheaply. [Proof, example and cost](discoveries/which-prime-rejects-a-wrong-factor-sum.md)

**How much would an extra clue help?** A mathematical quantity called a signed character total also encodes the sum. For a 2,048-bit product, an estimate of that total with absolute error at most $2^{1053}$ would leave at most 907,200 sums after checking small remainders. We know how to use such a reading and how to verify the factors. Obtaining the reading cheaply from the product alone remains open. [What accuracy buys and how the deliberately noisy example was made](discoveries/how-accurately-to-estimate-the-interval-total.md)

**One square-root guess can now check four relationships between the primes.** It found 1,343 of the 32,385 products made from distinct 12-bit primes, including 919 that ordinary Fermat search missed at the same square-root budget. Fermat found 14,500 in total. On both published 2,048-bit comparison products, Lean verifies that even a trillion guesses from this particular search cannot reach a factor. Those exclusions use the disclosed factors for diagnosis. [Four sign choices, small comparisons and large-input limits](discoveries/checking-both-sides-of-a-square-scale.md)

One 2,048-bit example illustrates the distinction. On the constructed “crossed” example we recovered both primes in 2.21 seconds after choosing a clue by looking at the published factors. We kept the clue fixed and applied it to a freshly generated product. It missed both primes. The construction gives us the answers for verification, and our searches have yet to recover those answers from the fresh product alone. [How we chose the clue](discoveries/testing-one-factor-remainder.md) · [How we generated the fresh comparison](discoveries/certified-fresh-control.md)

We have also proved a limit for one shortcut that worked quickly on a constructed example. If its later power steps use only primes up to 1,000, even trying every starting value from 2 to about two million can find a factor on fewer than one in $2^{177}$ randomly chosen products of two 1,024-bit primes with a 2,048-bit product. [Which shortcut was tested and why changing its starting value rarely helps](discoveries/trying-every-small-base-in-a-predecessor-sweep.md)

For example, take 10,403. The next square is 10,404, which is $102^2$. That gives $10{,}403 = 102^2 - 1 = (102-1)(102+1) = 101\times103$. The same shortcut works even on an enormous product when its two primes differ by two. Two randomly chosen primes give us no reason to expect that pattern.

**How far would the square-root guesses have to go on our fresh comparison?** Past $2^{1079}$ before even the first correct guess. On the crossed comparison the lower bound is $2^{64}$. **The length of the shortest fixed rule for these sums gives the larger prime.** At 77, a rule using the previous four even sums works forever and three positions cannot suffice. **The first change in a sum of signs identifies the smaller prime.** On 77 the change occurs at degree six and gives 7. ## One 512-bit count suffices for a 2048-bit semiprime

Given only an $n$-bit product $N=pq$ of two distinct odd primes, set $k=\lceil n/4\rceil$. Count triples satisfying $1<x\le y$, $xy=N$, and $0\le t<y\bmod 2^k$. The count is exactly the low $k$ bits of the larger prime. A published small-root theorem suffices to recover the prime from that count in polynomial time, even when the factor sizes differ. The quantitative Boolean tensor-contraction theorem gives a paper bound of $2^{O(n/\sqrt{\log n})}$ time and polynomial space for producing the count. This replaces a sequence of prefix counts with one quarter-width count at the same known asymptotic exponent. Computing it on a general 2048-bit input remains open in practice. [Proof, cost and examples](discoveries/quarter-width-count.md)

For an arbitrary integer $N>1$, one unweighted count of $xy=1+kN$ gives $\varphi(N)$ when $0\le x,y,k<N$. Miller's theorem uses this single value and the Dirichlet hypothesis to recover all prime factors in deterministic polynomial time. One weighted multiplication count gives $\sigma(N)$ instead and allows randomized polynomial-time decoding for arbitrary inputs. [Inverse count and RH connection](discoveries/counting-modular-inverses.md) · [Cost of the weighted contraction](discoveries/tagged-contraction-cost.md)

## One circuit can read every bit of the factor sum

Tag each bit on one input of a multiplication circuit, then count the assignments whose product is $N$. One forward and one reverse pass over the tagged arithmetic circuit give the number of factors with each bit set. Every count fits in the field of three elements. The resulting integer sum determines both factors of **any product of two distinct primes**. Lean checks the readout. The cost of constructing the circuit remains the limiting step. On $595489=641\cdot929$, the example used 157,515 compatible row joins to build a circuit with seven reachable arithmetic gates. [Derivation, upstream factoring proof and limits](discoveries/counting-the-factor-sum.md) · [Reproducible experiments](experiments/bit_marginals.py)

## Two square roots factor a structured 2048-bit input

Suppose $N=pq$ with $p\le q\le2p$ and $(2p+1)(2q+1)$ square. Set $a=\lceil\sqrt N\rceil$ and $h=a(a+1)-N$. Then $h=(p+q)/2$ and the two factors are $h\pm\sqrt{h^2-N}$. The search receives just the product and checks its answer. On a constructed 2048-bit input it recovers two 1024-bit primes with zero Fermat iterations. Lean checks the general formula and both primes in the example. A balanced control misses the formula and remains unresolved at the same budget. [Derivation and limits](discoveries/square-collision-density.md) · [Input and control](benchmarks/shifted.json)

A paper argument using prime distribution proves the formula succeeds on infinitely many balanced inputs whose prime gap is a fixed fraction of the smaller prime. A separate counting argument bounds how many such products occur when a gap of order $X/\log X$ suffices. [Infinitude and density](discoveries/shifted-square-infinitude.md)

## A second 2048-bit example from short modular periods

If the prime predecessors share a large divisor $g=\gcd(p-1,q-1)$, raising a unit to $N-1$ produces coprime periods at the two primes. A search given only $N$, a base and a table budget recovered a different 2048-bit product with 86 entries per table. The bound depends on the smaller residual period. [Proofs, costs and prior work](discoveries/common-predecessors.md) · [Product-only fixtures](benchmarks/reduced-orders.json)

How often does this structure occur? For primes chosen in $[X,2X]$ and fixed $0<\alpha<1$, the proportion with $g\ge X^\alpha$ has order $X^{-\alpha}$. For $\alpha\le1/2$ the paper proof also gives the leading constant $\zeta(2)\zeta(3)/\zeta(6)$. The result is asymptotic. [Counting argument and its hypotheses](discoveries/common-divisor-frequency.md)

A third 2048-bit example uses $N^3-1$. All four $\gcd(p\pm1,q\pm1)$ are at most 12, yet a factor of $p-1$ divides $q^2+q+1$ and leaves a local period of 53. Eight entries in each table suffice. The first two exponent choices exhaust that same budget. The constructed input also has a short weighted Fermat route. [Theorem, counting argument and comparison](discoveries/cyclotomic-periods.md) · [Certified examples and controls](benchmarks/cyclotomic-periods.json)

## Conjectures from the Dirichlet L-function proof

One boundary reading at height 1 determines the **exact integer sum** of two balanced primes if its absolute error is below $1/(8\sqrt N)$. For a 2048-bit product, $2^{-1027}$ suffices. A fast way to obtain that reading remains open. [Precision, derivation and alternatives](discoveries/reading-the-boundary.md)

The quadratic character of the product can also be evaluated directly. An interval of $W$ possible quadratic class numbers gives a factor search using $O(\sqrt W)$ class-group operations per auxiliary prime, plus logarithmic initialization. We derive the auxiliary-prime bound under Dirichlet GRH and check two failures of simpler recovery rules. The analytic truncation estimate still costs about $N^{1/5}$ up to logarithmic factors. [Derivation and Lean proofs](discoveries/quadratic-class-number.md)

For $N=pq$ with $p<q$, Lean proves that the first positive imaginary-axis zero of the principal Dirichlet L-function modulo $N$ has height $2\pi/\log q$. More usefully, a sign computed from one L-value tells whether $q$ lies above an integer threshold $x>\sqrt N$. For balanced primes the library proves an absolute error tolerance at each threshold. Efficiently computing that sign using only $N$ is open. [Derivation and work required](discoveries/character-conjectures.md#comparing-a-factor-with-a-threshold) · [Lean proofs](PrimeFactors/DivisorSignal.lean)

A second investigation bounds how many modular square tests suffice for every discriminant of a given bit length. The universal count grows linearly in the bit length under Dirichlet GRH. The tighter conjecture restricting both the number and size of the primes survived exhaustive examples for widths 9 through 20. We also found small widths where it fails. [Proofs, counterexamples and experiment](discoveries/character-conjectures.md#how-many-primes-suffice-for-all-discriminants)

## What reduces the work?

- **A signed modular exponent covers four relations between the primes.** A public Jacobi symbol chooses an exponent that can exploit a large common divisor of any of $p\pm1$ and $q\pm1$. On 24257, the calculation finds 191 from a successor gcd of 64. Dirichlet GRH bounds how far one must search to encounter each sign pattern. [Lean results, success probabilities and RH connection](discoveries/jacobi-separation.md)
- **A full balanced search can skip most odd trial divisors.** An affine congruence between the high digits gives a deterministic $N^{1/3+o(1)}$ method. On a 61-bit input it reported 20,565 modular candidates across 1,048,576 low classes. A 2048-bit input still requires $2^{683}$ low classes with this construction. [Derivation, cost comparison and limitations](discoveries/modular-hyperbola.md)
- **Higher prime powers have a sharp limit as factor-sum filters.** Every power of one odd prime coprime to the product retains at least one twelfth of the possible sum residues. The limit occurs at powers of three. [Published formula, Lean proof and comparisons](discoveries/prime-power-counts.md)

- **The highest product bits can be replaced by exact tests on the candidate factors.** For a balanced product, one interval on $17x+12y$ replaces the five highest product bits. At 2048 bits, 1,025 public intervals can replace 24 bits. A second construction works for any bounded factors and tests their leading bits directly. In small networks it both saved and added work, depending on the inputs and the number of bits removed. [Proofs, counterexample and measured costs](discoveries/truncated-products.md)

- **Choosing a shift has a provable cost.** At factor width $b$, even a list of $2^{b/2}/b^4$ rational functions of the product misses almost all balanced prime pairs as $b$ grows, under polynomial degree and coefficient-size bounds. This unconditional counting result explains why extending the successful two-square-root formula requires more structure. [Paper bound and Lean components](discoveries/large-sieve-for-shifts.md)
- **Several false midpoints can reveal a factor.** Lean gives sufficient conditions for a square-product triple to split the input and checks a split of 723889. Short triples of this kind are absent for most balanced inputs. [Witness, counterexamples and counting argument](discoveries/multiple-discriminants.md)

- **Weighted collisions improve the table bound to $O(N^{1/5})$.** Two different residues modulo the product can agree modulo one unknown prime. Lean verifies this step of Harvey's method and a matching lower bound on the complete tables used here. On a 2048-bit product, those tables still require more than $2^{408}$ entries. [Derivation and example](discoveries/searching-the-balanced-interval.md#collisions-visible-at-one-unknown-prime)

- **One square test can exclude a whole divisor interval.** On the product 34,387, Lean certifies that one test excludes all fourteen integers from 145 through 158. Changing the weights isolates the divisor 137 in another interval. A theorem for every balanced pair bounds the total number of weighted square tests by $2K(K+1)$ when $K$ is chosen near $N^{1/6}$. [Weighted intervals and the global bound](discoveries/searching-the-balanced-interval.md#one-square-test-for-a-divisor-interval)
- **The sum can be found by matching powers.** Every unit $u$ modulo a product of distinct primes satisfies $u^{p+q}=u^{N+1}$. This lets a classical table search combine two indices and check a window of $W$ sums with roughly $\sqrt W$ entries per table. The note gives the cost of selecting the bases, verifying matches and storing the table. The resulting bound is still exponential in the bit length. [Power tables and their assumptions](discoveries/searching-the-balanced-interval.md#matching-powers-to-recover-the-sum)
- **A stronger modulus can settle a small instance.** For 34,387 the moduli 3, 5 and 7 leave three possible even sums. Replacing 3 with 9 leaves only 388. Lean also identifies exactly which residue classes can benefit from this lift. [Prime-power proofs](PrimeFactors/PrimePower.lean)

## From the product to the sum

Write $S=p+q$. Then $S^2-4N=(q-p)^2$. Once $S$ is known, the roots of $X^2-SX+N$ recover both factors. The unknown is how to obtain enough information about $S$ from $N$ to make finding it cheaper.

For distinct 1,024-bit primes, the balance condition puts $S$ in $[2\sqrt N,3\sqrt{N/2}]$. Our research note deduces from the verified Dirichlet GRH result and a published small-prime theorem that **only the true sum** passes the discriminant test modulo every odd prime below three million in this interval. Lean verifies that such witnesses isolate the sum. The cutoff is derived on paper. The unresolved step is to find that unique integer with less work than checking candidates one at a time. [Isolation argument and sources](discoveries/factor-sum-residues.md#a-uniform-set-of-primes-that-isolates-the-sum) · [Finite Lean theorem](PrimeFactors/Isolation.lean)

- **What one prime tells us.** For any odd prime $\ell$ coprime to $N$, a candidate sum $s$ is compatible with the product modulo $\ell$ exactly when $s^2-4N$ is a square modulo $\ell$, including zero. Precisely $(\ell+1)/2$ residues survive when $N$ is a square modulo $\ell$, and $(\ell-1)/2$ otherwise. Every nonzero residue remains possible for an individual factor. [Local criterion](PrimeFactors/FactorSum.lean) · [Exact count](PrimeFactors/ResidueCount.lean)
- **What several primes tell us.** For coprime moduli the numbers of admissible classes per full period multiply. For example, $N=143$ leaves six possible sum classes modulo 35. It still takes a global constraint to distinguish $11+13$ from locally consistent false sums. [Chinese remainder theorem for sums](PrimeFactors/FactorSumCRT.lean)
- **Where the work remains.** A sparse set over a very long period can still be costly to search in a short interval. The research note bounds how much an exhaustive enumeration of complete periods can save and explains why that bound leaves the short-interval question open. [Derivation, proof status, and literature](discoveries/factor-sum-residues.md)

## What the Riemann hypothesis contributes

Alpöge’s November 2026 release establishes RH and zero-location results for Dirichlet L-functions. A theorem of Lamzouri, Li and Soundararajan then bounds the first prime outside a proper subgroup. For semiprimes `n = pq ≥ 3000` with `p < q` and `p − 1 ∣ q − 1`, this limits the bases needed to find a gcd split. For distinct primes of the same bit length (at least three bits), the divisibility hypothesis is impossible. The Lean library proves both this limitation and the arithmetic that extracts a factor when the hypothesis holds. An external Lean 4.36 proof now supplies the small-witness bound. Our Lean 4.19 library checks that its real bound implies an integer cutoff of 2,015,165 on balanced 2,048-bit products, taking the analytic statement as an input. The original RH source has no stated redistribution license and is cited by reference. [Release verification](https://leanprover-community.github.io/blog/posts/riemann-hypothesis/) · [Independent checks](https://math.mit.edu/~drew/grh/) · [Derivation and upstream licenses](docs/research.md)

A second route gives an exact formula. If `n = pq` for distinct primes and its exact divisor sum `σ(n)` is available, set `s = σ(n) − n − 1`. The factors are `(s ± √(s² − 4n)) / 2`. We prove the integer version in Lean. The Robin bound implied by RH is too wide to narrow the elementary factor interval. Computing `σ(n)` fast enough from `n` alone would be the substantive advance. [Lean theorem](PrimeFactors/DivisorSum.lean) · [Verified Robin consequence](https://math.mit.edu/~drew/grh/ArithSolution.lean)

## Reproduce an example

After building the proof library, the example driver requires Python 3.9+.

```sh
python3 scripts/factor.py 10403 --max-steps 1 --verify --lean Factors.lean
```

This produces `[101, 103]`. The generated theorem states `ExactFactorization 10403 [101, 103]`. Replay it with `lake env lean Factors.lean`. You can supply any positive decimal integer, including `1` for the empty factor list. A complete run exits `0`. An unfinished search exits `1` and reports a `remaining` list. An input or checker error exits `2`. `verified` becomes true only after Lean accepts the proof and the axiom audit passes.

To try the 1,050-bit case, read just the input from the fixture. This command supplies neither factor to the search.

```sh
n=$(python3 -c 'import json; print(next(c["n"] for c in json.load(open("benchmarks/subgroup.json")) if c["id"] == "subgroup-1050"))')
python3 scripts/factor.py "$n" --method subgroup --max-base 2 --max-steps 0 --verify --lean Large.lean
```

`--max-steps` counts Fermat midpoints across all splits. `--max-base` sets the inclusive subgroup limit. With no override the subgroup search uses the squared bit length, an integer upper bound for the literature’s `(ln n)²` window. Once a factor is found, small prime stripping and recursive Lucas certificates establish primality where possible. A difficult factor of `p − 1` can leave a cofactor unresolved. `--factor-hint D` lets a separate search program submit a divisor for checking.
