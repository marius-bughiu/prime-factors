# How far can we shorten the sum by regrouping its terms?

Starting with just 667, the terms of the fixed Salié sum fall into 155 groups under negation and inversion. Evaluating one projected term per group gives 155 terms in place of the 616 in the original direct sum. The block evaluator obtains the same rounded value with four trigonometric calls by generating many phases by multiplication. There is a limit even if we allow arbitrary exact rational combinations of the same roots of unity. For every product $N=pq$ of distinct odd primes, the **shortest possible rational expansion of the raw sum has exactly $(p+1)(q+1)/4$ distinct complex phases**. For 667 that is 180 phases. Folding opposite phases gives exactly 90 sine terms for 667, if we know which phases occur. The theorem concerns expansions of the raw sum before the common Gauss normalization. The four-phase formula for the normalized sum already uses the hidden mixed square root of one. ## What can be grouped using the product?

Write $A=2^{-1}\bmod N$, $e_N(s)=\exp(2\pi i s/N)$, and $\chi(x)=(x/N)$ for the Jacobi symbol. The raw sum is
$$K=\sum_{x\in(\mathbb Z/N)^\times}\chi(x)e_N\big(A(x+x^{-1})\big).$$
Inversion preserves the phase and sign. Negation conjugates the phase and multiplies its sign by $\chi(-1)$. Taking a cosine when $N\equiv1\bmod4$ and a sine when $N\equiv3\bmod4$ makes the projected contribution the same throughout each orbit. Represent the pair $\{x,-x\}$ by the smaller positive integer. Compare it with the smaller representative of $\{x^{-1},-x^{-1}\}$ to select one per orbit. All these operations use $N$ alone. Full enumeration still visits $(N-1)/2$ integers and can already meet the divisors in its unit tests. [Product-only orbit and polynomial-block evaluator](../experiments/salie_blocks.py)

Negation fixes no unit, inversion fixes the four roots of one, and negative inversion fixes the $r$ roots of minus one. Here $r=4$ when both primes are one modulo four and $r=0$ otherwise. Averaging the four fixed-point counts gives $O=(\varphi(N)+4+r)/4$ orbits. The count contains enough information to factor: try $\varphi=4O-4$ and $\varphi=4O-8$, then $S=N+1-\varphi$ and the square test for $S^2-4N$. Because $O$ itself requires work, this gives a target for an implicit counting method.

## Minimum number of complex phases

Allow any rational numbers $C_s$, including negative ones, and any phases among the $N$th roots of unity. Combine repeated phases and count the nonzero coefficients in
$$K=\sum_{s\bmod N} C_s e_N(s).$$
The optimizing representation can use the factors. The same result holds for any unit $A$ because changing $A$ permutes the phase labels. This applies to the fixed sample and to every diagonal sample at a unit frequency.

To obtain the upper bound, first group by the common trace $v=x+x^{-1}$. At a prime $l$, put $u_l(v)=\sum_{x+x^{-1}=v}(x/l)$. The nonzero values occur at $(l+1)/2$ labels. Inversion partitions the $l-1$ inputs into two singleton orbits $\{1\},\{-1\}$ and pairs. Distinct orbits have different traces because their members are the roots of $X^2-vX+1$. Both members of each pair have the same sign. CRT now makes the raw coefficient matrix $u_p(i)u_q(j)$ after relabeling the phases, so it has exactly $(p+1)(q+1)/4$ nonzeros. Four times this support is $N+p+q+1$. An exact count of the occupied trace labels therefore determines both factors. [Lean proof of the trace count](../PrimeFactors/SalieOrbit.lean)

For the lower bound use coordinates in which the basis phases are $\zeta_p^i\zeta_q^j$, with $\zeta_l$ a primitive $l$th root. The relabeling from the trace coordinates multiplies each index by a unit and preserves the local coefficient multiplicities. Over $\mathbb Q$, the kernel of evaluation on these $pq$ coordinates consists exactly of matrices $a_i+b_j$. Each complete row or column sums to zero. The space of their linear combinations has dimension $p+q-1$, since a function both row-constant and column-constant is constant. Evaluation spans $\mathbb Q(\zeta_{pq})$ of dimension $(p-1)(q-1)$, giving the same kernel dimension. Thus the minimum is an explicit problem about row and column shifts of the coefficient matrix. [Lam and Leung, Theorem 2.2, on the relations among roots of unity](https://arxiv.org/abs/math/9511209)

The local multiplicities are as follows, with all entries counting labels $v$.

| Prime $l$ | $u_l=0$ | $u_l=1$ | $u_l=-1$ | $u_l=2$ | $u_l=-2$ |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| $l\equiv1\pmod4$ | $(l-1)/2$ | 2 | 0 | $(l-5)/4$ | $(l-1)/4$ |
| $l\equiv3\pmod4$ | $(l-1)/2$ | 1 | 1 | $(l-3)/4$ | $(l-3)/4$ |

The singleton weights are $1$ and $(-1/l)$. The other occupied bins have weight $2$ or $-2$. Their multiplicities follow from the fact that the sum of all the weights equals the sum of the nontrivial quadratic character on the units, hence zero.

Fix the column shifts. For any two rows with the same slope $u=u_p(i)$ we may choose a common row shift achieving the greatest number of zeros for that slope. After doing this, choose a common best column shift for each value $v=u_q(j)$. These choices can only reduce the number of nonzeros of a given representation, so an optimum exists with equal shifts within each class. Translate vertically so the line for slope zero is the horizontal axis. Every other row is a line of slope $u\ne0$ with a unique intersection $t_i$ with that axis. All rows of one slope are now identical. At a column label $v$, the column shift chooses a height, and the number of zeros there is the number of lines meeting that height, counted with multiplicities.

Swap the primes if necessary so $q\ge7$, leaving only $3\cdot5$ as an exception. Write $A_0=(p-1)/2$. There are $A_0$ horizontal rows and $A_0+1$ nonhorizontal rows, including at least two different slopes. Write $w(v)$ for the column multiplicity, taking it to be zero off the table. Its maximum $H=(q-1)/2$ occurs at zero. Every $v\ne0$ has $w(v)\le H/2$ by the table.

At height zero, the matches are $A_0+\#\{i:t_i=v\}$. At any other height, there are at most $A_0$ matches unless **all** nonhorizontal rows coincide there. Two distinct slopes can meet only once, so at most one column label $e$ can be exceptional. Except for this possible single bonus the total matches are bounded by
$$qA_0+\sum_i w(t_i)\ \le\ qA_0+(A_0+1)H.$$
At an exceptional label the bonus is at most $w(e)$. It fits in the same bound. If $e=0$, none of the $t_i$ is zero, and two rows already leave a deficit of at least $2(H-H/2)=H$. If $e\ne0$, two lines of different slopes coinciding off the horizontal axis at $e$ must have different axis intersections. At least one such intersection is nonzero and leaves a deficit of at least $H/2\ge w(e)$. Therefore at most $qA_0+(A_0+1)H$ zeros are possible. Subtracting from $pq$ gives precisely $(p+1)(q+1)/4$. [Lean bounds for intersections, exceptional columns and the total number of matches](../PrimeFactors/SalieOrbit.lean)

For $3\cdot5$, the three row slopes are $-1,0,1$ and the column coordinates $-2,0,1$ have multiplicities $1,2,2$. If all three lines meet at one coordinate, the other coordinates have only singleton groups, giving at most $5+2\cdot2=9$ matches. Otherwise the three pair intersections, when distinct, form an arithmetic progression: the outer slopes intersect at the midpoint of the other two intersection coordinates. The set $\{-2,0,1\}$ contains no three-term progression. At most two column coordinates contribute a match above the baseline one, again giving at most nine zeros. Six nonzeros are necessary and attained.

For a 2,048-bit product the raw exact rational expansion requires more than $2^{2045}$ complex phases. The minimum number of nonzero basis terms in the projected sine or cosine expansion is exactly the ceiling of $(p+1)(q+1)/8$. Each cosine for a real raw sum, or imaginary sine for a purely imaginary raw sum, accounts for at most two complex phases with rational coefficients. The trace histogram attains this ceiling by grouping opposite phases: its coefficient at $-s$ is $\chi(-1)$ times its coefficient at $s$, and only zero can be its own opposite. Numerical approximation, changes of scalar field or basis, multiplication of shorter expressions, transforms and implicit evaluation remain open approaches. For example, the classical Gauss normalization itself changes the rational representation problem. The four-call block evaluator illustrates the distinction between expansion length and the work of evaluating an expansion. Its remaining costs include visiting the unit orbits and combining their weights. [Block evaluator](../experiments/salie_blocks.py)
