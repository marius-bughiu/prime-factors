# What a successful choice of power would reveal

Can we improve a factoring search by raising its starting number to a carefully chosen power? On our independently generated 2,048-bit example, a power chosen using its known primes makes a previously rare comparison succeed **three times in seven**. There is a catch that we can prove. For this particular search, even a power that succeeds just **once in $2^{503}$** would already encode enough information to recover the factor sum in another way, provided the power is between 1 and the product. We have no method for choosing such a power from the product alone. ## How much can changing the power help?

Fix $N=pq$, choose $u$ uniformly among the residues coprime to $N$, then use $u^E$ as the starting number. The exponent $E$ must be fixed before drawing $u$. We retained multiplier cap 64 and two positions per row. On the fresh product the only orders that give a factor during the comparison **after the preliminary checks** are $(7,8)$ and $(14,8)$ at the smaller and larger primes. The crossed input has no such pair for any starting number, including any powered one.

Write $P=p-1$, $Q=q-1$, $X=P/\gcd(P,E)$, $Y=Q/\gcd(Q,E)$. The two powered residues are uniform in cyclic groups of sizes $X$ and $Y$. A group contains six elements of order 7 if $7\mid X$, six of order 14 if $14\mid X$, and four of order 8 if $8\mid Y$. The number $C$ of winning pairs in this image is therefore 0, 24, or 48. The success probability is $C/(XY)$ and never exceeds $3/7$. Taking $E=\operatorname{lcm}(P/7,Q/8)$ on the fresh product gives $X=7$, $Y=8$, and 24 successes among 56 image elements. Another 28 elements give a factor in the preliminary checks. The 24 successes in the later comparison would all have given the smaller prime with the single test $\gcd((u^E)^{14}-1,N)$. On the fresh input the public exponents $1$, $N-1$, and $(N-1)/2$ all gave a comparison success probability below $2^{-2040}$. Using the least common multiple of the integers through 64 or through 256 made that probability zero. Our count includes every original starting number. The preliminary checks can still succeed. For all five public choices on both inputs, their probability was below $2^{-415}$. ## Why a successful exponent gives away the sum

Let $F=(p-1)(q-1)$. The identity $p+q=N+1-F$ turns a value for $F$ into both primes by solving a quadratic. Consider the fraction $E/F$ in lowest terms with denominator $b$. The sizes of the kernels of the two power maps divide $E$, and their own greatest common divisor divides $\gcd(P,Q)$. Consequently $b$ divides $\gcd(P,Q)XY$. For this control $\gcd(P,Q)=2$. If the comparison succeeds on at least one in $2^{503}$ uniform seeds, then $XY\le48\cdot2^{503}$. [Denominator theorem](../PrimeFactors/PowerImageDenominator.lean)

The known width of the primes bounds the difference $N-F=p+q-1$. Together the inequalities give $2b^2(N-F)<F$. When $0<E\le N$, it follows that $E/N$ and $E/F$ differ by less than $1/(2b^2)$. Legendre's theorem then places the exact fraction $E/F$ among the continued-fraction convergents of the known ratio $E/N$. If this convergent is $a/b$, then $F=Eb/a$. A candidate value can be checked by multiplying the two integer roots it predicts. The Euclidean algorithm gives the finite list of convergents. [Full-width inequality](../PrimeFactors/PowerImageWidth.lean) · [Lean proof using Legendre’s theorem](../PrimeFactors/PowerImageDenominator.lean)

For the example chosen with both primes visible, the second convergent of $E/N$ is $1/112$ and $F=112E$ exactly. None of the five tested product-only choices had its exact $E/F$ on the convergent list for either full-size input. The threshold is a sufficient condition under the stated assumptions for this comparison stage. It gives no new bound on the work required to construct an exponent meeting it. The argument uses finite group arithmetic and continued fractions. [Reproducible evaluation](../experiments/powered_trace_population.py)


The same denominator argument applies to any search with a suitably small set of successful powered values. A separate public remainder check can discard a proposed exponent without looking at any powers or known factors.
