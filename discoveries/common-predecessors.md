# Short periods from a common predecessor divisor

Let $N=pq$ for distinct primes $p<q$. Only the product is given. Write $g=\gcd(p-1,q-1)$, $r=(p-1)/g$ and $s=(q-1)/g$. The hidden periods $r,s$ are coprime. The method below finds a factor in expected $O(\sqrt r\,\operatorname{polylog}N)$ bit operations, using geometric polynomial evaluation with fast integer multiplication. It can choose its search width without knowing $p,q,g,r$ or $s$. For balanced factors, $g\ge N^{1/10+\delta}$ gives an exponent at most $1/5-\delta/2$ before logarithmic factors. Small $g$ can still leave exponentially large tables. The comparison is with the deterministic one-fifth bound; it makes no claim of priority or improvement over every factoring algorithm. [Balanced one-fifth literature](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.19076)

Galbraith and McKee already describe the square-root dependence on $p/g$ for unknown $g$, via a Pollard rho iteration and a random-map heuristic. They also discuss a faster bound when a common divisor is supplied. Here the interval of exponents guarantees a collision for every nonidentity $v$ once the table reaches the stated width. The expected bound uses only the exact random-base count. [Galbraith and McKee, §5](https://www.math.auckland.ac.nz/~sgal018/pair-mod-n.pdf)

## Why a short period gives a factor

Pick a uniform unit $u$ modulo $N$ and compute $v=u^{N-1}\bmod N$. One can sample integers in $[1,N-1]$ and take a gcd to obtain a unit or an immediate factor. Since $N-1=(p-1)q+(q-1)$, its gcd with $p-1$ is $g$, and likewise at $q$. In the cyclic unit groups modulo the primes, the orders $R_p$ and $R_q$ of $v$ therefore divide $r$ and $s$, respectively. In particular, $R_p$ and $R_q$ are coprime. The equation $u^{N-1}=1$ has exactly $g^2$ solutions among the $(p-1)(q-1)$ units by CRT. Discarding this observable case costs an expected $1/(1-1/(rs))\le 2$ trials. These order bounds and the exact count are Lean theorems. The classical Fermat-liar count and single-exponent gcd splitting already appear in the literature. [Lean proofs](../PrimeFactors/ReducedSlopes.lean) · [Beauchemin, Brassard, Crépeau, Goutier and Pomerance, pp. 56–58](https://math.dartmouth.edu/~carlp/probprime.pdf#page=4)

For a stride $m$, form the babies $v^i$ for $0\le i<m$ and giants $v^{mj}$ for $1\le j\le m$, all modulo $N$. The differences of exponents cover every positive $d\le m^2$. Once $m^2\ge r$, at least one pair agrees modulo $p$, since $R_p\le r$. Increasing $m$ by powers of two reaches this point with $O(\sqrt r)$ entries in total.

* A match modulo $N$ supplies $1\le k=mj-i\le m^2$ with $v^k=1$. Try as exponents both $z$ and $k/z$ for each divisor $z\le\sqrt k$. If $v\not\equiv1\bmod q$, the divisor $R_p\mid k$ works because $R_q>1$ is coprime to $R_p$. Otherwise exponent 1 works, since $v\ne1\bmod N$. A gcd of $v^e-1$ with $N$ recovers the factor. This uses at most $m$ trial divisions; modular exponentiations at successful divisors add at most a logarithmic factor per divisor in ring operations. Lean checks the separating-divisor argument and the complementary-divisor bound.
* If there is no whole-modulus match, form $F(X)=\prod_{j=1}^m(X-v^{mj})$. Compute $\gcd(F(v^i),N)$ for each baby. A proper gcd finishes. If a gcd is $N$, inspect that one baby's differences from each giant. Some difference has a prime factor in common with $N$, and each difference has distinct representatives modulo $N$. At most $m$ individual gcds are needed. This is the batched detection step in Harvey's Algorithm 4.1. [Harvey, §4](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2010.05450#page=8)
* If all the gcds equal 1, the product of all the differences is a unit modulo $N$. Lean proves that this certifies $r>m^2$. In particular $g<(p-1)/m^2<\sqrt N/m^2$. An exhausted table thus proves a quantitative exclusion even when both prime factors remain unknown. [Exclusion theorem](../PrimeFactors/ReducedSlopes.lean)

## Computing the batch

The Lean experiment builds $F$ by a product tree. Writing $F(X)=\sum_{j=0}^d f_j X^j$ and $C(k)=k(k-1)/2$, a single convolution evaluates all the babies up to a known unit. Reverse the coefficient list $f_j v^{C(d)-C(j)}$ and multiply it by the polynomial with coefficients $v^{C(k)}$, for $0\le k<d+m$. Coefficient $d+i$ of this product is $v^{C(i)+C(d)} F(v^i)$ since $C(i+j)=C(i)+C(j)+ij$. Lean proves the scaled evaluation identity. The scale preserves the gcd.

Every polynomial multiplication packs canonical coefficients as digits of a single integer. For operand lengths $a,b$, each output digit before reduction is at most $\min(a,b)(N-1)^2$, so the chosen base exceeds the largest digit. Packing and unpacking split the arrays in halves. With quasi-linear integer multiplication, this gives quasi-linear cost in the table length up to logarithms of $N$ and $m$. Sorting whole matches, bounded divisor enumeration, and refining at most one failed row fit the same bound. This complexity analysis is on paper. The experiment checks the computed convolution values against direct products on five small moduli at each width from 1 through 9. [Executable experiment](../experiments/ReducedOrders.lean)

## Checked examples

The larger fixture is a synthetic 2,048-bit product with two 1,024-bit prime factors. Its construction uses $g=2(363\cdot2^{1001}+1)$, where the parenthesized integer is a 1,010-bit prime. The factors are $7392g+1$ and $9623g+1$. Their ordinary Fermat midpoint is more than $2^{1016}$ steps above the initial midpoint. The experiment takes the decimal product, base 2 and stride 86. The first successful batched evaluation is at baby 2. Giant 43 agrees there modulo the smaller prime, with exponent difference $86\cdot43-2=3696$. The gcd of $v-1$ with the input is 1. There is no supplied factor or shared divisor in the search. This engineered example shows what the extra structure buys. The frequency of this structure among unrestricted balanced semiprimes is a separate question.

The second fixture, $77=7\cdot11$, takes the whole-modulus branch at exponent 15. A single-entry search on 77 leaves both factors unresolved, as does an identity base. The reproducibility command checks both complete factorizations with generated Lean certificates and uses the stored decimal factors only to validate the search output.
