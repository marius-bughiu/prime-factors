# Three equations revealed a factor where the four-row test missed

We gave both tests **22,663** and the same single interval. Since both primes have eight bits, their product and bit lengths put the smaller prime among the eleven odd numbers from 129 to 149. The center is 139. Three of the five equations produced by the reduction met the size bound. Their determinant was **21,971**. Its greatest common divisor with 22,663 was **173**, and division gave **131**. The previous test divides each four-row determinant by $N$ before taking its gcd with $N$. All five of those gcds were one. This shows that the three-equation test can find a factor the previous test misses in the very same window. The new rule still requires a location and suitable equations. On the complete 49-bit comparison it found a factor in the same window as before. The five windows we tested on each 2,048-bit input gave no factor. ## Why three rows can suffice

Write $N=pq$ for distinct primes. Suppose $p=c+sx$, where $c$ is a public center, $s$ is coprime to $N$ and $x$ is the unknown offset. Choose $t$ so that $N$ divides $c+st$. Then $x-t$ is divisible by $p$ and invertible modulo $q$. Each integer quartic $F$ returned by our basis construction satisfies $N^2\mid F(t)$ and $N\mid F'(t)$. If its coefficients are small enough on the given window, then $F(x)=0$ as an ordinary integer. [Window proof](../PrimeFactors/QuarticDivisorWindow.lean)

Reducing the coefficients modulo $q$ gives a double root at $t$ and another root at $x$. Over the field of residues modulo $q$ this makes each such polynomial a multiple of $(X-t)^2(X-x)$. Any quartic multiple is this cubic times a polynomial of degree at most one. There is room for only **two independent coefficient rows**. Thus every three-by-three coefficient determinant from three such quartics is zero modulo $q$. The implementation uses the coefficients of $X^2,X^3,X^4$. If that determinant is nonzero modulo $p$, its gcd with $N$ is exactly $q$. The theorem guarantees the divisibility by $q$; the check against $p$ still matters. The formal coefficient argument works in a commutative ring when $x-t$ is a unit. [Three-row theorem](../PrimeFactors/ThreeRowCofactor.lean)

On 22,663 the coefficients of $X^2,X^3,X^4$ in the three short equations were

$$\begin{pmatrix}146&-24&1\\37&9&7\\70&-17&8\end{pmatrix}.$$

The determinant and gcd use just these nine numbers and the input. The search obtained the rows from the public center and width before checking its answer against the known factors. It needed no polynomial root search. The five four-row coefficients it compared had absolute values 54,894, 21,429, 20,821, 29,329 and 600. All are coprime to 22,663. No determinant was zero or required removing a full factor of $N$. Each full-size comparison tried fifty triples, with no split. A missed gcd leaves that window unresolved by this test. ## Why the four triples together recover the cofactor

In the shifted variable $Y=X-t$, write $a=x-t$. At an occupied window the rows that vanish have the form $(Y-a)R(Y)$. Let $C=R(0)$ and $L=R'(0)$. Since $p\mid a$ and $a$ is coprime to $q$, the original two conditions on the product polynomial are equivalent to $pq^2\mid C$ and $q\mid L$. The remaining coefficients of $R$ are unrestricted. This equivalence holds for arbitrary coprime factors when the stated condition on $a$ is met. [Proof of the coefficient conditions](../PrimeFactors/DivisorWindowQuotient.lean)

If **four** rows of a full integer basis vanish, they generate the entire sublattice of vanishing polynomials. The fifth row has nonzero value, so its coefficient must vanish in any combination with value zero. Dividing by $Y-a$ gives a basis of the lattice described above. Its projection onto the three nonconstant coefficients is generated by $(q,0,0),(0,1,0),(0,0,1)$. Multiplication back by $Y-a$ takes a triple $(B,C,D)$ to $(B-aC,C-aD,D)$ in the three highest columns. This last transformation preserves three-by-three determinants. Consequently the gcd of the four triple determinants is **exactly $q$**. Lean checks the coefficient equivalence and the determinant statement for an arbitrary unimodular quotient basis. The identification of the four quotient rows with that basis uses the generation argument just given. On the 49-bit example the four determinants have gcd 28,141,469. [Determinant-content proof](../PrimeFactors/DivisorWindowQuotient.lean)

## When the three equations share a quadratic

Suppose three rows of a full integer basis are divisible by the same monic quadratic $Y^2-SY+P$. Write $D$ for their determinant in the three highest columns. Lean proves $|D|G=N^3$, where $G=\gcd(N^3,N^2 S,NP,P^2)$. For instance, if $N=pq$, $P=pr$, $q$ is coprime to $r$ and $p$ is coprime to $S$, then $G=p^2$ and $|D|=Nq^2$. These are extra hypotheses. Three short equations in a window containing a factor guarantee one common integer root. A shared quadratic requires additional evidence. [Quadratic remainder theorem](../PrimeFactors/QuadraticJetMinor.lean)

The same four-term gcd counts something for polynomials of any degree. For a nonzero $N$ and an integer polynomial $H$, impose the relations $N^2=NY=Y^2=H(Y)=0$. The number of distinct polynomial classes left is exactly $\gcd(N^3,N^2 H'(0),NH(0),H(0)^2)$. This counts classes in the full polynomial ring. Replacing the basis of five quartics by a different finite list requires a separate proof about the rows available. [Lean proof and representatives](../PrimeFactors/JetQuotientContent.lean)
