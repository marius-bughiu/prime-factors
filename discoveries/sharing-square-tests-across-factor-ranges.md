# How many factor ranges can one square test check?

Sixteen square tests found **1,051 × 2,011 = 2,113,561**. The ordinary Fermat search missed both factors in its first sixteen tests, even when it skipped impossible sums modulo 24. We selected this example after examining 2,424 small prime pairs. On the two 2,048-bit comparison products the same sixteen tests found neither prime. Using the ordinary search would actually have checked more possible factor locations on both. [Inputs, expected factors and exact counts](../benchmarks/shared-multiplier-prefix.json)

## Why the same test works in different places

Suppose we know only the product $N=pq$. Choose a positive integer $k$ and write it as $ab$. The unknown sum $S=ap+bq$ must satisfy

$$S^2-4kN=(ap-bq)^2.$$

Changing how we split $k$ between $a$ and $b$ changes which factor locations correspond to a small $S$. It leaves the square test at any particular $S$ identical. If we test all integers from $\lceil2\sqrt{kN}\rceil$ through $h-1$ and none succeeds, every divisor $x$ with $ax^2+bN<hx$ is ruled out for every allocation of that multiplier. Solving this quadratic inequality gives exact integer endpoints. A global gcd takes just one check after a square hit. For the small example, the trace was 13,520,145 and the square root was 6,225. The gcd of $(13{,}520{,}145+6{,}225)/2$ with the input is 1,051. [Endpoint, exclusion and gcd proofs](../PrimeFactors/WeightedTracePrefix.lean)

Multiplier-based Fermat searches have a long history. Hart explicitly discusses using a highly composite multiplier. Here we measure how much of the possible factor interval one shared calculation can cover, even if every allocation of its multiplier is available for free. The proof and experiment code were written for this project. [Hart, *A one line factoring algorithm*](https://doi.org/10.1017/S1446788712000146)

## What sixteen tests covered

We fixed $k=21{,}621{,}600$ and tried sixteen consecutive integer traces. We determined the feasible smaller-factor range from the product and the equal factor bit widths. We counted each integer position once, merging any overlapping ranges. The comparison methods each get sixteen discriminant square tests as well. The table gives base-two logarithms of position counts, rounded to two decimals. A difference of one means twice as many positions. The 576 allocations gave nine disjoint nonempty intervals on the fresh input and 22 on the crossed input. The exclusion certificates check the sixteen failed square tests once and use the endpoints to prove that every integer in each interval fails to divide the product. Computing the endpoints and listing the allocations has a cost. The reference implementation tries 4,649 possible divisors to enumerate this multiplier, which could be precomputed for later inputs. [Certificate generator](../scripts/weighted_trace_prefix_certificate.py)

Two ranges can also be compared without taking a square root. For allocations indexed by $a\le c$, and the same first omitted trace $h>0$, their open real intervals overlap exactly when $kN(a+c)^2<h^2ac$. In the coordinate $z=ax$ every allocation has the same two endpoints, so each full interval also has the same width on a logarithmic factor axis. When $h^2-4kN\le L^2$, two different integer allocations cannot share a point $x\ge L$. Indeed their scaled points would be at least $x$ apart inside a common open interval of length at most $L$. In particular for $N\le2^{2048}$, $k\le2^{1974}$ and the first $2^{32}$ consecutive integer traces, counts from the different allocations add exactly throughout the 1,024-bit factor range. The certificates can use this regardless of how the multiplier is chosen. [Overlap test and exact additive count](../PrimeFactors/SharedTraceOverlap.lean)

## Letting the product choose a multiplier

On 256 freshly sampled products we let the product choose whichever of eight fixed multipliers covered the most integer positions in sixteen admissible traces. Every input chose ordinary Fermat, including ties. So did both 2,048-bit comparisons. On an independent set of 256, we first tested sixteen ordinary traces, then chose a multiplier to cover as many *additional* positions as possible. That split 108 products versus 105 for continuing with ordinary Fermat. The choice itself required 24 extra integer square roots per input. Giving ordinary Fermat the same total of 56 square roots split 117. On the crossed full-size input, changing the multiplier added 23.4% more total positions at equal discriminant count, but covered 5.4% fewer at equal total square-root count. Both full-size inputs remained unresolved. [Frozen choices, sampling and counts](../research/shared-multiplier-selection.json) · [Reproduce the comparison](../experiments/shared_multiplier_selection.py)

We also tested a cheaper choice. A precomputed table uses the product modulo 24 and up to four integer comparisons to select a multiplier. On an initial 1,024 new products it found 437 pairs and ordinary Fermat found 420 with the same number of integer square roots. We froze the table and tested another 6,144 products with 12-, 16-, 20-, 24-, 28- and 32-bit prime factors. It found 1,733 versus 1,727. Of the products split by exactly one method, 133 favored the table and 127 favored ordinary Fermat (two-sided paired sign test $p=0.757$). The larger comparison gave no convincing evidence of an overall advantage. [Pilot](../research/cheap-multiplier-pilot.json) · [Independent confirmation and exact paired counts](../research/cheap-multiplier-confirmation.json)

One of those fresh products was **802,471,205,089**. The table selected $k=12$ with two comparisons. The first admissible trace at that multiplier, 6,206,339, gave a square with root 5,093 and a gcd of **776,429**. Division gave **1,033,541**. Both searches used 34 integer square roots, and ordinary Fermat missed. On the crossed 2,048-bit input the table covered 22.2% more positions than ordinary Fermat at the same total root count. More than $2^{1021}$ positions remained. On the fresh input the table chose ordinary Fermat. [Reproducible split and matching work counts](../benchmarks/cheap-multiplier-selection.json)

## Could a much better multiplier cover most of the range?

**Even a billion product-dependent choices cover fewer than $2^{564}$ integer positions** if each multiplier is at most $N$ and each search tests the first $2^{32}$ consecutive traces. This grants every divisor allocation of every multiplier. Each of our two comparison inputs has more than $2^{1020}$ feasible positions, so even that budget covers less than one part in $2^{456}$ of either interval. This counts locations. A well-chosen available location can still contain the true prime. The bound concerns prefixes starting at the minimum trace. [Coverage theorem](../PrimeFactors/SharedMultiplierCoverage.lean) · [Size of the two feasible intervals](a-shorter-pascal-row-for-equal-width-primes.md)

Two facts explain the bound. First, if $\tau(k)$ denotes the number of positive divisors, then $\tau(k)/k^{1/4}$ is maximized at the multiplier we tested, with value $576/(21{,}621{,}600)^{1/4}<9$. This is an instance of Ramanujan's theory of superior highly composite numbers. The proof checks the best exponent separately at each prime. The exponents at $2,3,5,7,11,13$ are respectively $5,3,2,1,1,1$, and primes above 13 need no correction. This choice maximizes a bound on the number of available allocations. Which allocations actually touch a particular factor interval depends on the product. [Ramanujan, *Highly Composite Numbers*](https://doi.org/10.1112/plms/s2_14.1.347) · [Sharp constant in Lean](../PrimeFactors/MultiplierDivisorReach.lean)

Second, write $N=t^4$, $k=r^4$ and allow trace excess less than $s^2$. For an allocation with a nonempty interval below $\sqrt N$, its weight satisfies $3a\ge r^2$ whenever $s^2\le\sqrt{kN}$. Two integer locations in that interval differ by at most $(6ts+3s^2)/r$. Multiplying the resulting count by less than $9r$ allocations cancels the possible gain from large $k$. Including integer endpoints, every multiplier $1\le k\le N$ accounts for at most $9(t+6ts+3s^2)$ positions. The full-size theorem allows one extra unit of trace height for rounding the starting point. [Diameter and union proofs](../PrimeFactors/SharedMultiplierCoverage.lean)

## How often can this approach find the actual prime?

For the eight fixed multipliers used above, **fewer than one in $2^{469}$ eligible prime pairs** can yield a factor by a square and a proper gcd within the first $2^{32}$ consecutive integer traces for each multiplier. Our mod-24 runs fit well inside that trace height. The population consists of uniformly sampled distinct 1,024-bit primes conditioned on their product having 2,048 bits, with each unordered pair counted once. A rule can use the entire product to decide which of these eight to try. The probability bound concerns this particular factoring route. [Population theorem](../PrimeFactors/SharedMultiplierPopulation.lean)

Here the order of choices matters. For a *fixed* smaller prime $p$ and one multiplier $k\le2^{2048}$, we count possible larger partners $q$. A square that gives a proper gcd forces $ap+bq$ to be the tested trace for some $ab=k$, including when the square was found through another allocation. Its excess above the minimum is $(\sqrt{ap}-\sqrt{bq})^2$. This restricts $\sqrt q$ to a short interval. Accounting for every allocation gives at most $2^{535}$ possible integer partners per multiplier. The existing unconditional prime-count estimate then turns partner counts into a fraction of eligible prime pairs. [Partner bound](../PrimeFactors/SharedMultiplierPartners.lean) · [Why every proper gcd is counted](../PrimeFactors/ShiftedEndpointPopulation.lean) · [Prime-pair normalization](../PrimeFactors/SquareRootPhasePopulation.lean)

More generally, a fixed catalogue of one billion multipliers succeeds on fewer than one pair in $2^{442}$ under the same trace budget. Even allowing 128 bits of product information to choose which billion-entry catalogue to use leaves the fraction below $2^{-314}$. The catalogue size in this calculation counts all choices available as the larger prime varies with the smaller prime held fixed. A selector with unrestricted access to the full product and to arbitrary multipliers still has the geometric coverage bound above; its success probability is a different question. [Catalogue and advice theorems](../PrimeFactors/SharedMultiplierPopulation.lean)
