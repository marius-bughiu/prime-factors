# Two readings recognize a repeat

For $119=7\times17$, a shift of three gives the pair $(2,48)$ and a shift of twelve gives $(65,6)$. Each agrees in one position with the starting pair $(2,6)$. Neither shift repeats the sequence. When **both** readings agree, every following term repeats. Lean proves this for all products of two distinct primes at least seven. The shift can be guessed using only the product. Obtaining the readings by direct summation still requires one pass over all residues. [Two-reading theorem and counterexamples](../PrimeFactors/JacobiTwoMomentPeriod.lean)

Write $M_{2j}$ for the complete sum of $x^{2j}(\frac{x^2-4}{N})$ over residues modulo $N$, starting at $j=1$. The test for a shift $d$ reads $M_{2(d+1)}$ and $M_{2(d+2)}$. At a prime $p$, positive half-indices repeat every $a=(p-1)/2$ steps. Away from the endpoint, the local value $w_j$ is the middle binomial coefficient $\binom{2j}{j}$ reduced modulo $p$, and consecutive values satisfy $(j+1)w_{j+1}=(4j+2)w_j$. Substituting $w_j=2$ and $w_{j+1}=6$ forces $j=1$ inside that interval. Neither pair spanning an endpoint has those two values. Applying the same reasoning at both primes identifies the full repeat. For a sequence sampled every $s$ steps, the two readings test the shift $d=st$ across all starting positions. ## Combining the two positions in one weighted sum

Subtracting three times the first reading from the second gives one weighted sum over the residues. Locally it is zero at each repeat for primes at least seven. There is one extra case. Modulo 13 it also vanishes at shifts congruent to four modulo six. That matters on actual products. For $143=11\times13$ the weighted sum vanishes at shift ten, even though the first true positive repeat is thirty. Testing the two starting values separately catches the difference. [Weighted criterion](../PrimeFactors/JacobiPeriodWeight.lean) · [Bounded shifts on three products](../benchmarks/jacobi-period-weight.json)

Moving the two readings forward by one position removes the exceptional prime when both primes are at least eleven. The new combination is $3M_{2(d+3)}-10M_{2(d+2)}$. It vanishes modulo each prime precisely when that prime’s local period divides $d$. At the same false shift ten on 143 it gives 77 modulo 143, so the gcd is 11. This reports which local period divides the proposed shift. The direct evaluation still uses the Jacobi signs across the full product. ## What a zero reading says about the primes

For any positive even degree $k$, an odd prime factor $p$ divides $M_k$ exactly when $p\equiv3\pmod 4$ and $p-1$ divides $k$. This includes the small primes 3 and 5. At the endpoint the local value is $1+(-1)^{(p-1)/2}$. The other even degrees reduce to nonzero binomial coefficients. The gcd therefore tells exactly which of the two primes meet both conditions. [Exact gcd theorem](../PrimeFactors/JacobiMomentZeros.lean)

On $77=7\times11$, degrees $2,6,10,30$ gave remainders $2,42,55,0$. Their gcds with 77 were $1,7,11,77$. If both primes are 1 modulo 4, the same theorem rules out a factor from the raw moment at **every** positive even degree. For instance, all positive even degrees have gcd one on $65=5\times13$. The 14 small control measurements used 157 Jacobi signs, reusing each product’s signs across degrees, and 770 modular powers.
