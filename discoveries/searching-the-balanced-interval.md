## One square test for a divisor interval

Allowing any minimizing integer cost for a chosen real slope extends the first-level test below. A population bound covers even very large weights and choices guided by the ends of the product. [Supporting slopes](supporting-slopes.md)

Choose positive integers $a,b$ and compute $m=\lceil\sqrt{4abN}\rceil$. If a divisor $x$ lies in the band

$$\bigl(2ax-(m+1)\bigr)^2\le 2m,$$

then $ax+b(N/x)=m$. To see the upper bound, at the first larger sum $m+1$ the identity $(2ax-(m+1))^2+4abN-(m+1)^2=4ax(ax+b(N/x)-(m+1))$ would have a negative left side and a nonnegative right side. The lower bound follows from $(ax-b(N/x))^2\ge0$. Thus one nonsquare $m^2-4abN$ excludes the *entire* band. A square $d^2$ leaves just $(m-d)/(2a)$ and $(m+d)/(2a)$ to check for integrality and divisibility. For fixed positive weights the band contains order $N^{1/4}$ consecutive integers. [Lean interval and endpoint proofs](../PrimeFactors/IntervalGap.lean)

For $N=34387$, the weights $(3,2)$ give $m=909$ and discriminant 993, between $31^2$ and $32^2$. This rules out every divisor from 145 through 158 in one test. The weights $(2,1)$ give $m=525$ and discriminant $23^2$. Between 124 and 139, only $(525+23)/4=137$ divides the product. Lean checks both interval statements, including the endpoints. [Checked examples](../PrimeFactors/IntervalGap.lean)

**A global guarantee.** For every integer $K\ge1$, Dirichlet approximation applied to $q/p$ supplies some $1\le b\le K$ and $b\le a\le 2b$ with $(K+1)|ap-bq|\le p$. Although we do not know those indices, we can construct every window using just $N$. For the correct indices the weighted sum $t=ap+bq$ lies from $m$ through

$$m+\left\lfloor\frac{\sqrt N}{2b(K+1)^2}\right\rfloor.$$

Lean proves coverage, extraction of a factor at the correct square, and an upper bound of $2K(K+1)$ on the **total** number of tests across all windows whenever $\lfloor\sqrt N\rfloor\le(K+1)^3$. Choosing $K$ near $N^{1/6}$ gives $O(N^{1/3})$ tests using integers of $O(\log N)$ bits. For $N=1900014700027$, $K=111$ gives 8,978 tests in the complete list. The first sum at $(a,b)=(19,10)$ gives discriminant $33^2$ and factors $1000003$ and $1900009$. This formalizes a balanced case of the classical Lawrence–Lehman approach. [Lean coverage, count and example](../PrimeFactors/Multiplier.lean) · [Lehman](https://doi.org/10.1090/S0025-5718-1974-0340163-2) · [Hittmeir’s time–space tradeoff](https://arxiv.org/abs/2006.16729)

**How many single-test bands would full coverage take?** Suppose we insist on covering every integer in $[\sqrt{N/2},\sqrt N]$ with the first band above for each of $T$ distinct weight pairs $1\le b\le a$. Each pair covers at most $1+\sqrt{2m}/a=O(1+N^{1/4}/\sqrt a)$ integers. There are at most $a$ choices of $b$ per $a$. Splitting at $a=\lceil\sqrt T\rceil$ bounds the sum of $1/\sqrt a$ over these pairs by $O(T^{3/4})$. Consequently their union covers at most $O(T+N^{1/4}T^{3/4})$ positions. The interval has order $\sqrt N$ positions, so this covering strategy needs $\Omega(N^{1/3})$ bands. This paper bound applies when every position needs a certificate of this particular form. A method can still succeed early or use other information to discard positions.

## What higher prime powers can remove

For an odd prime $\ell\nmid N$ let $D(s)=s^2-4N$. If $D(s)$ is a nonzero square modulo $\ell$, **every** representative of this sum class passes the test modulo **every** power of $\ell$. A nonzero root of $D$ can be lifted one digit at a time. At a singular class, where $D(s)=\ell u$, exactly one next sum digit passes modulo $\ell^2$. Writing the lift as $s+\ell t$, it is determined by $t=-u/(2s)\pmod\ell$. For the eleven even candidates in $I$ at $N=34387$, moduli $3,5,7$ leave $382,388,392$. Replacing 3 with 9 leaves only $388$, hence $137\cdot251$. [Lean lifting theorems and exhaustive example](../PrimeFactors/PrimePower.lean) · [Hittmeir, §7](https://arxiv.org/html/2205.10074v2#S7)

These facts give a lower bound uniform in the exponent. For $\ell\ge5$, at least $(\ell-3)/2$ classes are regular and admissible, so their density is at least $1/5$ at every power. If $N$ is nonsquare modulo 3 there is one regular admissible class. If $N\equiv1\bmod3$, choose $r$ with $r^2\equiv4N+9\bmod27$ by lifting a unit square. Every $s\equiv r\bmod27$ has $D(s)=9u$ with $u\equiv1\bmod3$. This class survives every power of 3. Lean checks the permanent class, including at the first two powers. Thus **at least $1/27$** of all residues survive at each odd prime power coprime to $N$. The density conclusion combines the local theorems on paper. [Regular-class counts](../PrimeFactors/ResidueCount.lean) · [Permanent class modulo 27](../PrimeFactors/PrimePower.lean)

## Matching powers to recover the sum

Every unit $u$ modulo $N$ satisfies $u^{p+q}=u^{N+1}$ because the exponent difference is $\varphi(N)$. The right side is computable from $N$. Write a candidate as $s=L+mi+j$, with $0\le j<m$. Store the values $u^j$, then look up $u^{N+1}/(u^L(u^m)^i)$ for each block $i$. Reject candidates outside $I$. Each lookup checks one block against the stored entries. With $m=\lceil\sqrt W\rceil$ there are at most $m$ baby entries and $\lceil W/m\rceil$ giant probes. Several bases can be used as coordinates of one table entry. A residue sieve at modulus $m$ can omit every baby index $j$ for which $L+j$ fails the local sum test. [Lean power identity and sieved table equivalence](../PrimeFactors/PowerSignature.lean) · [Hittmeir, §6](https://arxiv.org/html/2205.10074v2#S6)

For example, $N=34387$ has $I=[371,393]$. With $m=5$ only $j=1,2$ pass the local sieve. At base 2 the baby entries are $2,4$. The five giant probes are $27911,4096,128,4,21492$. The single match gives $s=371+5\cdot3+2=388$. This base happens to work on this input. In general a base of small order can match multiple sums.

**Choosing the bases.** Lean proves that any two integers in $I$ differ by less than $q-1$. In the cyclic unit group modulo $q$, a generator distinguishes any positive exponent gap this short. Lifting it to a unit modulo $N$ shows that the units failing to distinguish this gap form a proper subgroup. The small-prime-outside-a-subgroup theorem then supplies a small distinguishing base. Different gaps may use different bases. Under the published Dirichlet GRH bound, for two 1,024-bit primes we may use all prime bases through the square of the product's bit length. Each such base is coprime to $N$, and the width bound makes the vector test exact. Lean checks the implication from the finite small-prime hypothesis. [Power separation](../PrimeFactors/PowerSignature.lean) · [Lamzouri, Li and Soundararajan, Theorem 1.1](https://arxiv.org/html/1309.3595v3#S1.SS1) · [Dirichlet verification](https://math.mit.edu/~drew/grh/)

There is also an unconditional choice. Pick two independent uniform units modulo $N$. Their images modulo $q$ are independent uniform elements of a cyclic group of order $h=q-1$. In generator coordinates they jointly generate this group precisely when no prime dividing $h$ divides both coordinates. CRT therefore gives success probability $\prod_{\ell\mid h,\ \ell\ \mathrm{prime}}(1-\ell^{-2})>1/2$. On that event their joint period separates all candidates in $I$. Lean checks separation from the joint period and the lower bound on this finite product. The identification of the product with the sampling probability is the cyclic-group argument on paper. No knowledge of $q$ is used to sample the units. A gcd suffices for sampling, and a proper gcd itself supplies a factor. [Joint-period algebra](../PrimeFactors/JointPeriod.lean) · [Pomerance, pp. 191–192](https://math.dartmouth.edu/~carlp/PDF/paper123.pdf)

Per attempt, keep one baby index per signature, try at most one candidate per giant probe, and verify its discriminant and factors. With a separating pair every baby index is distinct in the table and the correct match is retained. With other pairs this policy still bounds the work and permits a fresh attempt. The expected number of attempts is less than two. Without a sieve, initialization uses $O(\log N)$ modular multiplications per base, and subsequent entries use one multiplication per coordinate per step. Storing and sorting the baby table and looking up the giant steps costs $O(\sqrt W)$ records and $O(\sqrt W\log W)$ record comparisons. Any optional sieve construction has its own cost. These are paper operation counts. For a balanced 2,048-bit product, the unsieved baby table alone has roughly $2^{510}$ entries. Known deterministic factoring algorithms already give stronger general asymptotic bounds. [Harvey and Hittmeir](https://arxiv.org/abs/2105.11105)

## How small could two additive tables be?

### Collisions visible at one unknown prime

Subdividing the ratio interval at fractions gives tighter integer endpoints for each window. A slope-dependent congruence then skips up to 23 out of 24 consecutive sums while retaining one shared power table. The implementation recovered two independently drawn 50-bit primes from their product in 7.59 seconds. [Complete coverage, timings and costs](farey-weighted-sums.md)

In a weighted window the true exponent is $t=ap+bq$. Modulo $p-1$ this equals the computable $a+bN$. Write $t=c+mj+i$ with $c=\lceil\sqrt{4abN}\rceil$ and $0\le i<m$. The powers $u^i$ and $u^{a+bN}/(u^c (u^m)^j)$ therefore agree modulo $p$. One baby table serves every window. For example, at $N=34387$, base 2 and weights $(3,2)$, the representatives are 16 and 23443. Their difference has gcd 137 with $N$. Sorting by residue modulo $N$ alone would miss that pair. Harvey's polynomial multipoint evaluation batches the gcd tests, with separate handling of equal representatives and large-order base selection. The paper proves the resulting bit complexity. [Harvey, Algorithms 4.1–4.2](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2010.05450#page=9)

Lean verifies coverage and the number $G$ of giant entries. For $m=(K+1)^2$ and $\lfloor\sqrt N\rfloor\le(K+1)^5$, it proves $G\le 2K(K+1)$. Taking $K=\lceil N^{1/10}\rceil$ gives $O(N^{1/5})$ entries. Conversely, for **every** positive $K,m$ this explicit complete table construction satisfies $N<128(G+m)^5$. Thus changing those two parameters cannot improve its exponent. A 2048-bit input needs more than $2^{408}$ records across the complete tables, even with the best choices. This lower bound applies to these table lengths only. The published faster logarithmic factors use other structure. [Finite proofs and example](../PrimeFactors/WeightedCollision.lean) · [Harvey and Hittmeir](https://arxiv.org/abs/2105.11105)

### Tables covering all local survivors

Suppose two finite integer tables $A,B$ cover every locally admissible offset in an interval of width $W$ by sums $a+b$. If the sieve has period $M$ and $H_M$ is its set of admissible classes, Lean proves

$$4\lfloor W/M\rfloor\,|H_M|\ \le\ (|A|+|B|)^2.$$

Indeed each full period contributes every class once, and the tables supply at most $|A||B|$ different outputs. Even an arbitrary function of a pair of indices obeys the product bound. Negative entries, repeated representations and extra candidates cannot increase the number of index pairs. [Lean finite cover bound](../PrimeFactors/AdditiveCover.lean)

For **any odd $M\le W$ coprime to $N$**, including repeated prime factors, the density result above and CRT give $|H_M|/M\ge 27^{-\omega(M)}$. Also $M\ge(\omega(M)+1)!$, hence $\omega(M)=O(\log W/\log\log W)$. Taking square roots in the finite bound yields $|A|+|B|\ge W^{1/2-o(1)}$. This paper deduction rules out a fixed improvement in the exponent for methods that explicitly build two tables covering every survivor of such a sieve. It leaves larger periods, other product information and implicit tables open.

**Open question.** For some fixed $\varepsilon>0$, can residues for a product-selected period $M>W$ be used to construct two explicit lists, in $O(W^{1/2-\varepsilon}\operatorname{poly}(\log N))$ bit operations, whose sums lie in $I$ and cover its admissible sums? Requiring the construction cost as well as the list size to fit the bound rules out hiding a full interval scan in preprocessing. The power identity would let us test the two lists by matching signatures. The complete-period argument above identifies a range of periods where this strategy cannot work.
