# Returning to an odd power can reveal a factor

For a product of two distinct primes both congruent to 3 modulo 4, a base with negative Jacobi symbol gives a deterministic way to find a factor by repeated squaring. The first successful step can be large. On a selected **62-bit** input, every such base surviving a simple gcd precheck has its first successful step at **500,001,179**. Neither of the repository's two 2,048-bit comparison products has both primes congruent to 3 modulo 4. [Theorem](../PrimeFactors/BlumSignedPowers.lean) · [Examples](../PrimeFactors/BlumSignedPowerExamples.lean)

## What the product tells us

Let $N=pq$ and write $N-1=2^s t$ with $t$ odd. Choose a unit $u$ with $(u/N)=-1$, evaluated from $N$. Its two Legendre symbols are opposite. For a prime $p\equiv3\pmod4$, the powers of two dividing $p-1$ stop at 2. Consequently an odd power of $u$ that reaches either $+1$ or $-1$ modulo $p$ can reach only its original Legendre sign. Indeed, raising $+1$ or $-1$ to the odd exponent $(p-1)/2$ preserves it, and Euler's criterion computes the sign. Define $r_p$ to be the local multiplicative order of $u^{N-1}$ and define $r_q$ in the same way. For every odd $m$,

$$ r_p\mid m \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad u^{tm}\equiv (u/p)\pmod p. $$

The same statement holds at $q$. Thus if either order divides $m$, at least one of $\gcd(u^{tm}-1,N)$ and $\gcd(u^{tm}+1,N)$ gives a proper prime factor. If both divide $m$, the two signs yield the two factors separately. This criterion applies to arbitrary odd $m$. The local orders need not be given to the search. The prior reduced-order result proves they are coprime. [Local sign and separation proofs](../PrimeFactors/BlumSignedPowers.lean) · [Reduced orders](../PrimeFactors/ReducedSmoothReadout.lean)

Set $x=u^t\bmod N$, repeatedly square it, and compare the resulting residue to both $x$ and $-x$ by gcd. Step $j\ge1$ tests $m=2^j-1$. Its first successful index is exactly $\min(\operatorname{ord}_{r_p}(2),\operatorname{ord}_{r_q}(2))$, taking the order modulo 1 as 1. The local orders $r_p,r_q$ are odd, so these returns always occur. Blum, Blum and Shub studied the periods of this modular squaring recurrence. The two local signs determine which returns give proper gcds here. The code uses one initial modular exponentiation, one modular squaring and two gcds per step. At a fixed public budget the procedure can also be run on inputs with unknown prime classes, with the above completion guarantee applying to the stated class. ## A complete small comparison

The comparison started the ordinary recurrence at $u^{N-1}\bmod N$, squared it, and checked a gcd against its initial residue at each step. If the two local return periods are equal, ordinary returns occur at both primes simultaneously and this gcd never splits. Checking differences between any two terms in the same ordinary orbit gives the same condition on their index difference because the local orders are odd.

We used every negative-Jacobi unit for every pair of distinct 7-bit primes congruent to 3 modulo 4, with a cap of 64 steps per base. Across 21 products and 84,330 bases, the signed test split all 84,330 by step 20. The ordinary test split 82,368. The 1,962 other bases had equal periods, including 1,728 with nontrivial orders on both sides. The signed test used 630,314 squarings and 1,260,628 gcds. The ordinary test used 734,912 squarings and 734,912 gcds. Inputs and bases were identical. The native differential check used the known primes only to compute and verify the predicted return indices. [All counts and work](../research/blum-signed-powers.json)

At $N=5{,}609=71\cdot79$, the least negative-Jacobi base is 3. The reduced orders are 35 and 39, and 2 has order 12 modulo each. Step 12 gives both factors in the signed test. At $N=4{,}000{,}061{,}596{,}123{,}066{,}801$, the least negative-Jacobi base is 7. Both methods exhausted the 64-step budget. We chose this product for prime predecessors of the form $p=2r+1$ and $r=2R+1$. At each of its primes every unit satisfying the public precheck $\gcd(u^2-1,N)=1$ has reduced order exactly $r$. The two return periods are 500,001,179 and 500,006,519. These periods certify the first successful index without executing half a billion squarings. The bound applies to this recurrence and interval of indices. Other factoring methods can handle a 62-bit integer readily. [Checked arithmetic](../PrimeFactors/BlumSignedPowerExamples.lean) · [Benchmarked split and partial result](../benchmarks/blum-signed-powers.json)

Miller's classical method uses powers and square roots of unity when an annihilating exponent is available. Here finding a negative-Jacobi seed and reaching a suitable exponent have separate costs. For a balanced 2,048-bit product, Lamzouri, Li and Soundararajan's Theorem 1.1(2), applied with Dirichlet GRH, gives a negative-Jacobi prime below $(\log N)^2<2^{21}$. This published bound controls finding the seed. The local return indices still depend on the input. The Lean module checks the finite algebra independently of the analytic result. Reproduce the comparison with `python3 -m experiments.blum_signed_powers`.
