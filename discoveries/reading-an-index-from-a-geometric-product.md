# Reading the factor sum when several powers match

The search factored **668,556,045,362,066,899** from the product alone in **0.443 seconds** on the test machine. It covered 99,197,914 possible sums by building one polynomial and reading it in two directions. The answer was **650,523,521 × 1,027,720,019**. Lean checked the factorization separately. On each 2,048-bit comparison, the required polynomial degree still exceeded $2^{510}$, so the calculation stopped before building it. On **34,393**, an initial calculation gave zero. Three further readings of the same product gave the factor **163**, leaving **211**. On **30,353** the same three readings gave two possible positions. Just one was in range, and it gave **127 × 239**. Both examples were chosen to test those branches of the calculation. Lean checks the readings from the public input, the arithmetic, and the resulting prime factors. On the two 2,048-bit controls the same low-digit test still left more than $2^{1017}$ terms apiece. ## What the readings measure

Suppose $N=pq$ with distinct primes $p<q\le 2p$. The public interval starts at $L=\lceil2\sqrt N\rceil$ and ends at $L+W=\lfloor3\sqrt{N/2}\rfloor$. The unknown index is $j=p+q-L$. Choose a unit $g$ modulo $N$, and compute $y=g^{N+1-L}\bmod N$. The right index always gives $g^j=y$. The polynomial $F(Y,G)=\prod_{i=0}^W(Y-G^i)$ has value zero at $(y,g)$. The way its value changes there can reveal which term vanished. This use of the public power identity builds on Hittmeir’s factor-sum search. [Power identity](../PrimeFactors/PowerSignature.lean) · [Hittmeir, §6](https://arxiv.org/html/2205.10074v2#S6)

Measure the coefficients of $t$ and $t^2$ in $F(y(1+zt),g(1+t))$ at $z=0,1,-1$. If the target occurs only once modulo each prime, the coefficient of $t$ is $u(z-j)$ with $u$ invertible modulo $N$. Two directions recover $j$ by modular division. If the target occurs once modulo one prime and more often modulo the other, a gcd with the linear coefficient can return a prime directly. A unique match modulo the whole product can still give zero linear readings at both primes. For $N=33,127$ and $g=12$, the index 3 is globally unique and both first derivatives vanish. [Linear readout and the three cases on 33,127](../PrimeFactors/GeometricIndexReadout.lean)

## Recovering two local matches

Suppose that at each hidden prime there are exactly two matching indices $a<b$ in the interval. Modulo that prime, the quadratic reading $C(z)$ is $u(z-a)(z-b)$ for some nonzero $u$. The difference $d=b-a$ is the order of $g$ at that prime. With $A=C(1)+C(-1)-2C(0)$, $B=C(-1)-C(1)$ and $D=B^2-8A C(0)$, we obtain $D=A^2 d^2$. For a prime $p$ write $k=(p-1)/d$. Then $k^2D-A^2$ is zero modulo $p$. With two matches, the interval width is less than $3d$. On a 2,048-bit product of two 1,024-bit primes, $p<2^{1024}$ and the full interval has $W\ge2^{1020}$. Thus $1\le k<48$. If the two prime-side cofactors differ, one of those small gcd tests returns a proper divisor. [Checked coefficient identities, interval width and cofactor bound](../PrimeFactors/GeometricDoubleMatch.lean)

If the cofactors agree, the corresponding gcd returns $N$. There are still only two candidates. The true index must be the smaller of the two matches at both primes or the larger at both. If the other match is $j+d$, then $j<d\le W-j$. If it is $j-d$, then $W-j<d\le j$. The position of $j$ relative to the middle of the public interval therefore determines a common sign. The two residue candidates are $(kB\pm A)/(2kA)\bmod N$. The true representative lies in $[0,W]$. An integer square test on the proposed sum checks each in-range candidate. [Same-rank theorem and exact recovery from the readings](../PrimeFactors/GeometricMatchRank.lean)

| 30,353, base 22 | Cofactor 14 gives the whole product. The two representatives are 17 and 28,202. Only 17 is at most 20. The sum is $349+17=366$. |

## When there are more matches

At one prime the matching indices form an arithmetic progression with period $d$. If it contains $r$ indices and includes the true $j$, its ranks to the left and right of $j$ are $\lfloor j/d\rfloor$ and $\lfloor(W-j)/d\rfloor$. Both decrease or stay fixed as $d$ increases. Equal totals at the two primes therefore imply equal ranks on each side. This removes the need to try independent ranks for each prime. [Exact count in an interval](../PrimeFactors/GeometricMatchRank.lean)

The first potentially nonzero coefficient is then at order $t^r$. As a polynomial in $z$, its roots are the $r$ indices, and its top three coefficients give their sum and their squared spacing. In particular, for indices $x_i=a+id$, the identity $12(r\sum x_i^2-(\sum x_i)^2)=r^2(r^2-1)d^2$ recovers the squared period when the indicated factors are invertible. With equal cofactors and an invertible denominator, there are $r$ linear candidates for the index. At 2,048 bits, for equal multiplicities from 2 through $2^{64}$, Lean proves the normalization is invertible and that fewer than $16(r+1)$ possible positive cofactors need to be tried. If the multiplicities differ, the leading coefficient at the smaller multiplicity has gcd equal to the prime with the larger multiplicity. This higher-order result is a theorem about the required readings. The measured implementation uses orders through two. [Directional product and moments](../PrimeFactors/GeometricMatchMoments.lean) · [Cofactor tests and recovery](../PrimeFactors/GeometricMultiplicityReadout.lean)

## Evaluating many terms together

For an odd product $N$, lift the geometric product to the integer polynomial $F_z(t)$. Compute its value at $t=0,N,-N$ modulo $N^3$. The difference $F_z(N)-F_z(-N)$ divided by $N$ gives twice the linear coefficient modulo $N$. The second difference $F_z(N)+F_z(-N)-2F_z(0)$ divided by $N^2$ gives twice the quadratic coefficient. Multiplying by the inverse of two completes the extraction. The value at zero is shared across all three directions, so seven scalar products supply all seven coefficients. [Lean proof for arbitrary representatives and for the actual product](../PrimeFactors/GeometricReadingLift.lean)

At a given $t$, set $G=g(1+t)$ and $Y=y(1+zt)$. With block size $b$, form the monic polynomial $P_b(X)=\prod_{i<b}(X-G^i)$. Each complete block starting at $b j$ is $G^{b^2j} P_b(Y G^{-bj})$. Its evaluation points form a geometric progression, so many blocks can use one convolution. Three baby polynomials suffice because all the directions share $G$ for a given $t$. The incomplete tail is evaluated directly. These identities work modulo the composite $N^3$ since $g$ and $1\pm N$ are units. For $C$ terms, a block size near $\sqrt C$ uses $O(\sqrt C)$ coefficients and evaluation points. The number of bit operations also depends on coefficient size and polynomial multiplication. This applies classical geometric-product evaluation to the derivative readings. [Block and tail identities](../PrimeFactors/GeometricBlockReading.lean) · [Bostan and Yurkevich, Algorithms 1–2](https://arxiv.org/abs/2012.08656) · [Harvey, geometric evaluation](https://arxiv.org/abs/2010.05450)

The 60-bit held-out product **668,556,045,362,066,899** had 99,197,914 candidate sums. Three polynomials of degree 9,960 and 69,713 block evaluations supplied the readings. The remaining tails needed 43,918 scalar terms. The linear readout gave **650,523,521 × 1,027,720,019**. One local run took 1.32 seconds for the search and another 1.26 seconds to generate and check the certificate. The 30-bit factor stream, base 2 and first sample were chosen before observing the result. No full direct loop was run on that product. On a fresh 37-bit input all seven coefficients agreed with direct evaluation. Across eight alternating runs, median times were 12.18 milliseconds for blocks and 51.82 milliseconds for the direct product. [Fixture origins, operation counts and remaining cofactors](../benchmarks/geometric-readout-blocks.json) · [Scalar block evaluator](../experiments/geometric_readout_blocks.py)

On the two unchanged 2,048-bit comparisons, the respective unfiltered block degrees would be about $2^{510.39}$ and $2^{510.27}$. The cap of 16,384 stopped both before constructing any polynomial. Even with the modulo-32 filter, at least $2^{1016}$ candidates survive on every target input. If at most four lanes each use blocks of degree at most $b$, at most $g$ full-block evaluations per lane and one shorter tail, coverage requires $\max(b,g+1)\ge 2^{507}$. This prices the block construction evaluated here. [Full-size proof](../PrimeFactors/GeometricSumLanes.lean)

## Stopping after the first derivative

Three scalar products modulo $N^2$ suffice to try the linear readout. They evaluate the unperturbed product and two directions at $t=N$. If a quadratic reading is needed, six products modulo $N^3$ recover all the coefficients from the seven-product reference. The first coefficient depends linearly on the direction, so its value at $-1$ is twice its value at 0 minus its value at 1. This saves one of the two side evaluations at $-1$. Starting at cubic precision lets both stages share the initial three products. Starting at quadratic precision makes a successful first stage cheaper and requires recomputation on a miss. The benchmark checks both schedules. [Three- and six-sample theorems](../PrimeFactors/GeometricReadingStencil.lean) · [Checked comparisons](../benchmarks/geometric-readout-blocks.json)

On the 60-bit input, representative medians over six rotating runs were 1.411 seconds for the reference, 0.769 seconds starting at cubic precision, and 0.560 seconds starting at quadratic precision. All three recovered the same prime pair. Starting at quadratic precision was slower on the two deliberately selected inputs that required the second derivative. These are local search times. The Lean certificates run separately.

For every fixed target product, at least $1403/5408$ of its units yield a factor or the true index from the first derivative alone. The count includes bases whose order at the smaller prime is its maximum, one half, or one quarter of that maximum, and includes the corresponding one-eighth case at the larger prime. Orders this large exceed the search width on that side. The other side either also has a single match, giving the index, or gives a proper gcd. This probability is over a fresh uniform base for the fixed product. It provides no input-specific shortcut for evaluating the products. [Uniform count and recovery theorem](../PrimeFactors/GeometricGeneratorFraction.lean)

## One grid for all three directions

Use the same balanced interval and assume $N=pq$ for distinct odd primes $p<q\le2p$. From the product and a chosen base $g$ coprime to $N$, compute $G=g^N\bmod N^3$ and $Y=G^{N+1-L}\bmod N^3$. If the original powers agree at an index modulo $p$, their $N$th powers agree modulo $p^2$. The balance assumption gives $\gcd(N,p-1)=\gcd(N,q-1)=1$, so raising to $N$ preserves the matching indices at each prime. [Local congruence and order proofs](../PrimeFactors/GeometricPrincipalLift.lean) · [Power-lifting lemma in Mathlib 4.19 (Apache-2.0)](https://github.com/leanprover-community/mathlib4/blob/v4.19.0/Mathlib/NumberTheory/Basic.lean#L28)

For $z=-1,0,1$, let $V_z$ be the least nonnegative residue modulo $N^3$ of

$$
\prod_{i=0}^W \bigl(Y(1+zN)-(G(1+N))^i\bigr).
$$

Every direction uses the same sequence of powers. The code builds its root polynomial once and uses the same geometric grid to evaluate the required target values. Let $H_z=(V_z/N)\bmod N$. At each prime this gives the first coefficient of the product expanded in $t$ about zero with target $Y(1+zt)$ and base $G(1+t)$. A single match on each side guarantees that the unperturbed product is divisible by $N^2$. For example, on 33,127 with base 2 its residue modulo $N^3$ is $28{,}035N^2$. [Proof of the digit extraction](../PrimeFactors/GeometricLiftedDigit.lean) · [Regression test](../tests/test_geometric_lifted_readout.py)

The search starts with $H_{-1}$. When $W+1<p$, every candidate index avoids $-1$ modulo either prime. A prime divides $H_{-1}$ exactly when it has at least two matching indices. This gives three cases. [Negative-direction coefficient and gcd](../PrimeFactors/GeometricNegativeDirection.lean)

| $\gcd(H_{-1},N)$ | What the search can read |
| --- | --- |
| A proper divisor | The gcd itself splits $N$. For $33{,}127$ and base 13, $H_{-1}=14{,}915$ gave the factor 157 from one target. |
| $1$ | Each prime has a single match. Read $H_0$ and recover its common index $j=-H_0/(H_0-H_{-1})\bmod N$. The integer square test checks the sum $L+j$. |
| $N$ | Each prime has at least two matches. In this case $N^2\mid V_z$ and $(V_z/N^2)\bmod N$ gives the quadratic coefficient. Three targets supply the cofactor and square tests above. If both primes have more than two matches, all three quadratic coefficients vanish. |

The 60-bit run used one degree-9,960 polynomial, 19,918 block values and 12,548 individual tail terms. The two targets took 0.443 seconds. Computing all three on that grid took 0.540 seconds. A seven-product reference at the same transformed base took 1.418 seconds and agreed on the measured coefficients. At 37 bits, the two targets took 4.1 milliseconds, all three took 5.3 milliseconds, and the independent direct evaluation took 50.6 milliseconds. These are local search or comparison times. The fixture records the full-size degree cap of 16,384 and the unvisited sums. [Implementation](../experiments/geometric_lifted_readout.py) · [Benchmark records](../benchmarks/geometric-lifted-readout.json)

## What the low digits save

At modulus 32, exactly two sum classes survive for $N\equiv5\pmod8$ and four for every other odd $N$. Each complete set of 32 consecutive candidates costs two or four terms. The search merges classes into longer progressions where possible. Within a progression $S=L'+mi$, the base becomes $g^m$ and its public target becomes $g^{N+1-L'}$. This retains the true index while reducing the length of each product. The 31-bit example used the first pair from the documented deterministic 16-bit prime streams at sample 1, base 2. Its successful progression had 605 power updates, two modular powers, one inversion and one final integer square test. [Affine identity, coverage and finite cost](../PrimeFactors/GeometricSumLanes.lean) · [Search and counters](../experiments/geometric_sum_readout.py)

## Which parts of the polynomial can we skip?

The baby polynomial can be built about 30 times faster on the 60-bit example. Its roots are successive powers of a single unit. Writing the product in the opposite order determines its upper coefficients from its lower coefficients. The classical finite geometric product identity supplies a recurrence for the lower half. On the test machine this took 7.95 milliseconds to build the degree-9,960 polynomial, compared with 250.04 milliseconds using a multiplication tree. In five alternating runs the median time for the whole search fell from 496.17 to 245.49 milliseconds. A batch inversion checks the denominators. If one shares a proper divisor with the input, that divisor is already a result. If inversion fails without exposing a divisor, the tree still computes the exact polynomial. [Coefficient proof](../PrimeFactors/ReflectedGeometricBlocks.lean) · [Implementation](../experiments/reflected_geometric_blocks.py) · [Bostan and Yurkevich on finite geometric products](https://arxiv.org/abs/2012.08656)

A different shortcut would write a polynomial with very few nonzero coefficients that vanishes on a whole block of consecutive powers and gives a nonzero answer at the next power. Lean proves that a block of length $L$ requires at least $L+1$ nonzero coefficients, even over a composite modulus. The sequence of evaluations of an $s$-term polynomial obeys a recurrence of order at most $s$. Once $s$ consecutive evaluations vanish, so do all subsequent ones. This counts expanded coefficients. For example, a dense geometric sum can itself be a product of a few binomials and can be evaluated quickly. [Proof, sharp example and computational limitation](../PrimeFactors/GeometricSparseFilter.lean) · [Related sparse-interpolation theory](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0747-7171(03)00088-9)

An odd auxiliary modulus also lets us discard impossible sums. Suppose we keep the same odd primes but choose higher powers of some of them after seeing the input. For any such modulus at most $2^{2048}$ and coprime to the input, the extra filtering improves the density by a factor below 4,096. This compares the fractions of allowed residue classes over complete periods. Particular short intervals can have quite different counts. [Finite bound](../PrimeFactors/OddSumWheel.lean) · [Modular hyperbola counts](https://arxiv.org/abs/1212.2930) · [Hittmeir’s sieve](https://arxiv.org/abs/2205.10074)


## A shared grid for the admissible sums

The product tells us some endings that the sum of the primes cannot have. On the same 60-bit input, the exact filter modulo 32 kept 12,399,740 of the 99,197,914 possible sums. Because the retained positions are arithmetic progressions with the same step, they share a single polynomial of degree 1,761. The unfiltered version needed degree 9,960. The median search time in four alternating runs went from 470 to 160 milliseconds using the multiplication-tree builder in both. All three modular readings also agreed with an independent direct multiplication over the 12,399,740 retained indices. [Search](../experiments/geometric_wheel_readout.py) · [Comparison fixtures](../benchmarks/geometric-wheel-readout.json)

More precisely, let $h=G(1+N)$ and $y=Y(1+zN)$ with the same public lift as above. In a residue class with first offset $a$ and length $b$, the required factors are $y-h^{a+32i}$ for $0\le i<b$. Their product is $h^{ab}$ times the product with target $y h^{-a}$ and base $h^{32}$. Multiplying those corrected answers recovers the readings at the original indices across all the permitted classes. There is at most one additional position in each class after the last common full turn. On both full-size controls the reduced polynomial degree still exceeded $2^{507}$, and both searches stopped before constructing it. [Identity and counts](../PrimeFactors/GeometricWheelBlocks.lean)

With the polynomial degree capped at 8,192, the filtered search recovered **6,860,100,517 × 7,585,863,317** from their 66-bit product **52,039,784,862,843,034,889** in 0.69 seconds locally. The input gave 875,188,048 possible sums. The filter kept 109,398,506 and reduced the required degree from 29,584 to 5,230. We generated the two factors from SHA-256 of the ASCII strings `prime-factors/shared-wheel/33bit/v1/p` and `prime-factors/shared-wheel/33bit/v1/q`. For each digest we kept its low 32 bits, set bits 32 and 0, and took the first prime at or above the result. The search got the product, base 2 and the public cap. It used the first generated pair. The benchmark stores the expected factors only for comparison and separately checks the certificate with Lean. [Fixture](../benchmarks/geometric-wheel-readout.json)

Removing positions can change which match comes first at each prime. For 793,157 and base 79 the two surviving hits at one prime were positions 36 and 80. At the other they were 16 and 36. The correct position is 36. The earlier cofactor decoder reached its limit of 48 on this input. [Mixed-rank comparison](../benchmarks/geometric-wheel-readout.json) A higher-precision polynomial recovers the common position even when the rankings differ.
