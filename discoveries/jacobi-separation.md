# Short periods from signed divisors of the hidden primes

For a quadratic equation that splits at exactly one hidden prime, a conjugate power test can recover a factor on a small input. We proved an upper bound on its chance of success at each general 2,048-bit input and derived an exact factor formula from two counts. Directly computing those counts still takes too much work. [Proof, costs and examples](frobenius-shift-count.md)


Given only $N=pq$ with distinct odd primes $p<q$, one can use all four common divisors $\gcd(p\pm1,q\pm1)$ in the short-period search. The algorithm chooses a random unit $D$ modulo $N$, computes its Jacobi symbol $j=(D/N)$, and raises a norm-one element of a quadratic algebra to the public exponent $N-j$. A large common divisor in any of the four sign patterns can shorten the search. Galbraith and McKee discuss common divisors of the two predecessors and the two successors. Williams uses local groups of order $p+1$ in his factorization method. Here the common divisor may be unknown and may contain large primes. [Galbraith and McKee, §5](https://www.math.auckland.ac.nz/~sgal018/pair-mod-n.pdf) · [Williams, *A p+1 method of factoring*](https://doi.org/10.1090/S0025-5718-1982-0658227-7)

## What the public exponent removes

Write $\epsilon_r=(D/r)$ for the hidden Legendre sign at $r=p,q$. Put $P=p-\epsilon_p$, $Q=q-\epsilon_q$ and $g=\gcd(P,Q)$. The Jacobi symbol gives $j=\epsilon_p\epsilon_q$. In the algebra $A=(\mathbb Z/N\mathbb Z)[w]/(w^2-D)$, conjugation sends $w$ to $-w$. Modulo $r$, the norm-one group is cyclic of order $r-\epsilon_r$. If $D$ is a square modulo $r$, the algebra splits into two fields and its norm-one group consists of the pairs $(a,a^{-1})$. If $D$ is a nonsquare, the algebra is the quadratic field extension and the norm-one group is the kernel of $z\mapsto z^{r+1}$ in its cyclic multiplicative group.

Set $E=N-j$. Reducing modulo $P$ gives $E\equiv\epsilon_p Q\pmod P$. Interchanging the primes gives $E\equiv\epsilon_q P\pmod Q$. Consequently

$$\gcd(E,P)=\gcd(E,Q)=g.$$

The image of $u\mapsto v=u^E$ has local sizes $a=P/g$ and $b=Q/g$. The local orders of $v$ divide $a$ and $b$, which are coprime. The factorization data enter this proof through $P$ and $Q$. Evaluating $v$ requires only $N$, $D$, $j$ and arithmetic on pairs of residues. Lean checks the Frobenius formula for a quadratic square root, its norm-one exponent, the signed gcd identities, the order divisibility and coprimality, and the exact kernel count for cyclic groups of these sizes. [Lean proofs](../PrimeFactors/JacobiSeparation.lean)

## Exact probabilities and search cost

A uniform unit $D$ has each pair of Legendre signs with probability $1/4$ by the Chinese remainder theorem. Given $D$, choose a uniform unit $z$ in $A$ and take $u=z/\bar z$. One can sample $z$ as a uniform pair and test $\gcd(z\bar z,N)$. A proper gcd already gives a factor, and rejection of nonunits takes constant expected time. In a split component the chance of a unit is $(1-1/r)^2$. In an inert component it is $1-1/r^2$. Conditional on being a unit, $u$ is uniform on each norm-one group independently. In the split case the first coordinate is the ratio of two independent uniform units. In the inert case the map is $z\mapsto z^{1-r}$, with image size $r+1$.

Exactly $g$ seeds from each cyclic norm-one group map to $1$. Thus the chance that $v$ equals $1$ in both components is $1/(ab)$. A single gcd with the norm of $v-1$ gives a proper factor with probability $(a+b-2)/(ab)$, the probability that exactly one component equals $1$. Lean checks the two-component count. This immediate success probability can be small. The full table search succeeds for every $v$ with at least one nonidentity component once its stride $m$ satisfies $m^2\ge\min(a,b)$. For $P\ne Q$, at least half the seeds have a nonidentity component. The remaining possibility $P=Q$ forces $q=p+2$ and is covered beforehand by testing whether $N+1$ is a square. [Finite cyclic kernel count and half-survival bound](../PrimeFactors/JacobiSeparation.lean)

Compare the babies $v^i$ for $0\le i<m$ to the giants $v^{mj}$ for $1\le j\le m$. The differences $mj-i$ cover $1$ through $m^2$. A whole-modulus collision supplies an exponent divisible by both local orders. Their coprimality allows a separating divisor to be found by enumerating the complementary divisor pairs up to that exponent's square root. A collision only at one hidden prime can be detected by batched polynomial evaluation followed by scalar gcds of the norms. At a split prime a difference of two norm-one elements is zero or a unit, since equality in one coordinate forces equality in both. At an inert prime the same dichotomy holds in a field. Lean also verifies directly over any field of characteristic different from 2 that the difference has zero norm exactly when both coordinates agree. Hence a row product has norm divisible by the prime exactly when some member of that row collides there. A row gcd of $N$ can be resolved by inspecting its $m$ differences after checking for whole-modulus matches. Polynomial arithmetic in this rank-two algebra uses a constant number of scalar polynomial operations per multiplication. The earlier geometric evaluation argument therefore costs $O(m\,\operatorname{polylog}N)$ bit operations per trial over the widths of interest. [Separating-divisor argument and geometric evaluation](common-predecessors.md) · [Harvey, Algorithm 4.1](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2010.05450#page=8)

Let $R$ be the minimum of $\min(P,Q)/\gcd(P,Q)$ over the four hidden sign patterns. Double $m$ from 1 and use 16 fresh independent $(D,u)$ trials at each width, sampling $u$ conditional on the chosen $D$. At widths $m^2\ge R$, each trial has success probability at least $1/8$ from the minimizing sign pair and the nonidentity estimate. Failure of all 16 has probability at most $(7/8)^{16}<1/4$. Thus the doubled widths beyond $\sqrt R$ have a summable expected cost. This gives $O(\sqrt R\,\operatorname{polylog}N)$ expected bit operations without supplying any sign or common divisor. One may cap the widths at the input size since all local sizes are at most $N$ and repeat the last width until success. The cost and sampling analysis are on paper. A product can still have four small signed gcds, leaving this bound exponential in its bit length. [An explicit family with four small signed gcds](cyclotomic-periods.md)

## A checked separation on 24257

Take $N=24257$, $D=-1$ and $u=(3+4w)/5$. The inverse of 5 is 9703 modulo $N$, and $u\bar u=1$. The public Jacobi symbol is 1. Binary powering gives $u^{24256}=23494+0w$. The gcd of $23493$ and $24257$ is $191$, yielding the prime factorization $127\cdot191$. The ordinary base-two projection gives $2^{24256}\equiv9146\pmod N$ and $\gcd(9145,N)=1$. Here $\gcd(126,190)=2$ and $\gcd(128,192)=64$. More generally, if $g_-=\gcd(p-1,q-1)$ and $g_+=\gcd(p+1,q+1)$, their gcd divides 2 and their lcm divides $q-p$. Lean proves $g_-g_+\le2(q-p)$. [Arithmetic example and gap bound](../PrimeFactors/JacobiSeparation.lean)

## How many products benefit from the successor case alone

Fix $1/2<\alpha<1$ and put $H=X^\alpha$. Among distinct primes in $I=(3X/2,7X/4]$, there are $\Theta_\alpha(X^{2-\alpha}/(\log X)^2)$ products with $\gcd(p+1,q+1)\ge H$, both primes congruent to 3 modulo 4, and $q-p\ge\delta_\alpha X$ for some fixed $\delta_\alpha>0$. On every one of them the fixed discriminant $D=-1$ gives expected $O_\alpha(N^{(1-\alpha)/4}\operatorname{polylog}N)$ bit operations. All three other signed gcds are $O(X^{1-\alpha})$. Indeed a predecessor and successor gcd satisfy the gap bound above. For the two mixed cases, $\gcd(n,r-1)\gcd(n,r+1)\le 2n$ since their common gcd divides 2 and both divide $n$. Set $n=p+1$ or $n=q+1$ to obtain the two bounds. This distinguishes the guaranteed table widths for these four choices. Individual bases can still split earlier. [Checked gcd inequalities](../PrimeFactors/JacobiSeparation.lean)

Here is the change to the predecessor counting proof. Write $P_d$ for the number of primes $r\in I$ satisfying $d\mid r+1$ and $r\equiv3\pmod4$. Restrict the complementary divisor $k$ of $r+1$ to multiples of 4 in $[X/H,1.1X/H]$. The primes $r\equiv-1\pmod k$ automatically meet the mod-4 condition and give $d=(r+1)/k\in[H,2H]$. The sum of $1/\varphi(k)$ over these multiples of 4 is bounded below by a positive constant, using $\varphi(k)\le k$. Bombieri–Vinogradov applies at both interval endpoints because $k\ll X^{1-\alpha}$. Thus $\sum_{H\le d\le2H}P_d\gg_\alpha X/\log X$. For upper bounds one may drop the mod-4 condition. The interval Brun–Titchmarsh estimate applies to residue $-1$ at each modulus, and the elementary large-modulus bound is $P_L\le(2X+1)/L$ since $r+1$ is a positive multiple of $L$. The two Cauchy–Schwarz estimates, removal of pairs within $\delta_\alpha X$, and matching upper bound then follow from the predecessor proof with these substitutions. The resulting proportion has order $X^{-\alpha}$ among all pairs in the box. This frequency result is an unconditional asymptotic argument on paper. [Detailed incidence and second-moment proof](common-divisor-frequency.md) · [Bombieri–Vinogradov, equation (1.2)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2006.06572#page=2) · [Montgomery and Vaughan, Theorem 2](https://personal.science.psu.edu/rcv4/personal/Publications/large_sieve.pdf#page=3)

## How far the character information goes

For an ordinary integer base $D$, a negative Jacobi symbol says that the two Legendre signs differ. If $p-1$ and $q-1$ have equal 2-adic valuation, then the nonresidue has maximal 2-part in its order and the residue has a strictly smaller one. Without the equal-valuation hypothesis, even the order discrepancy can disappear. At $N=39$, the base $-1$ has Jacobi symbol $-1$ and order 2 at both 3 and 13. Every power of this base therefore reaches 1 at both primes simultaneously. [Checked counterexample](../PrimeFactors/JacobiSeparation.lean)

The common kernel of the two Legendre characters has index four in $(\mathbb Z/N\mathbb Z)^\times$. Each ordered sign pair specifies one of its cosets. Under Dirichlet GRH, for $N\ge20000$, Theorem 1.4 of Lamzouri, Li and Soundararajan gives a prime $D$ in each coset bounded by $\max(10^9,(3\log N+15+\tfrac52(\log\log N)^2)^2)$, with natural logarithms. Scanning the primes in this public window covers all four choices. The Jacobi symbol still reveals only the product of the signs. The bound concerns $D$ and supplies neither a large signed gcd nor a small deterministic seed $z$ outside the power kernel. The randomized estimate above is unconditional. The application of the analytic theorem is on paper and independent of the Lean algebra. [Lamzouri, Li and Soundararajan, Theorem 1.4, arXiv v3](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1309.3595#page=3) · [Dirichlet GRH proof context](../docs/research.md)
