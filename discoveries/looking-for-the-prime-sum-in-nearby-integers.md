# Looking for the prime sum in nearby integers

Starting with **108,671**, add four. Dividing the resulting 108,675 by small primes exposes the divisor 675. It predicts a prime sum of $675-4+1=672$. Taking the square root of $672^2-4\times108{,}671$ gives 130, so the primes are **271 and 401**. There is also a checked continued-fraction explanation for this hit. The product of the two prime predecessors is $270\times400=108{,}000$. The public exponent $108{,}675/7=15{,}525$ reaches the exact ratio $15{,}525/108{,}000=23/160$ in its fourth quotient block. This example was chosen from the small census to show how the two calculations agree. ## When adding a shift can work

Write $F=(p-1)(q-1)$ and $D=N-F=p+q-1$. Choose a positive shift $k\le D$ and an exponent $1\le E\le N$ dividing $N+k$. The intermediate fractions of $E/N$ contain the desired ratio $E/F$ **exactly when** $E$ divides $D+k$ or $D+k$ divides $E$.

In the first case the candidate $F$ is a multiple of $E$, which the initial reciprocal guesses already check. In the second, put $g=D+k$, $a=E/g$ and $d=(N+k)/E$. If it is a new hit, $a,d\ge2$. The actual fraction is $a/(ad-1)$ and its position in the fourth block is $a-1$. This gives a direct way to test divisors of the neighbor. For a proposed $g\mid N+k$ in the public range, try $S=g-k+1$ as the prime sum. It takes one nonnegative perfect-square test to verify the proposal. The hard part is finding a useful divisor. [Equivalence and constructive row](../PrimeFactors/IntermediateShiftedExponent.lean)

If we grant every exponent $1\le E\le N$ dividing $N+k$, a hit beyond the initial reciprocal guesses exists exactly when $g=D+k$ divides $N+k$ and the complementary quotient $(N+k)/g$ is composite. For $N=209$ and $k=1$ the correct divisor is 30, but the complementary quotient is the prime 7. This yields a factor from the direct sum test alone. Shift seven has a later hit. The construction using $k=q-p-1$ works for a broad residue class of prime pairs, but finding that shift would already reveal their gap. ## Cost and coverage

For each of the first 64 shifts we remove all prime powers through 31 from $N+k$. Write the result as $N+k=HR$, with $H$ the fully factored part. We can form the divisors of $H$ and those divisors multiplied by the entire remainder $R$. If $H$ falls below the public interval for $g$ and $R$ is above it, we skip that shift. Any actual solution would then require a proper divisor of $R$. This skip applied to every tested shift on both 2,048-bit inputs. It uses the product alone. [Skipping proof and what an unresolved divisor must satisfy](../PrimeFactors/ShiftedDivisorReadout.lean)

The scan found 466 of all 903 products of two distinct nine-bit primes. The simpler choice that tested just the extracted parts and the remaining cofactors found 109. A retrospective count using the known primes shows that 543 products have any correct shifted divisor in the first 64 neighbors, even with every divisor available. Of the 466 recoveries, 404 also supplied a later continued-fraction witness. We spent 771,223 small-prime divisibility checks and 1,092 candidate square roots. Giving Fermat the same total square-root budget per input found 339 products. The two methods shared 313 successes. This comparison leaves the extra divisibility and divisor-generation work visible. The bounded scan recovered one of 64 independently selected products with 12-, 20-, 28- or 36-bit factors. That one recovery, 3,167 × 3,209 at shift 41, also took only the first ordinary Fermat midpoint. The shifted search found neither 2,048-bit pair. Ordinary Fermat already resolved all 903 small cases within 1,024 midpoint tests. For uniformly chosen distinct 1,024-bit primes whose product has 2,048 bits, fewer than one pair in $2^{976}$ admits the required divisor at **any** shift from 1 through 64. The bound grants all divisors of every neighbor for free. For shift one alone the fraction is below $2^{-988}$. In the other direction, fix a single eligible product and draw a uniform shift from 1 through $2^{1024}$. Even with the best exponent supplied for that shift, the probability of a new intermediate hit is less than $2^{-784}$. These are different sampling experiments. ## What the known control factors tell us

A separate calculation used the published primes to form $F$ and $D$ exactly. One weighted square test on each control certified that $F$ has no divisor in $(D,D+2^{343}]$ for the fresh input and $(D,D+2^{342}]$ for the crossed input. Thus every positive shift through $2^{343}$ is excluded on the fresh input and every positive shift through $2^{342}$ on the crossed input for this shifted-divisor strategy. We also certified a successful shift computed from the known prime gap on each input. The exact first successful shift remains unknown. These checks describe the two controls after their factors are supplied.
