# Getting a factor from a single quadratic-form class

We recovered 23 and 31 from 713 by choosing the twist prime 3 and squaring a form built from the prime 5. The method applies to any product $N=pq\equiv1\pmod4$ of two distinct odd primes. It uses a public auxiliary prime to arrange that one carefully chosen form suffices to recover a factor. Finding a suitable power of that form still requires an expensive class-number search. [Checked example and group identities](../PrimeFactors/AmbiguousTwist.lean)

## Choose a twist from the product

Scan primes $\ell\equiv3\pmod4$ until the Legendre symbol $(N/\ell)=-1$. A zero symbol already gives a factor unless $N=\ell$, excluded by the input promise. Put $D=-\ell N$ and let $G$ be the proper class group of primitive positive definite binary quadratic forms of discriminant $D$. This is a fundamental discriminant with three prime-discriminant factors $p^*,q^*,-\ell$, where $r^*=(-1)^{(r-1)/2}r$. Genus theory gives $|G/G^2|=4$ and hence exactly four classes whose square is the identity. [Genus characters and the principal genus theorem](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1109.1228#page=5)

One involution is public. The ramified form $T=[\ell,\ell,c]$ with $c=(N+\ell)/4$ is primitive and equivalent to its inverse by the substitution $x\mapsto x-y$ applied to the middle coefficient. It represents $c$, which is coprime to $D$. Its genus character $\chi$ at the prime discriminant $-\ell$ therefore has the value

$$\chi(T)=(c/\ell)=(N/\ell)=-1.$$

The second equality follows from $4c\equiv N\pmod\ell$. The identity class has sign $+1$. Every pair $g,gT$ has one member of each sign. Thus $G$ is the direct product of $\langle T\rangle$ and $K=\ker\chi$. Exactly two members of $K$ square to the identity. Denote the nonidentity one by $S$. Normalizing $g$ means retaining $g$ when its sign is positive and taking $gT$ otherwise. [Legendre equality, normalization and index-two bijection in Lean](../PrimeFactors/AmbiguousTwist.lean)

To see why $S$ exposes a factor, reduce it to $[a,b,c]$. For an odd negative discriminant the ambiguous reduced forms have $b=a$ or $a=c$ with positive $b$. In the first case set $(u,v)=(a,4c-a)$ and in the second set $(u,v)=(2a-b,2a+b)$. Both give $uv=\ell N$. Conversely each unordered bipartition $u\le v$ of $\ell N$ gives exactly one of these forms. When $3u\le v$ it is $[u,u,(u+v)/4]$. When $v<3u$ it is $[(u+v)/4,(v-u)/2,(u+v)/4]$. The integrality follows from $uv\equiv3\pmod4$ and primitivity from squarefreeness. The partitions $\{1,\ell N\}$ and $\{\ell,N\}$ give the identity and $T$. For the second partition, writing $c=(u+v)/4$, the determinant-one change $(x,y)\mapsto(x+y,-2x-y)$ identifies $[u,u,c]$ with $[v,v,c]$. The change $(x,y)\mapsto(-y,x+y)$ gives $[c,2c-u,c]$ when the symmetric representative is needed. The other two partitions both give $1<\gcd(u,N)<N$. [Reduction and equivalence operations](https://flintlib.org/doc/algorithms/number-theory/binary-quadratic-forms.html) · [Proper-gcd proof](../PrimeFactors/AmbiguousTwist.lean)

## Find a class that reaches the factor

Try odd primes $r\nmid D$ with $(D/r)=1$. A root of $b^2\equiv D\pmod{4r}$ gives the form $g=[r,b,(b^2-D)/(4r)]$. Its public sign is $(r/\ell)$. Normalize $g$ to obtain $x\in K$. Given a positive annihilator $e$ of $x$, remove all powers of two from $e$ to obtain the odd part $u$. Starting at $x^u$, square until reaching the identity. If $x$ has even order, the last nonidentity class must be $S$, so its reduced form gives a factor. [Checked extraction](../PrimeFactors/AmbiguousTwist.lean)

There is a class of suitable primes even though we cannot evaluate the two hidden genus characters. Across residue classes coprime to $2\ell N$, the three prime-discriminant signs take all eight patterns by quadratic reciprocity and CRT. The split patterns have product $+1$. Let $t$ be the three signs of $T$; its third entry is $-1$. The two patterns $(-1,-1,+1)$ and $(-1,-1,+1)t$ both normalize to $(-1,-1,+1)$. Their normalized classes have even order, since an odd-order element cannot have a negative sign under a real character. The two patterns together are one coset of the subgroup $\{1,t\}$ of the eight patterns, hence one coset of index four on units. The search tests split primes in order and recognizes success from the factor.

The initial twist also requires a single index-four coset, now modulo $4N$, determined by $\ell\equiv3\pmod4$ and $(\ell/N)=-1$. Under Dirichlet GRH the least-prime-in-a-coset bound gives a prime in each required coset by $10^9$ at 2,048 bits. Indeed Theorem 1.4 gives the maximum of $10^9$ and $(3\log Q+15+\tfrac52(\log\log Q)^2)^2$ for index four and $Q\ge20000$; first take $Q=4N$ and then $Q=2\ell N$. Both logarithmic terms are below $10^9$. The existence of suitable primes also follows unconditionally from Dirichlet's theorem without this explicit search bound. [Lamzouri, Li and Soundararajan, Theorem 1.4](https://arxiv.org/html/1309.3595v3#S1.SS2) · [Released Dirichlet statement and project assumptions](../docs/research.md)

If $[L,U]$ contains the class number $h(D)$, an interval search for $|K|=h(D)/2$ gives an annihilator. One can also search for $h(D)/4$ using $x^2$, then double the matching exponent. This last saving uses the familiar genus divisor four. The scan needs one interval search per trial prime and no search over pairs of trial primes. The twist changes the discriminant and can widen the interval. The existing analytic estimate still gives fifth-root-scale work up to logarithmic factors. We have not obtained a narrow class-number interval for a general 2,048-bit input. [Interval algorithm, cost and earlier pair construction](quadratic-class-number.md)

## The calculation on 713

The first twist prime is 3, since $713\equiv2\pmod3$. At $D=-2139$ the prime 2 is inert and 3 ramifies. The first split prime, 5, gives $g=[5,1,107]$. Its sign $(5/3)$ is negative. Its normalized form is $gT=[15,-9,37]$, obtained by taking a middle coefficient congruent to $1\bmod10$ and $3\bmod6$. The square of the normalized form equals $g^2$ because $T^2=1$. Since $\gcd(5,1)=1$, Gauss composition squares $g$ by lifting its middle coefficient from $1\bmod10$ to $-19\bmod50$. The result is $[25,-19,25]$, equivalent to $[25,19,25]$. Thus the two parts of $3N$ are $50-19=31$ and $50+19=69$. Taking their gcds with 713 gives 31 and 23. The arithmetic and the primality of both factors are checked in Lean.
