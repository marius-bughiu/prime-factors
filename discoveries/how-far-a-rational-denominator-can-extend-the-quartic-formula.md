# Even every denominator through 2⁵¹² covers a tiny fraction

Consider a 2,048-bit product of two distinct 1,024-bit primes. Suppose we could choose the best denominator for this input for free, from **every integer between 1 and $2^{512}$**. Fewer than **one in $2^{227}$** eligible products have a quartic representation that either fourth-root formula can read at any of these scales. The proof counts products with at least one such representation, including successes at more than one scale just once. [Population theorem](../PrimeFactors/AllSmallQuarticDenominatorCoverage.lean)

This range includes every $d$ satisfying $6d^4\le N$, the known size condition that guarantees a successful rational quartic representation splits the two primes by gcd. The counting theorem allows larger denominators than this guarantee requires. Its scope is the two public fourth-root recognition intervals. An arbitrary rational point could have coordinates outside both intervals. [Conditional recovery from supplied coordinates](a-rational-quartic-witness-and-its-cost.md)

## Why adding so many scales still falls short

At a successful scale, $d^4N=x^4+4y^4$. In one interval the formula reads $x$ first, in the other it reads $y$ first. Call that coordinate the *leading coordinate*. On a squarefree input we can cancel any common divisor of the coordinates and the denominator. The reduced point still lies in the same recognition interval. It therefore suffices to count coprime $x,y$. The denominator of such a point is odd, and both coordinates are coprime to it. [Cancellation](../PrimeFactors/ReduceQuarticDenominator.lean) · [Coprimality of the coordinates and denominator](../PrimeFactors/QuarticUnitLift.lean)

For $d\le2^{200}$, the leading coordinate is below $2^{712}$ and the other below $2^{535}$. Counting the possible coordinate pairs suffices. For $2^{200}<d\le2^{512}$ the leading coordinate is below $d^4$ and the other below $2^{769}$. At fixed $d$ and fixed smaller coordinate, the entire leading coordinate is then determined by its remainder modulo $d$ among values satisfying the congruence. [Coordinate bounds](../PrimeFactors/QuarticStripCoordinateBounds.lean)

To see why, suppose two candidate values $z,w$ are in the same remainder class modulo $d$ and have equal fourth powers modulo $d^4$. Since $d$ is odd and $w$ is coprime to it, both $z+w$ and $z^2+w^2$ are coprime to $d$. Cancelling these in the difference of fourth powers proves $z\equiv w\pmod{d^4}$. Both lie in $[0,d^4)$, so they are equal. The coefficient four in the other branch is also invertible modulo $d^4$. This uniqueness holds for composite denominators as well. [Fourth-power uniqueness for every power of an odd denominator](../PrimeFactors/QuarticUnitLift.lean)

The larger box therefore contributes at most $d\cdot2^{769}\le2^{1281}$ products per branch, per denominator. The smaller box fits the same bound. Including both branches and every denominator gives at most $2^{1794}$ products. The repository’s proved lower bound on the number of eligible prime pairs turns that count into the fraction above. This is an upper bound on what the formula can recognize, even with ideal denominator selection. [Per-denominator proof](../PrimeFactors/LargeQuarticDenominatorCoverage.lean) · [Total and denominator range](../PrimeFactors/AllSmallQuarticDenominatorCoverage.lean)

## What if all the congruence tests pass?

Giving the search that information still leaves a large gap. About **one eighth of all eligible prime products** pass every locally primitive quartic test at every modulus and have **no successful reading at any denominator through $2^{512}$**. With the published Dirichlet-GRH prime-count estimates as inputs, the share differs from one eighth by less than $2^{-145}$. Any true reading passes the local tests, so subtracting the recognized set gives this result directly.
