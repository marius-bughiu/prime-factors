# Choosing the digit base for a factor search

Changing how we write the two unknown primes let us skip more of the search. On eight fresh products, the old search checked 4,478 possible pairs and the new one checked 1,853. Both found the two primes in every case and finished the entire search. Simply trying each odd divisor in the same interval was faster on all eight products. At 2,048 bits the new search would still have to visit more than $2^{680}$ groups of possible factors. ## Changing the radix

Let the input be an odd $N=pq$ with two unknown primes $p\le q\le2p$. Write $x=u+Rv$ and $y=w+Rz$. If $R$ is coprime to $N$, the low digit $u$ must be a unit and $w=Nu^{-1}\bmod R$. Set $d=(N-uw)/R$. Then

$$xy\equiv N\pmod{R^2}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad z\equiv u^{-1}d - Nu^{-2}v\pmod R.$$

The high digits are limited by the balanced factor intervals. We count points of each affine graph inside those intervals using Euclidean division, then test the surviving pairs by multiplying. This test works at every coprime radix. Its existing Lean proof already used an arbitrary nonzero radix. [Search and proof](modular-hyperbola.md)

Let $b$ be the bit length of $\lfloor\sqrt{2N}\rfloor$ and $Q=2^{\lceil2b/3\rceil}$ the previous radix. Take the largest product $W$ of consecutive primes starting at 2 with $W\le Q/16$, using 1 when necessary. Choose $R=W\lfloor Q/W\rfloor$. This choice uses the input length alone. The implementation falls back to $Q$ if $\gcd(R,N)>1$ or $R^2\le\lfloor\sqrt{2N}\rfloor$. For sufficiently large balanced prime pairs both checks always pass because $15Q/16\le R\le Q$ and $R<p$. For $Q<16$ the empty wheel also gives $R=Q$. [Implementation](../experiments/modular_hyperbola.py)

The binary radix visits $Q/2$ low-digit classes. Our radix visits $\varphi(R)$. One elementary upper bound is $O(Q/\log\log Q)$ for sufficiently large $Q$. Indeed for $z=\lfloor\sqrt{\log_2 Q}\rfloor$, the product of all primes up to $z$ is at most $z^z\le Q/16$ eventually. Those primes therefore divide $W$. Expanding $\prod_{p\le z}(1-1/p)^{-1}$ as geometric series includes every term of the harmonic sum through $z$, which is at least $\log(z+1)$. This proves the claimed bound on the low classes. The number of high-digit pairs and the cost of their range counts require separate accounting. The earlier worst-case cube-root exponent still applies.

We enumerate units directly. When adjoining a prime power $p^e$ coprime to the current prefix modulus $m$, the lifts of one unit $a$ are $a+mt$ for $0\le t<p^e$. Exactly $p^{e-1}$ are divisible by $p$, so every parent costs at most twice the number of retained children. Except possibly at the first stage for the prime power 2, each parent has at least two children. The total number of divisibility tests in this traversal is at most $4\varphi(R)+2$. A depth-first traversal stores one path. To factor the public radix, ordinary trial division suffices here. All the prime factors of $R/W$ are at most $R/W<16p_{\rm next}$ where $p_{\rm next}$ is the first prime excluded from $W$. The setup tests are included in the measurements. For a uniform bound, let $p$ be the largest prime in $W$. For $p>2$ the preceding prime is at least $p/2$ and $p_{\rm next}\le2p$ by Bertrand’s postulate. Thus $p^2\le2W$ and the selector visits $O(\sqrt Q)$ integers, each compared with at most $\log_2 Q$ stored primes. Trial factoring $R\le Q$ also takes $O(\sqrt Q)$ divisibility checks. The class-enumeration saving therefore survives these setup costs. The earlier bound on possible *integer products* of modular hits contains $1+O(N/R^2)$ terms. Replacing $Q$ by $R\ge15Q/16$ can increase that bound by a constant factor and gives no uniform logarithmic saving in reporting the hits. [Bertrand’s theorem in Mathlib](https://github.com/leanprover-community/mathlib4/blob/v4.19.0/Mathlib/NumberTheory/Bertrand.lean)

## Which low-digit graphs can share work?

Let $U=(\mathbb Z/R)^\times$, $T=\#\{u\in U:u^2=1\}$, and $A=-N U^2$. The slopes form exactly $A$, of size $S=\varphi(R)/T$. The preimages of a slope represented by $u$ are precisely $u$ times the square roots of 1. For any slope we may translate either axis, reverse either axis, or swap the two coordinates. These operations preserve cyclic intervals. They identify exactly the slopes among $s,-s,s^{-1},-s^{-1}$ that belong to $A$. A translation can adjust the intercept arbitrarily. Distinct slopes on the full residue domain are distinguished by the difference in values at 1 and 0. This does not count implementations that share partial computations between different graphs or use additional information about their particular windows. [Lean fiber and covering bounds](../PrimeFactors/AffineSlopeClasses.lean)

Inversion always preserves the coset $A$. Negation preserves it precisely when $-1\in U^2$. For $R>2$ write $f_+$ and $f_-$ for the numbers of $s\in A$ with $s^2=1$ and $s^2=-1$, respectively. If $-1$ is not a square the exact number of graph types is $(S+f_+)/2$. Otherwise it is $(S+f_++f_-)/4$. These follow by counting the fixed points of the two or four coordinate symmetries acting on $A$, since no unit equals its own negative for $R>2$.

Using a different base on each factor provides a further check. Write $a=x\bmod A_0$, $b=y\bmod B_0$, with both moduli coprime to $N$. The product gives $x\equiv N/b\pmod{B_0}$. The pair of digits is compatible exactly when the two residues for $x$ agree modulo $\gcd(A_0,B_0)$. The Chinese remainder theorem thus puts compatible unit pairs in bijection with one unit modulo $L=\operatorname{lcm}(A_0,B_0)$. A product test modulo $A_0B_0=L\gcd(A_0,B_0)$ has at most the precision of the symmetric test modulo $L^2$ on the same low classes. The counts for the resulting high-digit queries may differ. [Lean equivalence and refinement](../PrimeFactors/AffineSlopeClasses.lean)

## Exactly 2,048 bits

The public rule gives $Q=2^{684}$, $W$ the product of the first 94 primes through 491, and $R=320W$. All target primes exceed 491. This radix is divisible by $2^7$ and has 93 distinct odd prime factors. At an odd prime power there are two square roots of 1, since a prime cannot divide both $s-1$ and $s+1$. At $2^e$ with $e\ge3$ there are four, since one of the two consecutive even neighbors of odd $s$ has exactly one factor of 2. CRT therefore gives $T=2^{95}$. The number of low classes is $320\prod_{p\mid W}(p-1)>2^{680}$; the number of slopes lies between $2^{585}$ and $2^{586}-2^{95}$. Because $4\mid R$, negation exchanges square cosets and $f_+\le 2^{95}$. Even granting free translations, reversals and swaps, there are strictly between $2^{584}$ and $2^{585}$ graph types. The binary radix has $2^{681}$ slopes and at least $2^{680}$ such types. These graph counts do not give equally large savings in the time taken to find a factor. [Kernel-checked integer cutoffs](../PrimeFactors/AffineSlopeClasses.lean) · [Prime-power units](https://kconrad.math.uconn.edu/blurbs/gradnumthy/primepowerunitsandGLnQ.pdf)

Could another similarly sized radix do much better? For every $2^{683}\le R\le2^{685}$ coprime to $N$ there are **more than $2^{581}$ graph types**, even allowing the four orientations. The product of the first 96 primes exceeds $2^{685}$, so $R$ has at most 95 distinct prime factors. Its unit fraction is at least the product of $1-1/p$ over the smallest 95 primes, which exceeds $1/12$. The prime-power root counts give $T\le2^{96}$ including the possible extra factor at 2. Every type accounts for at most four slopes. The finite comparisons and this last numerical implication are checked in Lean. The prime-factor sorting and CRT arguments in this paragraph are on paper.

## Independent products and costs

On the earlier 41-bit input $1{,}900{,}014{,}700{,}027$, the binary radix 16,384 had 8,192 unit lanes, 1,024 graph types, 64,561 Euclidean steps and 422 candidate pairs. The nearby radix 16,380 had 3,456 lanes, 54 types, 26,824 steps and 160 candidates. Both recovered $1{,}000{,}003\times1{,}900{,}009$. The graph types here can be reproduced by collecting the least residue of $s,-s,s^{-1},-s^{-1}$ for each unit slope. The implementation processes every unit lane separately. [Decimal benchmark fixtures](../benchmarks/modular-hyperbola.json)

We fixed the radix rule before drawing eight new pairs of distinct 23-bit primes, taking the first accepted SHAKE256 outputs on two separate streams. There was no rejection based on search outcome, spacing or product width. Both searches saw only the product, completed the whole search and recovered all eight pairs. Lean verified each factorization. Across these products the radices were 65,536 and 64,680. The low-lane totals were 262,144 and 107,520, the Euclidean steps were 1,956,315 and 805,778, and the candidate counts were 4,478 and 1,853. The new digit traversal made 16,412 small divisibility tests per product.

We then added independent odd trial division over exactly the same possible smaller factors, from the first odd integer at or above $\lceil\sqrt{N/2}\rceil$ through $\lfloor\sqrt N\rfloor$. It tested 7,210,669 divisors across the eight products and completed every interval after finding a factor. The three methods used the same arm64 Mac. For each product we rotated their order over five runs and checked every returned pair. Taking the median of the eight per-product medians gave **0.155 seconds for the binary radix, 0.067 for the primorial radix and 0.032 for ordinary trial division**. Trial division was fastest on every product. The times include setup, low-digit enumeration, range counts and candidate testing as applicable. The earlier two-way measurement gave 0.140 and 0.062 seconds, respectively. Run `python3 experiments/affine_radix_holdout.py --verify` to repeat the comparison and replay its certificates.
