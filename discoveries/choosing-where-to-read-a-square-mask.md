# Choosing where to read a square mask

**One count gives the sum of the two primes.** Start with the Jacobi symbol of each residue $t$ modulo $N=pq$ and multiply its argument by $(t-1)^2$. At $N=2{,}183$, the symbol changes on **93** residues. Adding three gives $p+q=96$. The discriminant is $96^2-4\cdot2{,}183=484=22^2$, so the primes are 37 and 59. The complete *signed sum* of these same masked readings is just **1**. Computing that exact count cheaply from a large product remains open. [Prime-sum identity](../PrimeFactors/CircleMarkChangedPrimeSum.lean) · [Independent count and recovered factors](../benchmarks/shared-square-mask-samples.json)

An individual change can also reveal a prime. Of those 93 residues, 57 give $\gcd(t-1,2183)=37$, and 35 give 59. The remaining residue is $t=1$, whose gcd is the whole input. In general the counts are $q-2$, $p-2$ and one, so the total is $p+q-3$ for distinct odd primes. Equivalently the count is $N-\varphi(N)-2$, so knowing it exactly carries the same information as knowing the totient. Scanning the positive residues in order, the first change after 1 occurs at $p+1$. The surviving units are exactly the adjacent pairs $t-1,t$ coprime to $N$, counted by the classical second Schemmel totient $(p-2)(q-2)$. [Singleton gcd partition and first hit](../PrimeFactors/CircleMarkSingletonChanges.lean) · [Schemmel (1869)](https://doi.org/10.1515/9783112389409-015) · [Definition on prime powers](https://arxiv.org/abs/1412.3080)

For **2,183**, two carefully chosen squares find the factor **37**. Take the five offsets 24, 22, 20, 18 and 16. At each test square $x^2$, multiply the five differences $x^2-y^2$ modulo 2,183 and take the gcd of the product with 2,183. Starting with $x=23$ gives 1. Trying $x=13$ gives 37. The two rows cost ten modular multiplications by root terms and two gcds, plus forming the squares. The second gcd already gives the prime. To identify which offset caused the hit, one extra gcd at $y=24$ suffices. Ordinary trial division in the balanced interval finds 37 in **two divisions ascending or five descending**. [Directed-row proof](../PrimeFactors/CircleMarkDirectedDigitQueries.lean) · [Recorded counts and witness checks](../benchmarks/shared-square-mask-samples.json)

The construction uses only $N$ and the promise that $N=pq$ for distinct odd primes $p<q<2p$. Exactly one row and offset satisfy $x+y=p$. Every difference $x-y$ is coprime to $N$, so the row gcd equals the gcd of the product of its candidate sums $x+y$. Those sums are consecutive odd integers in descending order. For 2,183 the first row starts at 47, one place above the largest feasible odd divisor, 45. The second row starts at 37. Grouping the candidates saves individual gcds, but computing the row products still costs work. Classical polynomial batching already evaluates blocks of candidates on the fourth-root scale in $N$, up to logarithmic factors. [Interval and work identities](../PrimeFactors/CircleMarkDirectedDigitWork.lean) · [Earlier factorial-progression experiments](factorial-progressions.md) · [Bostan, Gaudry and Schost, introduction](https://specfun.inria.fr/bostan/publications/BoGaSc07.pdf#page=3)

What if we try a uniform random residue? For the six digit questions at 2,183, **638** of the 2,183 residues change at least one reading from the unmasked Jacobi symbol. On **556** of these, two questions give different readings. On the other **82**, every question gives zero despite a nonzero unmasked symbol. For any two distinct 1,024-bit primes, a uniform draw from *all* residues changes at least one question built from the public digit coordinates with probability below $2^{-509}$, even if every such question is asked. A 50% chance of seeing one such event needs more than $2^{508}$ independent draws. [Shared-event theorem](../PrimeFactors/CircleMarkSharedOccurrence.lean) · [57-product census](../experiments/shared_square_mask_samples.py)

## Counts, costs and precision

- **Longer prefixes.** Using roots $1^2,\dots,d^2$ changes exactly $H=d(p+q-2-d)$ readings when $1\le d\le(p-1)/2$. Consequently $H/d+d+2=p+q$. Within this range the survivor count matches the value of the $(d+1)$st Schemmel totient since each prime excludes $d+1$ distinct classes. At 2,183 the count is 184 for $d=2$ and 1,368 for $d=18$. At $d=19$ a square first repeats modulo 37, and the observed 1,386 differs from the formula's 1,425. The experiment checked 796 eligible product/length pairs and the first length outside the range on each of 57 products. [Prefix count](../PrimeFactors/CircleMarkChangedPrimeSum.lean) · [Bounded controls](../benchmarks/shared-square-mask-samples.json)
- **Exact work through the first row hit.** Put $K=\lfloor\sqrt N/2\rfloor$, $m=\lfloor\sqrt K\rfloor+1$ and $s=(2K+1-p)/2$. For $K\ge2$, the first hit is row $\lfloor s/m\rfloor$, starting at zero. Through that row direct evaluation multiplies $m(\lfloor s/m\rfloor+1)$ root terms. Descending odd trials from $2K+1$ reach $p$ after $s+1$ candidates. The excess is exactly $m-1-(s\bmod m)$. Applying the balanced bounds first can shorten the trial interval further. Each original digit question also corresponds to a product of selected odd candidates whose gcd with $N$ is either 1 or $p$. [Row count and overrun](../PrimeFactors/CircleMarkDirectedDigitWork.lean) · [Candidate-product equivalence](../PrimeFactors/CircleMarkDigitCandidateProduct.lean)
- **Measured work.** On all 57 balanced products whose larger prime has 3–6 bits, the first hit took 66 row evaluations and 297 modular root multiplications in total. Taking 66 row gcds recovered every smaller prime. A separate check computed 66 Jacobi symbols to identify the hitting rows and then used 151 individual gcds to locate the offsets, followed by 57 divisibility confirmations. The ascending and descending trial scans took 171 and 167 divisions. [Experiment and counting rules](../experiments/shared_square_mask_samples.py) · [Results](../benchmarks/shared-square-mask-samples.json)
- **Counting changed draws.** Let $U$ contain all queried coordinates, and let $a_r$ count the distinct nonzero squares of those coordinates modulo $r$. Then $L(U)=(p-1-a_p)(q-1-a_q)$ counts the residues surviving every mask. Exactly $(p-1)(q-1)-L(U)$ draws change from the unmasked symbol. For two masks, summing the product of their scores over all residues gives $L$ of their union. At 2,183 the eight coordinates leave 1,450 of 2,088 units alive. Across the cohort, 23,594 draws change from the unmasked symbol, including 3,641 on which every mask gives zero. [Joint score identity](../PrimeFactors/CircleMarkSharedScores.lean) · [Exact common-event count](../PrimeFactors/CircleMarkSharedOccurrence.lean)
- **Estimating one digit sum.** For a nonempty query of size $d$, the two possible complete sums differ by $d$. Let $S$ be the true sum, $L$ its surviving-unit count, and $H=(p-1)(q-1)-L$. For $k$ independent uniform residues, averaging the masked scores scaled by $N$ gives mean squared error $(NL-S^2)/k$. Subtracting the unmasked symbol on each of the same draws gives $(NH-S^2)/k$. For two 1,024-bit primes whose product has 2,048 bits, root mean squared error below $d/2$ requires more than $2^{3069}$ draws for the ordinary mean and more than $2^{2558}$ after subtraction. Sampling uniformly without replacement leaves fewer than $2^{1025}$ or $2^{1536}$ residues unexamined respectively at that accuracy. These bounds concern those two estimators. [Ordinary-sampling proof](../PrimeFactors/CircleMarkDigitSampling.lean) · [Baseline-subtracted proof](../PrimeFactors/CircleMarkDigitCenteredSampling.lean) · [The two possible readings](../PrimeFactors/CircleMarkDigitQueryValue.lean)


## Which analytic reading contains the factor sum?

A periodic function on the residues modulo $N$ defines an $L$-function. Its residue at $s=1$ is the average of one complete period. The choice of function matters here. For the **signed** singleton score, the residue is $1/N$, already known from the product. Expanding this score in Dirichlet characters instead gives a principal coefficient $1/\varphi(N)$. The normalization is different because the average defining that coefficient runs over units. The singleton mask sends the unit 1 to zero, whereas every Dirichlet character takes the value 1 there. [Character expansion and signed residue](../PrimeFactors/CircleMarkMaskCharacters.lean) · [Mathlib's periodic residue theorem](https://github.com/leanprover-community/mathlib4/blob/c44e0c8ee63ca166450922a373c7409c5d26b00b/Mathlib/NumberTheory/LSeries/ZMod.lean#L133)

For the **indicator of a change**, the residue is $H/N$. At 2,183 the singleton residue is $93/2183$. Using the first 16 squares gives $1248/2183$. Even a supplied reading of $0.57$ for the latter gives the correct prime sum 96 after rounding. For a product of two distinct 1,024-bit primes we can choose $d=\lfloor\sqrt{\lfloor N/2\rfloor}/2\rfloor$ from the product. An absolute error below $2^{-1026}$ in that indicator residue suffices. The pinned RH repository states zero-location and nonvanishing results for Dirichlet $L$-functions. We have no deduction from those statements that evaluates this residue to the required precision for a specified product. [Precision and example](../PrimeFactors/CircleMarkChangedResidue.lean) · [Pinned RH statements](https://github.com/leanprover-community/riemann-hypothesis/blob/40e56bab/src/Statements.lean)

We tested an estimate available immediately from the product by substituting $2\sqrt N$ for $p+q$ in the residue formula. Measured in units of the allowed error $d/N$, its error is $(\sqrt q-\sqrt p)^2$ for every permitted prefix length. Across the 57 toy products it yielded the correct sum 32 times. The first Fermat test succeeded on those same 32 products and 14 more. At 2,183 the rounded geometric-mean prediction is 94; the true sum is 96. The successful reading of $0.57$ above was supplied.
