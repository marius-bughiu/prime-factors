# Do another 128 public bits predict the missing sum?

**Even the best possible predictor gets the exact sum remainder right only about once in 537,510 tries.** It sees the product modulo 7,239,457,225 and all of its lowest 128 bits for free. It can use any rule or lookup table to predict one value of $p+q$ modulo 7,239,457,225. The bound covers the entire population of distinct 1,024-bit primes with a 2,048-bit product, assuming the explicit Dirichlet-GRH prime-count estimates at the larger modulus. The input to this predictor leaves the other digits unread. [Full-size prediction theorem](../PrimeFactors/RefinedSumPrediction.lean)

Guessing the remainder of either prime gives the same answer as before to the displayed precision. Even after seeing those additional bits, the best ordering still averages **980,179,200.50 guesses**. The mathematical benchmark is $980{,}179{,}200+1/2+8/3{,}920{,}716{,}800$. Lean bounds the difference between the best prime-population mean and this benchmark by $2^{-113}$ guess. The count measures where the correct pair appears in the list. ## Why extra digits of the product have so little effect here

Take a smaller example. Suppose we know the product remainder modulo 25 and then get its lowest four bits. Together those observations specify the product modulo 400. At each invertible product modulo 400, fixing a possible first-factor remainder modulo 25 leaves exactly eight first-factor remainders modulo 400. Each determines exactly one compatible second factor remainder. The eightfold repetition is the same for every first remainder. The frequencies of all the possible coarse sums therefore stay exactly the same. Across all unit products in this model, the best single guess succeeds on **7 in 40** pairs both before and after reading the extra four bits. [Small exhaustive comparison](../experiments/refined_product_observations.py) · [General equal-fiber proof](../PrimeFactors/ProductResidueRefinement.lean)

For actual examples, $1{,}531\times1{,}571=2{,}405{,}201$ and $1{,}579\times1{,}619=2{,}556{,}401$. Both products leave remainder 1 modulo 400. Their sums leave different remainders modulo 25, namely 2 and 23. These close primes were deliberately selected, and their small integer certificates are independent of the population calculation. ## Counting the best single guess

Let $T=3{,}920{,}716{,}800$ be the number of invertible first-factor remainders at the five prime squares. At a square of a prime $r$, the most frequent possible sum has weight $r$ when the product is a square modulo $r$, and weight 2 otherwise. For a fixed product, the five weights multiply. Even the most favorable product pattern reaches only $85{,}085/T=1/46{,}080$. Averaging the best choice over all 32 product patterns gives exactly $57/30{,}638{,}080$ in the uniform modular model. Some predictor attains this value. [Exact frequencies and maximizing guesses](../PrimeFactors/FiveSquareOneSumGuess.lean)

The equally sized groups argument works for reduction from *any positive multiple* of the original modulus in the uniform model. For actual prime pairs we proved a quantitative transfer when that multiple is at most $2^{161}$. This covers the five-square modulus times $2^{128}$. The public remainder modulo the combined number records both observations because the five-square modulus is odd. Even the optimum across all predictors for the actual prime population differs from the exact modular probability by at most $2^{-146}$. The Lean theorem takes the published pointwise inequalities as premises. It is a population estimate, and permits exceptional products. ## What if we also read the leading digits?

Reading the next **64 bits after the leading one** still leaves the best single sum prediction between one success in 537,511 and one in 537,510. The predictor retains the 128 low bits and the five-square product remainder. It can pick a different lookup table for every leading-bit pattern. Across eligible prime pairs its optimum differs from $57/30{,}638{,}080$ by at most $2^{-81}$. Its best mean for guessing either factor remainder stays within $2^{-48}$ of the earlier benchmark. These results use the explicit prime-count estimates at the combined modulus. The leading bits divide the full product range into $2^{64}$ intervals. Some intervals contain many more prime pairs than others. The comparison retains the actual frequency of each interval, then bounds the difference between its residue counts. An absolute error bound allows even an empty interval to contribute correctly. The final bound applies across all eligible prime pairs. The size of the population matters. Among all **1,718** products of two distinct ten-bit primes with a twenty-bit product, fitting the best single-guess table to the entire sample gives 436 correct sums modulo 25 from the product modulo 25. Reading four more low bits raises this to 511, and three more high bits raise it to 1,065. These counts measure the best predictions on this small population. They illustrate why a finite example alone cannot settle the full-size question. [Reproduce the complete small census](../experiments/refined_product_observations.py)

There are also small cases that the observations leave indistinguishable. The two 22-bit examples above have leading binary digits 1001 and product remainder 1 modulo 400. A third deliberately chosen example, $2{,}479{,}601=1{,}543\times1{,}607$, has the same observations and sum remainder 0. The three correct answers are 2, 23 and 0.
