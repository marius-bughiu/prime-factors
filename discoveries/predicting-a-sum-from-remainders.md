# Can the low digits of a product predict the sum of its primes?

Suppose we know the lowest **512 bits** of a product of two 1,024-bit primes. We may use those bits in any way to choose 648 guesses for the sum. Even allowing each guess an error of $2^{400}$, fewer than one in $2^{487}$ target pairs can match. This is the same error allowance that let us find two deliberately chosen primes in four polynomial calculations. The bound applies to uniformly chosen unordered pairs of distinct 1,024-bit primes conditioned on a 2,048-bit product. ## How the count works

Write the odd product as $N=2z+1$. Imagine a procedure that reads successive remainders of $z$ and then chooses its guesses. Different branches may query different moduli. Each complete transcript fixes one class $z\equiv r\pmod m$. Classes from distinct transcripts are disjoint. Assume each applicable $m$ is coprime to the smaller prime $p$. Powers of two always satisfy this on our target. A query that discovers a proper gcd with the input is itself a factorization. [Transcript refinement lemma](../PrimeFactors/CRTResidueAdvice.lean)

Fix $p$ for the count. Then $z=p\lfloor q/2\rfloor+\lfloor p/2\rfloor$. A transcript fixes one possible residue for $\lfloor q/2\rfloor$ modulo $m$. For a guess $C$, the condition $|p+q-C|\le E$ confines that half-word to an interval of diameter $E$. At most $\lfloor E/m\rfloor+1$ partners remain. There is no restriction on the location of the interval. In $[0,E)$ the transcript owns at least $\lfloor E/m\rfloor$ integers. These sets of integers are disjoint, so all their floor terms together cost at most $E$.

Consequently $K$ guesses at each of $L$ leaves cover at most $K(E+L)$ partners per smaller prime. The moduli themselves can exceed $E$. Reading 512 low product bits gives at most $2^{511}$ leaves because the last bit is one. An additional 100 arbitrary binary answers about $N$ multiply the bound by at most $2^{100}$, leaving the fraction below $2^{-387}$. These estimates use the project's published prime-count inequality as an explicit hypothesis in Lean. An algorithm using the full product for further computations falls outside this observation model. ## How many sums pass a locally chosen test?

A different strategy can inspect **every digit** of $N$ to select a modulus $M$. It keeps a possible sum $s$ if it can find residues $x,y$ with $xy=N$ and $x+y=s$ modulo $M$. For every $1\le M\le2^{401}$ coprime to $N$, **every** interval of $2^{401}$ consecutive integers contains more than $2^{325}$ passing sums. The assertion also holds for several tests if their joint least common multiple is at most $2^{401}$. For odd $N$, including parity in that joint modulus ensures every survivor is even. Shorter clipped intervals and larger combined moduli require their own estimates. [Uniform bound and simultaneous tests](../PrimeFactors/LocalSumMenu.lean)

The proof counts the distinct sums modulo prime powers, combines counts across distinct primes, and charges the loss of density to the size of the modulus. In one complete period at least a fraction $2^{-75}$ survives. Complete periods inside the interval give the stated count. The two earlier comparison products each have a Lean certificate excluding the actual prime sum from every tested band. A modularly admissible sum can therefore survive without being the sum of the true factors.
