# How often does an initial weighted search reach the actual factors?

Imagine giving the search both a table of $2^{64}$ starting powers and permission to try $2^{64}$ blocks **at each** of its 23 weight pairs. Even then, fewer than one in $2^{421}$ of the target prime pairs can have its true weighted sum in a searched block. Allowing every reduced weight pair with a denominator up to $2^{32}$ raises that fraction to **at most one in $2^{360}$**. Here the target is a uniformly chosen pair of distinct 1,024-bit primes whose product has 2,048 bits. The count allows the choices to depend on the entire product. A search can also find a factor if two different exponents happen to give the same power modulo just one prime. The frequency of those additional successes is open. ## Two full-size examples where the starting cost is known exactly

For the two existing comparison products, we used their independently certified factors to answer a retrospective question. Suppose someone who knew the factors picked the best of the 23 weight pairs, assigned the factors to the weights in either order, and chose any table length. How cheaply could the search reach the actual weighted sum, counting one entry for each starting power and each consecutive block? Lean checked the exact minima at roughly **$1.6390\times10^{152}$** entries on the first input and **$1.7181\times10^{152}$** on the second. The best weights were $(1,1)$ and $(7,5)$ respectively. The factors serve as evidence for the cost calculation. [Exact integer results and proofs](../PrimeFactors/TrueTraceTableCost.lean) · [First input's primality certificates](../benchmarks/controls/PocklingtonControl.lean) · [Second input's factorization certificate](https://marius-bughiu.github.io/prime-factors/proofs/unit-quartic-crossed-2048.lean)

If the first true sum on the best row has zero-based index $i$, a table length $m$ and $g$ initial blocks reach it exactly when $mg\ge i+1$. With a total of $M$ entries the largest possible product comes from splitting $M$ as evenly as possible. Thus the least possible $M$ is $\lceil\sqrt{4(i+1)}\rceil$. We certify the index at the winning weight and check a lower bound at every other weight by comparing integer squares. This explains why even a [19-block plan](reusing-a-factor-search.md#what-does-this-buy-at-2048-bits) still needs an enormous table.

## From the smaller prime to the number of possible partners

Write $N=pq$, with $p\le q$, $U=2^{1024}$ and $b\le a\le2b$. Suppose a true sum appears in the first $g\le B$ blocks of a row using $m\le B$ starting powers. Let $s$ be the row's stride. By comparing the sum to the label just before the start of its progression, we have

$$(ap-bq)^2 < 2(ap+bq)s(1+mg)\le288bUB^2.$$

Here $s\le24$ and $ap+bq\le3bU$. It follows that for any fixed smaller prime $p$, every possible partner $q$ lies within $D=2^{517}B$ of the integer $\lfloor ap/b\rfloor$. The other assignment of the weights can only increase the trace, since $(aq+bp)-(ap+bq)=(a-b)(q-p)\ge0$. Each slope therefore allows at most $2D+1$ partners for each first prime. Taking the union already permits trying different slopes on different products. [Window theorem and union count](../PrimeFactors/TraceHitFrequency.lean)

There are 23 reduced directions for $b\le8$ and at most $2^{65}$ for $b\le2^{32}$. The published prime counts bound the ratio of the number of possible first primes to the number of eligible unordered pairs by $44331/2^{1023}$. Multiplying gives a reachable fraction below $B/2^{485}$ for the smaller catalogue and $B/2^{424}$ for the larger one. The Lean proof takes the prime-population ratio as an explicit input. The neighboring-sum, interval, catalogue and final numerical bounds are kernel-checked. [Population derivation](stride-budget.md#an-explicit-bound-at-exactly-2048-bits) · [Rosser and Schoenfeld, Corollary 1](https://doi.org/10.1215/ijm/1255631807)

## Counting only primes inside the windows

At $B=2^{64}$, each window has at most $H=2^{582}+1$ consecutive integers. For denominator cutoff $K$, there are at most $K(K+3)/2$ weight pairs, obtained by summing the $b+1$ allowed numerators for each $b$. On the 23-weight catalogue, counting only primes strengthens the frequency bound from $2^{-421}$ to $2^{-428}$ using a sieve theorem and to $2^{-430}$ using RH. Applying the RH estimate also to the size of the target population strengthens it to $2^{-433}$. For $K=2^{32}$ the corresponding three prime-count bounds are $2^{-370}$, $2^{-372}$ and $2^{-375}$.

The unconditional Brun–Titchmarsh inequality bounds the number of primes in an interval of length $H$ by $2H/\log H < H/201$. Padding a shorter interval to length $H$ preserves the upper bound. [Montgomery and Vaughan, Theorem 2, equation (1.12)](https://personal.science.psu.edu/rcv4/personal/Publications/large_sieve.pdf#page=3)

For the RH calculation, put $X=2^{1023}$ and $U=2X$. Clip each partner interval to the prime band $(X,U)$, whose endpoints are composite. Schoenfeld bounds the error in $\pi(t)-\operatorname{li}(t)$ in absolute value by $\sqrt{t}\log(t)/(8\pi)$ for $t\ge2657$ under RH. Subtracting at the two endpoints gives at most $H/\log X+\sqrt U\log U/(4\pi)<H/709$ prime partners. Indeed $\log X>709+1/16$, $\log U<710$, and the sum of the endpoint errors is below $57\cdot2^{512}$. This is where the pinned zeta result enters. [Schoenfeld, Corollary 1, equation (6.18)](https://doi.org/10.1090/S0025-5718-1976-0457374-X) · [Pinned zero-location statement](https://github.com/leanprover-community/riemann-hypothesis/blob/40e56bab/src/Statements.lean)

The same estimate gives fewer than $X/708$ primes in $(X,2X)$ and more than $1+0.584X/710$ primes in $(\sqrt2 X,2X)$. Every distinct pair in the latter interval gives a full-size product. The ratio of all possible smaller primes to eligible unordered pairs is therefore below $4200/X$, since $2\cdot710^2/(708\cdot0.584^2)<4200$. The lower count follows by integrating $1/\log t$ over the interval of length $(2-\sqrt2)X>0.585X$ and subtracting the two endpoint errors. These applications of published analytic theorems are paper deductions. The Lean calculation takes the per-window prime count and the population ratio as inputs.
