# Three quadratic forms that always contain a factor

For **every** product $N$ of two distinct 1,024-bit primes, a proper factor occurs among the gcds of $N$ with
$$u^2+v^2,\qquad u^2+2v^2,\qquad u^2-2v^2,\qquad 1\le u,v\le 2^{512}.$$
The list is public. It needs neither a square root modulo $N$ nor any information about the primes. A polynomial product lets us test a whole row at once. The resulting search has a fourth-root running-time bound in $N$, still about $2^{512}$ entries before logarithmic costs at the target width. The earlier search for two points on the same ellipse had a cube-root bound. Existing general deterministic factorization algorithms have a stronger fifth-root bound. [Lean guarantee](../PrimeFactors/NormOrbit.lean) · [Earlier ellipse search](searching-norm-directions.md) · [Harvey and Hittmeir](https://arxiv.org/abs/2105.11105)

On $77$, for instance, $3^2+2\cdot1^2=11$ gives a factor. The whole modulus has no square root of $-2$ because $-2$ is a nonsquare modulo 7. This example works because the equation needs to vanish at just one prime. Testing the four numbers $u^2+2$ with $1\le u\le4$ together gives $\gcd(3\cdot6\cdot11\cdot18,77)=11$. [Checked example](../PrimeFactors/NormOrbit.lean)

## Why the grid is complete

Take either hidden prime $r$. At least one of $-1,2,-2$ is a square modulo $r$. If $-1$ and $2$ are both nonsquares, their product $-2$ is a square, by multiplication of Legendre symbols. Write the square as $-d$, where $d$ is one of $1,2,-2$. The proof can use any root $z$ of $-d$ at $r$.

Put $R=2^{512}$. The inequalities $1\le R<r<(R+1)^2$ hold. On the circle of residues modulo $r$, the $R+1$ points $0,z,2z,\ldots,Rz$ have an adjacent pair at distance at most $r/(R+1)$, including the wraparound gap. Their indices differ by some $1\le v\le R$. Thus $zv\equiv\pm u\pmod r$ with $1\le u\le R$. The residue is nonzero since $z$ is a unit and $v<r$. Squaring gives $r\mid u^2+dv^2$.

The first two forms are positive. The third cannot vanish since $u^2=2v^2$ would give different parities for the exponent of 2 on its two sides. Each absolute value is at most $3R^2<N$. The gcd therefore contains $r$ and is strictly below $N$. This argument requires no assumption on the distribution of primes. The Lean theorem `guaranteed_box` takes the displayed inequalities for arbitrary $N,r,R$ and proves the existence of the positive coordinates and proper gcd. [Formal proof](../PrimeFactors/NormOrbit.lean)

Construct $G(X)=\prod_{u=1}^R(X+u^2)$ over $\mathbb Z/N\mathbb Z$, then evaluate it at all $d v^2$. A polynomial product tree constructs $G$. A remainder tree computes the evaluations using division by monic polynomials, which works over this composite residue ring. A nonunit row value reveals a factor unless its gcd is $N$. In that case scan the individual terms of this one row. Some term is a nonunit and every individual term has nonzero absolute value below $N$. The total work and storage are $\widetilde O(R)$, where the notation suppresses logarithmic factors in arithmetic on the input and in polynomial multiplication. This uses the established idea of batching divisibility tests by polynomial evaluation. [Bostan, Gaudry and Schost](https://cs.uwaterloo.ca/~eschost/publications/pollard.pdf) · [Remainder trees over commutative rings](https://flintlib.org/doc/algorithms/polynomials/subproduct-tree.html)

## Why three, and when one will do

Three is the minimum size of a fixed universal list of nonzero rational nonsquares. For a general list $D_i$, consider their classes modulo rational squares. The prescribed character values $\chi(D_i)=-1$ extend to a character on their span exactly when every subset whose product is a square has even cardinality. To see this, assign the sign $(-1)^k$ to any product of $k$ generators. This assignment is well defined precisely under the even-cardinality condition. Every character on the span occurs at primes: extend it to independent square classes of $-1,2$, and $(-1)^{(\ell-1)/2}\ell$ for odd primes $\ell$ dividing a numerator or denominator. Quadratic reciprocity and CRT realize the independent bits in unit residue classes, which contain primes by Dirichlet's theorem. Handle 2 and the primes dividing any numerator or denominator directly. This square-product criterion is the unramified-prime part of the classical problem of products of quadratics with local roots. [Mishra, Theorem 1](https://doi.org/10.1080/00029890.2022.2005408)

Thus an odd-cardinality subset with square product guarantees a local square at every unramified prime. Without such a relation, the all-negative pattern occupies a $2^{-h}$ fraction of unit residue classes, where $h$ is the rank of the list modulo squares. Both factors then miss in a limiting $4^{-h}$ fraction of the balanced prime pairs. The prime number theorem in fixed progressions gives this statement for full-width products by the rectangular partition argument in the earlier note. Two nonsquare discriminants cannot have an odd square-product relation. Here $(-1)\cdot2\cdot(-2)=4$. Omitting any of the three leaves both forms inert at one of the four odd residues modulo 8, with limiting failure $1/16$ for prime pairs. [Lean odd square-product criterion](../PrimeFactors/NormOrbit.lean) · [Sign distribution and full-width conditioning](searching-norm-directions.md#the-limiting-frequency-and-an-all-input-guarantee)

The bit-width promise lets us choose just one discriminant on many inputs. If $N\not\equiv1\pmod8$, take any of the three $D$ with Jacobi symbol $(D/N)=-1$. It is square at exactly one hidden prime. When the grid covers **both** possible prime widths, this one form $u^2-Dv^2$ suffices. The inert prime cannot divide any tested term because $v$ is invertible there. So every nonunit row gcd is already a proper factor. For $N\equiv1\pmod8$ the three Jacobi symbols are all positive. Trying the forms in any fixed order, the limiting expected number of whole forms required is at most $7/4$ in this residue class, or $19/16$ averaged over all balanced inputs after the single-form selection. In the residue class $N\equiv1\pmod8$, the first form works for two possible prime residues and each remaining form adds one.

Alternatively a fresh uniform unit $D\bmod N$ has Jacobi symbol $-1$ with probability $1/2$ on every distinct odd semiprime. Discard positive signs and test any nonunit by gcd. The separating coefficient may be arbitrarily large: inertness still excludes a leaf or row gcd equal to $N$. This selection also relies on covering both hidden primes. The smaller side $R=\lfloor N^{1/4}\rfloor$ is guaranteed to cover the smaller prime, so all three fixed forms may be needed at that width. [Inert-prime lemma](../PrimeFactors/NormOrbit.lean) · [Exact success probability for small boxes](short-norm-boxes.md)

## How much can small boxes cover?

**Side $2^{510}$ misses every target. Side $2^{512}$ suffices for every target.** For the three fixed forms, every tested value is nonzero and has absolute value at most $3H^2$ in a box of side $H$. As long as $3H^2<2^{1023}$, every leaf is smaller in magnitude than either prime and every row product has gcd 1 with $N$. This includes $H=2^{510}$. The universal guarantee above therefore comes within a factor of four in the side length of this explicit exclusion. [Lean proof for all three forms](../PrimeFactors/NormOrbit.lean)

The same deterministic test covers the catalog of 8,647 positive coefficients consisting of 1, the 131 primes through 739, and all products of two different such primes. Its largest coefficient is $733\cdot739=541687$. For $1\le u,v\le H=2^{501}$, every value satisfies
$$0<u^2+d v^2\le (1+541687)H^2<2^{1023}.$$
Consequently the chance of finding a factor is **exactly zero for every target**, including at the smaller side $2^{450}$. A supplied root or an adaptive choice within the catalog preserves this size bound. A uniformly sampled Jacobi-negative coefficient can be as large as $N-1$, so its small-box probability calls for a separate calculation. [Checked catalog cutoff](../PrimeFactors/NormOrbit.lean) · [Random-coefficient probability](short-norm-boxes.md)

For other fixed catalogs, a counting bound applies in the intermediate range. Put $X=2^{1023}$ and count only public triples with $X\le|u^2+dv^2|<X^2$. Each such value can have at most one prime divisor in $[X,2X)$. This prime can be paired with fewer than $1.511X/\log X$ possible partners, in either order. There are more than $(0.22X/\log X)^2$ eligible ordered pairs with full-width product. Thus $A$ triples in this range cover a fraction at most $[2(1.511)(710)/0.22^2]A/X$. [Prime-count denominator](stride-budget.md#an-explicit-bound-at-exactly-2048-bits) · [Lean divisibility lemma](../PrimeFactors/NormOrbit.lean)

If one requires positive definite forms only, an intermediate option uses $d=1$ and the individual primes through 739. For a hidden prime $r$, this list has a local square unless $(-1/r)=-1$ and $(\ell/r)=+1$ for every small prime $\ell$. Both hidden primes must have that same pattern for the construction to miss. With $M=4\prod\ell$ the pattern occupies $\varphi(M)/2^{132}$ unit residue classes, by reciprocity and CRT. So at most $T=[\varphi(M)/2^{132}]\lceil X/M\rceil$ integers in the interval can be exceptional primes. Dividing $T^2$ by the prime-pair denominator gives a finite miss bound below $2^{-247}$ (the rational upper bound is less than $2.733\cdot10^{-75}$). For a fixed list of $m$ primes the limiting proportion in that pattern at both hidden primes is $4^{-(m+1)}$ by the prime number theorem in progressions. If the list extends through $(\log(2X))^2$, the least quadratic nonresidue bound from Dirichlet GRH supplies a local square for every target prime. The three signed forms already give the unconditional guarantee. [Sign counts](searching-norm-directions.md) · [Lamzouri, Li and Soundararajan, Corollary 1.1](https://arxiv.org/html/1309.3595v3#S1.SS1)

## What can one known representation generate?

Suppose a unit root $z$ of $-d$ modulo $N$ is already available, for example from $t^2+da^2=kN$ and $z=t/a$. Multiplying this representation by any $c+e\sqrt{-d}$ gives coordinates $tc-dae,ac+te$. Their ratio is again $z$ wherever defined. Indeed $a(tc-dae)-t(ac+te)=-e(t^2+da^2)$. If $a$ is a unit and the new denominator is a nonunit, $c^2+de^2$ was already a nonunit. Repeated multiplication by elements with invertible norms and simultaneous conjugation therefore stay within $\{z,-z\}$. [Lean multiplication identity](../PrimeFactors/NormOrbit.lean)

There is a complete classification for polynomial transformations. Reduce a polynomial modulo $Y^2+d$ to $c+bY$. Its square equals $-d$ at both $z$ and $-z$ exactly when
$$cb=0,\qquad c^2-db^2=-d\pmod N,$$
since $2,z$ are units. This gives tests using the coefficients alone.

- If $\gcd(c,N)$ is proper, it already splits the input.
- If $c$ is a unit, then $b=0$, and $c$ itself is an explicitly available square root.
- Otherwise $c=0$. Since $d$ is a unit, $b^2=1$. Then $b=\pm1$ or $\gcd(b-1,N)$ is proper.

At each split odd prime the four possibilities are the two constant roots and the two sign operations. CRT gives exactly 16 maps on the formal root for a product of two split primes. Two are global signs, four are constant roots, and the other ten give a proper gcd directly from their coefficients. Over the rational numbers with $d>0$ the same coefficient equations force $c=0$ and $b=\pm1$. The polynomial degree does not affect the classification.

For a rational formula, multiply numerator and denominator by the conjugate of the denominator $a+bY$. If its scalar norm $a^2+db^2$ is a unit, the same test applies. If the norm has a proper gcd, this already gives a factor. If it vanishes, first test $\gcd(b,N)$. When $b$ is a unit, write the denominator as $b(Y+w)$ for $w=a/b$, itself a root of $-d$. Any numerator satisfying the squared identity must vanish at $-w$ because $N$ is squarefree. Its affine remainder is therefore also a scalar multiple of $Y+w$, so the quotient is constant wherever defined. If $b=0$, the denominator is zero at every root. As a linear special case, the determinant of an invertible norm similitude divided by its multiplier is already a square root of 1. Either global sign preserves the pair. [Lean polynomial, rational and determinant proofs](../PrimeFactors/NormOrbit.lean)

## A public class of order two and its possible square roots

For $N=pq\equiv1\pmod4$ consider primitive positive forms of discriminant $-4N$. Their class group has four elements whose square is the identity. The public reduced form $T=[2,2,(N+1)/2]$ represents a nonidentity one. A class $A$ with $A^2=T$ has order four. The principal genus theorem decides its existence. The odd value $(N+1)/2$ represented by $T$ is congruent to $1/2$ at $p,q$, so its two independent genus characters are $(2/p)$ and $(2/q)$. Both must be positive. Equivalently the primes must both be 1 modulo 8 or both be 7 modulo 8. Conditional on $N\equiv1\pmod8$, this holds for half the balanced prime pairs in the limit, since the two remaining possible matching residues are 3 and 5. [Genus characters and the class group in this repository](quadratic-class-number.md) · [Bosma and Stevenhagen](https://numdam.org/item/JTNB_1996__8_2_283_0/)

There is an explicit bridge to a norm equation. A solution of $x^2+N=2y^2$ gives the lift $[y,2x,2y]$ when $N\equiv1\pmod8$. Both coordinates are odd, and squarefreeness of $N$ gives $\gcd(y,2x)=1$. Composition squares the form to $[y^2,2x,2]$ since $4y^2\mid(2x)^2+4N$. Exchanging coordinates with one sign change gives $[2,-2x,y^2]$. Translating its first variable by $(x+1)/2$ times the second gives $T$. Whenever both primes split in $\mathbb Z[\sqrt2]$, generators of prime ideals at $p,q$ have norms $\pm p,\pm q$ since this ring is norm-Euclidean. Their product, with a sign adjustment by the unit $1+\sqrt2$ of norm $-1$, gives $x^2-2y^2=-N$. This proves existence. Finding such generators from $N$ requires further work.

One lift and its inverse generate only the known involution $T$ and the identity among elements of order dividing two. For a second lift $B$, the quotient $A/B$ is an involution. It differs from both $1,T$ exactly when $B$ differs from $A,A^{-1}$. The resulting ambiguous class reveals a factor of $N$. For example $697=17\cdot41$ gives $(x,y)=(5,19),(19,23)$. The difference of cross products gives $\gcd(19^2-5\cdot23,697)=41$. In the class group the quotient reduces to $[29,24,29]$, yielding $29-12=17$ and $29+12=41$. The classical random polynomial-time computation of the 2-primary class group by successive square roots assumes the factorization of the discriminant as input. The order-four observation identifies the arithmetic a product-only method must supply.
