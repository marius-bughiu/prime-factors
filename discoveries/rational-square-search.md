# When two smaller numbers give better odds of finding factors

Pick a random number, square it, and take the remainder after dividing by the product we want to factor. An old factoring method keeps the remainder when all its prime factors are small. We tested a different question about **the same remainder**. Can we express it as a fraction of two smaller numbers, each of which has only small prime factors?

On the first product in a fresh 79-bit sample, the fraction method found **574,883,478,241 × 911,431,354,859 = 523,966,827,459,249,076,123,019**. Both searches received only the product. With the same 2,097,152 draws, the fraction method found 914 usable rows and the direct search found 15. Only the fraction method obtained a combination that split the product. [Checked example](https://marius-bughiu.github.io/prime-factors/#rational-online-79) · [Full paired run](../research/rational-square-first.json)

For **every** 2,048-bit product of two distinct 1,024-bit primes, we derived a finite lower bound on this improvement. With a prime cutoff of 65,536, the fraction search needs over $2^{46}$ times fewer random draws in expectation to **find a factor** than the direct search needs to **reach its first usable row or immediate gcd**. Draws are independent and uniform in this comparison. The fraction search does extra arithmetic per draw. Even with that gain, its probability of obtaining one smooth row in $2^{48}$ uniform unit draws is below $2^{-803}$ at this cutoff. This compares two particular ways of acquiring relations. The better-known sieves generate them differently.

Searching every proportion extends the balanced box used here. On the first sampled product it cut the number of random draws by a further factor of seventeen and used more CPU time. A bound covering the whole range still rules out a practical chance of a smooth fraction under the tested random-draw strategy. ## How the product gives a relation

Let $N=pq$ and sample $1\le x<N$ uniformly. First check $\gcd(x,N)$. A nonunit gives a factor immediately. Otherwise put $r=x^2\bmod N$ and $H=\lfloor\sqrt{(N-1)/2}\rfloor$. The direct method centers $r$ in $[-(N-1)/2,(N-1)/2]$. The fraction method uses Euclid's algorithm to look for

$$ar\equiv b\pmod N,\qquad 1\le a\le H,\quad 0<|b|\le H.$$

Whenever it succeeds, $(ax)^2\equiv ab\pmod N$. If $|a|$ and $|b|$ are both **$B$-smooth**, meaning all their prime factors are at most $B$, their signed product gives one row for the usual congruence-of-squares calculation. The unknown primes are each larger than $H$, since $q<2p$ for ordered primes of the same bit length. Consequently nonzero entries in the box are units.

Start with the lattice basis $(N,0),(r,1)$. Each Euclidean division replaces the older vector by its remainder vector, preserving the integer span. At the first remainder $b_0\le H$ the older remainder $b_1$ exceeds $H$. Up to a common sign change in the second coordinate, the two vectors are $(b_0,T),(b_1,-S)$ with $T>0,S\ge0$. Every nonzero lattice vector has either first coordinate of magnitude at least $b_1$ or second coordinate of magnitude at least $T$. Indeed an integer combination with coefficients of the same sign adds first coordinates, and opposite signs add magnitudes of second coordinates. The case of a zero coefficient follows from the corresponding nonzero vector. Thus if any pair in the box exists, $T\le H$. Its signed numerator and positive denominator are obtained by making the new vector's second coordinate positive.

The determinant of any two pairs representing the same residue is divisible by $N$. In the box its absolute value is at most $2H^2<N$, forcing it to vanish. Reduced fractions are therefore unique. A pair produced by the recurrence has coprime coordinates on this input family. Indeed its coordinates have the form $(sN+tr,t)$ with $\gcd(s,t)=1$, so a common prime would divide both $N$ and the nonzero remainder at most $H$. Lean checks the span update, adjacent-vector bound, determinant argument, uniqueness of reduced positive fractions and the square relation. The implementation is an independent extended Euclidean algorithm. [Lean proofs](../PrimeFactors/RationalSquare.lean) · [Wang reconstruction and its variants, Monagan](https://ftp.cecm.sfu.ca/personal/monaganm/papers/MQIRR.pdf) · [Bounded reconstruction algorithm, FLINT](https://flintlib.org/doc/algorithms/number-theory/rational-reconstruction.html)

## Counting successful draws without the unknown primes

Take positive coprime smooth integers $b,a\le 2^{1023}$. They fit in the box of every eligible $N$ because $2^{2047}<N$. Colour each by the two Legendre symbols of that integer at the hidden primes. There are four colours. Equal colours mean $b/a$ is a square modulo both primes. It then has exactly four roots modulo $N$. Each ordered coprime pair gives a different residue by the determinant bound. We never need to compute the colours or list those roots. We bound the number of equal-colour pairs for **every** colouring, then let a random draw find them.

**Counting disjoint prime selections.** Group primes by a weight $w$ with $p\le2^w$. An integer selects $k_w$ distinct primes in each group of size $m_w$, with $\sum w k_w\le1023$ and $12 k_w\le m_w$. For a given choice of all the counts, there are
$$\prod_w {m_w\choose k_w}{m_w-k_w\choose k_w}$$
ordered pairs with disjoint supports. They are coprime. Choose twelve mutually disjoint selections with these same counts. Of the 132 ordered pairs of different positions, at least 24 have equal colours. For colour class sizes $c_i$, their number is $\sum_i c_i(c_i-1)$, minimized by four groups of three. Each ordered pair at fixed positions has the same number of completions, namely $\prod_w\prod_{j=2}^{11}{m_w-j k_w\choose k_w}$. Thus at least $2/11$ of the disjoint pairs match colours. The all-zero choice contributes the single fraction $1/1$. Summing the product across count choices counts distinct ordered pairs. Lean verifies the four-colour inequality, incidence count and bin completions. [Lean counting lemmas](../PrimeFactors/RationalSquareCount.lean)

**Discarding overlaps.** A second integer count gives a stronger lower bound for the two larger cutoffs. Use positive squarefree products of the admitted primes, with upper-rounded quarter-bit weights and total weight at most $4\cdot1023$. Omit weights up to 16 quarter bits to reduce shared factors. Write $S$ for the number of products and $S_w$ for the number containing one specified prime of weight $w$. At least $S^2/4$ ordered pairs have matching colours by Cauchy–Schwarz. At most $\sum_w m_w S_w^2$ pairs share a prime. Consequently at least $S^2-4\sum_w m_w S_w^2$ **unit roots** survive after converting fractions to their four roots. The code sums coefficients of $\prod_w(1+t^w)^{m_w}$ through the weight budget and divides out a single $(1+t^w)$ when counting the selections containing a prime. Lean verifies the four-colour inequality with the diagonal included and the overlap bound by squared support counts. The generating functions and numerical lower bounds use integer arithmetic. [Finite pair and support bounds](../PrimeFactors/RationalSquareCount.lean) · [Reproduce both counts](../experiments/rational_square_count.py)

## From one row to a factor

Retaining a fraction depends only on $r$. Conditional on this residue, a uniform unit draw still has four equally likely roots, and multiplying by its unit denominator bijects these onto the four roots of $ab$. Signs and prime exponent parities give at most $F=\pi(B)+1$ coordinates per row. After $F+s$ accepted rows there are at least $s$ independent ways to multiply rows to get a positive integer square. Conditional on their observed residues the probability all fail to split the input is at most $2^{-s}$. The proof uses the two hidden CRT signs and holds even if some residues repeat. By summing this tail the expected number of accepted rows needed to find a factor is at most $F+2$. The online option tests each independent dependency as soon as it is formed. [Exact sign law and assumptions](random-relation-signs.md) · [How correlations change it](masked-relations.md)

Let $R$ be the lower bound on fraction roots in the table. Ignore any factor from a nonunit draw when deriving an upper bound for the fraction method. Independent uniform proposals wait at most $(N-1)/R$ draws on average per accepted row. In this model the geometric waiting times are independent of the sequence of accepted roots. Hence at most $(\pi(B)+3)(N-1)/R$ proposals are needed in expectation until a split. The direct search must first see a centered smooth root or one of the $p+q-2<2^{1025}$ nonunit proposals. If their total is at most $C$, that wait alone costs at least $(N-1)/C$ in expectation. Comparing these bounds gives the following conservative improvements.

| Cutoff $B$ | Fraction search's expected proposals to split versus direct search's expected proposals to its first event |
| ---: | ---: |
| $2^{16}$ | more than $2^{46}$ times fewer |
| $2^{24}$ | more than $2^{50}$ times fewer |
| $2^{32}$ | more than $2^{21}$ times fewer |

The comparison counts random proposals. Euclidean divisions, smoothness testing, table construction, elimination and final gcds still cost time. The formal modules cover the algebra, counting and conditional sign law. The finite bounds combine those results with the integer computations and the expectation argument above. No Riemann hypothesis is required. Dixon analyzed random-square factoring, Vallée developed a different sampler for small modular squares, and rational reconstruction into two smooth parts has precedents in prime-field logarithm computations. The finite bounds here apply to the specified two samplers on balanced composite inputs. [Dixon](https://pages.cs.wisc.edu/~cs812-1/dixon.pdf) · [Vallée](https://doi.org/10.1090/S0025-5718-1991-1068808-2) · [Rational reconstruction in prime fields, Guillevic](https://www.iacr.org/archive/asiacrypt2015/94520141/94520141.pdf)

## Charging for the arithmetic as well as the draws

At $B=2^{16}$ the margin is large enough to pay for Euclid and relation processing in an explicit arithmetic model. Charge one unit for addition, subtraction, multiplication, division with remainder, comparison, a bit operation, an indexed memory access, or drawing one fair bit. Each operand and result fits in 8,192 bits. Write $D$ for the direct search's expected number of proposals to its first smooth row or nonunit. Each direct proposal costs at least one unit. The following fraction algorithm uses **less than $D/2^{24}$ units in expectation**, for every input in the stated family.

1. Generate the 6,542 primes through 65,536 once. Even trying every possible divisor through 256 for each candidate costs less than $2^{28}$ units.
2. Collect blocks of $m=6544$ smooth rows. Given their square residues, any nonempty dependency chosen by their parities splits with probability one half. Each block contains one because there are only $m-1$ parity coordinates. Discard a failed block and use fresh draws. The expected number of blocks is at most two. Ignoring early nonunit splits, the expected number of raw proposals is at most $2m(N-1)/R < D/2^{45}$.
3. A proposal costs less than $2^{20}$ units **in expectation conditional on reaching it**. Drawing an integer uniformly below $N-1$ and adding one needs fewer than two 2,048-bit attempts on average. Building each attempt one bit at a time costs under $2^{14}$ units. A gcd and the extended Euclidean walk each make at most 4,100 divisions, since every two remainders at least halve the previous positive remainder. Fewer than 16 operations per iteration cover quotient, remainder, coefficient update, counter and loop control. The Euclidean coefficients alternate in sign and have magnitude at most $N$, by the adjacent determinant identity. For smoothness, trial divide $|ab|$ by the base and store the exponents. There are at most 6,542 unsuccessful divisions and 2,048 successful ones, each costing fewer than 16 units with counters and array writes. Row initialization and residue arithmetic fit in a further $2^{15}$ units. This arithmetic algorithm uses fresh bits and trial division. The paired timing experiment uses a reproducible stream and a precomputed prime product.
4. Process each block by Boolean elimination, tracking parity and row selection in separate arrays. There are at most $m^2$ column visits. Testing for a pivot and updating the two rows when needed take at most $16m$ units per visit. This is less than $2^{43}$ units. For a dependency, sum the selected exponent vectors and halve the column sums in under $2^{31}$ units. Each sum is less than $2^{24}$. Form the integer square root **modulo $N$** by binary powering each prime to its halved exponent. Multiply the corresponding known left roots modulo $N$ and take their difference gcd. This uses less than $2^{25}$ further units. Products before modular reduction have at most 4,096 bits. The block total, including storage initialization, is below $2^{45}$ units.
5. From $C<2^{1034}$ and $N>2^{2047}$ we have $D>2^{1012}$. The expected proposal work is below $D/2^{25}$. Setup and block work add less than $2^{47}$, giving the stated total. Lean checks this last inequality from the three numerical premises. The operation counts above are for this algorithm and cost model. A unit here has no fixed duration on a PC. ## What happened in the tests

We fixed the sample and budgets before drawing the 16 products. The seed was `rational-square-panel-v1`. Each 79-bit product has two independently drawn distinct 40-bit primes, conditioned on the product width. The reproducible SHAKE stream supplies the same roots to both methods on each input. The probability theorems assume ideal independent uniform draws. Each of the 16 prime pairs and its product passed Lean certificate replay. At $B=4096$ and 8,192 draws per product, 79,866 of 131,072 proposals had an eligible fraction. The fraction method found 61 smooth rows, the direct method found zero, and both searches exhausted their budgets without a split. [All 16 inputs and work counts](../research/rational-square-panel.json)

We then fixed a larger budget for the **first** sampled input. At 2,097,152 proposals the direct search found 15 rows and no dependency. Fractions yielded 914 rows, 356 independent dependencies and the two prime factors. Testing dependencies as they arrived found the split after 1,211,456 proposals and 540 smooth rows. The bounded tests on a 2,048-bit control used 1,024 proposals per method at $B=65536$. There were 617 in-box fractions and zero smooth rows for either method. These fixed experiments report every budget and unresolved cofactor. [Long comparison and CPU times](../research/rational-square-first.json) · [CI examples](../benchmarks/rational-squares.json)
