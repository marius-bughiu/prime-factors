# One batch gcd across scattered factor intervals

Let $N=pq$, with unknown primes $p<q\le 2p$. Suppose a public calculation on $N$ returns $K$ intervals, or more generally $K$ finite arithmetic progressions, in $[1,\lfloor\sqrt N\rfloor]$. A wheel of modulus $W\ge1$ retains the candidates coprime to $W$. Write $S$ for the number retained, counting repeated occurrences. Given these progressions, **one can find $p$ if it occurs anywhere in them, or certify its absence, in deterministic time**
$$
   \widetilde O\big((W+K+\sqrt S+1)\log(2N)\big)\quad\text{bit operations}.
$$
The bound applies when $W$ is coprime to $N$ and each stride is coprime to $NW$. Plain intervals have stride one. Taking $W=1$ removes the wheel condition. Assume the input integers have $O(\log N)$ bits, including $W$. The tilde suppresses polylogarithms in $N+K+2$. Add the cost of generating the progressions from $N$ to get the end-to-end bound. The public balanced band itself, $[\lceil\sqrt{N/2}\rceil,\lfloor\sqrt N\rfloor]$, supplies one interval on every input in the stated class.

## A reusable polynomial for every stride

Write a progression as $a+bj$, $0\le j<m$. Compute $u\in[0,W)$ satisfying $bu\equiv a\pmod W$, using $u=0$ for $W=1$. Put $c=ab^{-1}-u\pmod N$. At the index $y=u+j$,
$$
   \gcd(a+bj,W)=\gcd(y,W),\qquad a+bj\equiv b(c+y)\pmod N.
$$
The first equality uses the invertibility of $b$ modulo $W$, and the second is an identity modulo $N$. The factor $b$ is a unit there. Thus every progression can use the same wheel offsets on its translated index interval $[u,u+m)$, even when its spacing and its starting term differ from all others. Computing the gcds and inverses costs $\widetilde O(K\log(2N))$ bits of work. Any proper gcd with $N$ found in setup already splits it. [Lean translation and unit-scaling proofs](../PrimeFactors/BatchIntervals.lean)

Build the sorted list $R=\{0\le r<W:\gcd(r,W)=1\}$ by $W$ gcds. It includes zero when $W=1$. Write $f=|R|$. The number of wheel survivors below $x\ge0$ is $f\lfloor x/W\rfloor+\#\{r\in R:r<x\bmod W\}$. Two binary searches per progression give its exact contribution to $S$. Return gcd one immediately when $S=0$. Otherwise choose
$$
 t=\lceil\sqrt S/f\rceil,\qquad H=tW,\qquad d=tf.
$$
The sorted offset list $A$ within one length-$H$ block consists of all $jW+r$ with $0\le j<t$ and $r\in R$. Its $d$ entries obey $\sqrt S\le d<\sqrt S+f$. The polynomial $F(X)=\prod_{e\in A}(X+e)\bmod N$, evaluated at $c+kH$, tests the whole $k$-th aligned block of any progression. There are at most $\lfloor S/d\rfloor$ wholly included blocks over all progressions, counting occurrences. Listing full block indices by endpoint division costs one step per listed block.

A partial block at an endpoint requires only a contiguous range of ranks in $A$. Build a binary tree on $2^h$ offset ranks, with $h=\lceil\log_2 d\rceil$, and assign the polynomial one to unused leaves. An internal node holds the product of its children's polynomials. Descend at each of the at most two partial blocks of a progression, selecting the maximal nodes wholly inside the desired rank range. A range consists of at most $2h+1$ such nodes. At any depth, only the two boundaries can force nodes to split. Traversing the tree costs $O(h+1)$ per endpoint. The Lean proof counts each requested rank exactly once, proves every selected node has dyadic width and alignment, and bounds the output length. [Range decomposition](../PrimeFactors/BatchIntervals.lean)

In total at most
$$
 E\le\lfloor S/d\rfloor+2K(2h+1)
$$
evaluations are needed. **Group the points by node polynomial.** Evaluating a degree-$e$ polynomial individually at $m$ points could cost $em$ operations. A product tree of the monic linear factors $X-z_i$ and a downward tree of remainders cost $\widetilde O(e+m)$ modular coefficient operations with fast polynomial arithmetic. Reduction by a monic polynomial uses the inverse of a reversed polynomial with constant term one, so it requires no inversions of point differences. This applies to the composite modulus and to repeated evaluation points alike. At each depth the node polynomials have total degree $d$, independent of $K$. Building the shared tree and evaluating its queried nodes therefore costs $\widetilde O(d+E)$. For instance, coefficients can be packed in integer blocks wide enough for a sum of $O(d+E)$ coefficient products, multiplied as integers and reduced modulo $N$. This gives the claimed soft bit bound, including the final product and gcd. The stored coefficient and query data also fit in $\widetilde O((W+K+\sqrt S+1)\log(2N))$ bits. [Classical product and remainder tree over a commutative ring](https://flintlib.org/doc/algorithms/polynomials/subproduct-tree.html)

Every tested integer is positive and at most $\sqrt N<q\le2p$. Consequently it has a common prime divisor with $N$ exactly when it equals $p$. Products can have repeated candidates, and the discarded unit powers do not change their gcd with $N$. The single aggregate gcd is therefore $p$ on a hit and one on a miss. The same evaluations can be aggregated separately by progression if its individual outcome is needed. [Lean batch gcd theorem](../PrimeFactors/BatchIntervals.lean)

## What this saves

For $K$ ranges of comparable length with large $S/K$, running a separate square-root-sized computation per range costs about $\sqrt{KS}$ before logarithmic factors. Sharing the polynomials replaces that quantity with $K+\sqrt S$. For example, when $K\le\sqrt S$, the shared bound has order $\sqrt S$ up to logarithms and wheel construction. A simple limitation holds in the model that explicitly lists $d$ offsets and can cover at most $d$ candidate occurrences per evaluation. Covering $S$ occurrences needs $S\le dE$, whence $d+E\ge2\sqrt S$. This is a bound on those two schedule sizes. Repeated identical candidates can instead be deduplicated, and algorithms exploiting other algebraic structure may use different schedules.

On the full balanced band with fixed $W$, $S=\Theta(\sqrt N)$, so the method retains the classical Pollard–Strassen exponent $1/4$. Small-prime wheels in deterministic factoring already appear in Costa and Harvey, who proved a square-root-of-log-log improvement on the earlier full-range bound. Bostan, Gaudry and Schost also study shared work across several selected indices of a polynomial recurrence. Here the bound is expressed in the *total retained length* of explicit, possibly scattered progressions, including the cost of exact boundary exclusions. Harvey's unconditional one-fifth algorithm and the project's discussion of subexponential tensor factoring give stronger general asymptotics. [Bostan–Gaudry–Schost, §§5 and 7](https://cs.uwaterloo.ca/~eschost/publications/pollard.pdf) · [Costa–Harvey](https://arxiv.org/abs/1201.2116) · [Harvey](https://arxiv.org/abs/2010.05450) · [Subexponential result and its scope](tagged-contraction-cost.md)

## A faster classical bound on these same inputs

**The smaller prime is also a small root in the progression index.** Handle even $N$ by division by two. For odd $N$, partition each index interval into consecutive blocks of at most $B=\lfloor(2N)^{1/4}\rfloor$ indices. In a block beginning at $k$, the remaining index $j$ satisfies $0\le j<B$. Let $C$ be the least nonnegative residue of $ab^{-1}+k$ modulo $N$. If $p=a+b(k+j)$ then $p\mid j+C$. Write $j+C=pv$. Since $q$ is odd, one of $v,v+q$ is even. Thus at least one of the **two public monic polynomials** $T+C$ and $T+C+N$ has $j$ as a root modulo $2p$. This hidden divisor of $2N$ satisfies $(2p)^2\ge 2N$ by $q\le2p$. Lean verifies this parity step and the divisor-size inequality. [Parity lift proof](../PrimeFactors/BatchIntervals.lean)

May's Theorem 7 gives all roots up to $c_M M^{\beta^2/\delta}$ for a monic polynomial of degree $\delta$ modulo an unknown divisor at least $M^\beta$. Use $M=2N$, $\delta=1$, $\beta=1/2$, and $c_M=1$. Its proof obtains the endpoint by setting $\epsilon=1/\log M$ and translating the polynomial over shorter subintervals. The lattice dimension can grow as $O(\log M)$ and the bit cost remains polynomial in $\log M$ for each call. For every returned root, check its block bounds and whether $a+b(k+j)$ is a proper divisor of $N$. The number and bit lengths of returned roots are polynomially bounded per call. This yields an exact hit or miss for every progression, charging both polynomials for every block. [May, Theorem 7 and proof, pp. 37–38](https://digital.ub.uni-paderborn.de/hsmig/download/pdf/3235#page=38)

Let $L$ be the total **unfiltered** progression length. This lattice method needs $O(K+L/B)$ block pairs. To compare it fairly with wheel-aware batching, let $g(W)$ be the least length of a consecutive integer interval guaranteed to contain a number coprime to $W$. After the index translation above, every progression of length $m$ contains at least $\lfloor m/g(W)\rfloor$ wheel survivors. Thus $L\le g(W)(S+K)$, including runs having no survivors. Iwaniec's Jacobsthal bound gives $g(W)=O(1+r^2\log^2(2+r))$ for $r$ distinct prime divisors of $W$. As $r\le\log_2 W$ for $W>1$ and $g(1)=1$, this factor is polynomial in $\log(2N)$. The algorithm scans unfiltered blocks and need not construct the wheel. Since $\gcd(W,N)=1$, a true occurrence of $p$ always survives it. [Iwaniec, corollary on arbitrary sets of primes](https://doi.org/10.1515/dema-1978-0121)

As an alternative, factor $N$ outright by Harvey's deterministic algorithm, then check progression membership by $K$ exact divisions with remainder. Choose the cheaper bound from $L,K,N$, or interleave the two algorithms. Up to logarithmic factors in $N+K+2$, the bit cost is at most
$$
 O\!\left(K+1+\min\{N^{1/5},S/N^{1/4}\}\right)
 \ \le\ O(K+1+S^{4/9}).
$$
For $S>0$, raise the first entry of the minimum to power $5/9$ and the second to $4/9$ and multiply. The powers of $N$ cancel. For $S=0$ the unfiltered scan already costs $O((K+1)\operatorname{polylog}N)$. These are worst-case bounds from existing algorithms; a complete one-fifth search is only one of the two alternatives. The elementary shared-polynomial search therefore gives a square-root batching guarantee, but **classical small-root methods combined with general factoring already give a smaller exponent for this representation of the candidates**. All bounds still require the cost of obtaining the input progressions. [Harvey, Theorem 1.1](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2010.05450#page=1)

For the project's modular-hyperbola interval filter, put $X=\sqrt N$ and give each smaller-factor interval length at most $B$. The divisor estimate leaves at most $B X^{o(1)}$ nonempty intervals after one auxiliary modulus. If their locations have been computed, the present batch costs $B X^{o(1)}\operatorname{polylog} N$, using $W=1$ and $S\le KB$. The current row-by-row location method already costs order $X$ row tests. Also, for $B\le N^{1/4-\epsilon}$, an individual surviving interval approximates a balanced factor closely enough for the classical polynomial-time small-root method. The batched gcd gives an elementary way to use surviving windows of different sizes without assuming an unproved speedup in their production. [Product-dependent surviving intervals](hyperbola-boxes.md) · [Coppersmith’s small-root method](https://doi.org/10.1007/s001459900030)

## Bounded checks

Take $N=15049$, $W=6$, and the triples $(a,b,m)=(89,5,7),(95,7,4),(91,5,7)$. Their eight surviving occurrences are $89,109,119,95,109,91,101,121$. Here $A=(1,5,7,11)$, $d=4$, and $H=12$. Six evaluations, using four distinct node polynomials, multiply to $2828\bmod N$; the gcd is 101 and the partner is 149. Replacing the last progression by $(91,5,2),(106,5,4)$ excludes precisely 101. The six evaluated values now multiply to $5504\bmod N$, whose gcd is one. Lean checks both modular products and gcds, and primality of both factors. [Checked computations](../PrimeFactors/BatchIntervals.lean)

For the existing 62-bit product $N=3221225472647483647$, the public smaller-factor band is the half-open interval $[1269099184,1794777277)$. Its 525,678,093 integers contain 140,180,824 wheel-30 survivors. The schedule has degree 11,840, with 11,838 whole-block queries and 18 boundary-node queries. These are schedule counts. The complete cost also includes multiplying the polynomials and performing their multipoint evaluations. A 2,048-bit balanced input still gives a degree of order $2^{512}$ at a fixed wheel size.
