# Short periods from a factor of a cyclotomic value

Let $N=pq$ for distinct odd primes. For a fixed positive integer $k$, the search computes $E=N^k-1$ from the product. Define the hidden residual group sizes

$$R_p=\frac{p-1}{\gcd(p-1,q^k-1)},\qquad R_q=\frac{q-1}{\gcd(q-1,p^k-1)}.$$

The expected cost to find a factor is $O(\sqrt{\min(R_p,R_q)}\,\operatorname{polylog}N)$ bit operations at fixed $k$. This is a structured-input refinement of the earlier common-predecessor bound. Cubic roots of unity give an unconditional lower bound on the number of inputs where it improves the deterministic one-fifth benchmark, even with all four signed gcds small. The counting and complexity arguments are on paper. The local arithmetic, exclusion from an exhausted table and separating exponents are formalized in Lean. [Lean theorems](../PrimeFactors/CyclotomicPeriods.lean) · [Earlier method](common-predecessors.md)

## Why the product supplies the exponent

Modulo $p-1$, $N$ equals $q$. Therefore $\gcd(p-1,E)=\gcd(p-1,q^k-1)$. Raising an arbitrary unit $u$ to $E$ yields a unit $v$ whose order at $p$ divides $R_p$. Swapping the primes gives the analogous bound at $q$. Also $N-1\mid E$, so the two orders divide the coprime orders of the projections of $u^{N-1}$. These facts hold for every base. For $k=3$, a large prime divisor of $p-1$ can disappear from the order if it divides $q^2+q+1$, including when it divides neither $q-1$ nor $q+1$.

For $v\ne1\pmod N$, double the geometric table width $m$ until it yields a split. Once $m^2\ge\min(R_p,R_q)$, at least one pair of table entries agrees at a prime divisor. A whole-modulus match yields a separating divisor of its positive exponent difference, since the two periods are coprime. A local match is detected by batched polynomial evaluation and gcds. The earlier proof accounts for whole-modulus and mixed-prime collisions, and gives quasi-linear work in the table width up to logarithms of $N$. A fully exhausted table certifies $m^2\gcd(p-1,q^k-1)<p-1$, and the symmetric inequality at $q$. The algorithm needs neither residual to choose its widths. [Collision detection and cost](common-predecessors.md#why-a-short-period-gives-a-factor) · [Harvey, Algorithm 4.1](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2010.05450#page=8)

Uniform random units project to independent uniform units modulo the two primes. The two cyclic power maps have image sizes $R_p,R_q$, so $\Pr(v=1)=1/(R_pR_q)$. When $R_pR_q>1$, a nonidentity occurs with probability at least $1/2$. To handle $R_p=R_q=1$ without knowing the residuals, write $E=2^th$ with $h$ odd. Whenever $v=1$, compute gcds along the squaring chain from $u^h$ to $1$. If the two orders of $u^h$ differ, exactly one component reaches $1$ first. For $R_p=R_q=1$, the odd part $h$ kills the odd parts of both unit groups. If their 2-primary group sizes differ, half the units at the larger component have its full 2-power order. If the sizes agree, exactly half the pairs contain one full-order and one smaller-order component. Thus each independent draw gives a split with probability at least $1/2$ in this case, too. Sampling integers and taking an initial gcd gives either a unit or an immediate proper divisor. The extra exponent costs $O(k\log N)$ bits. [Miller's known-multiple method](https://www.cs.cmu.edu/~glmiller/Publications/Papers/Mi76.pdf)

A single search can cover any fixed finite list of degrees by using $E=\operatorname{lcm}_{k}(N^k-1)$. Every local residual divides the corresponding residual for each listed degree, $N-1$ divides the combined exponent, and its bit length is at most $(\sum_k k)\log_2 N+1$. This removes the need to select the best degree from knowledge of the factors. Multiplying $E$ by a public smooth exponent also shrinks the residuals and preserves coprimality. This use of smooth exponents is classical. Bach and Shallit study supplied multiples of cyclotomic values at an unknown prime and the associated smoothness methods. Galbraith and McKee discuss shared factors of signed predecessors and successors. Here the test uses a large shared divisor of $p-1$ and $q^2+q+1$, with no smoothness condition on that divisor. [Bach and Shallit](https://www.ams.org/journals/mcom/1989-52-185/S0025-5718-1989-0947467-1/S0025-5718-1989-0947467-1.pdf) · [Galbraith and McKee, §5](https://www.math.auckland.ac.nz/~sgal018/pair-mod-n.pdf)

## How many balanced products benefit

Fix $1/5<\alpha<1/2$ and let $H=X^\alpha$. For all sufficiently large $X$, at least

$$\gg_\alpha \frac{X^{2-\alpha}}{(\log X)^3}$$

distinct products have $3X/4\le p\le4X/5$ and $9X/10\le q\le19X/20$, with all four values $\gcd(p\pm1,q\pm1)\le(\log X)^4$, and $R_p\le X/H$ for $k=3$. On every such product, the expected cost is $O(N^{(1-\alpha)/4}\operatorname{polylog}N)$. Taking fixed $\alpha$ close to $1/2$ brings the exponent close to $1/8$. The estimate counts a subset of balanced inputs and gives no corresponding speed guarantee on uniformly sampled balanced semiprimes.

To prove the count, take primes $H\le\ell\le2H$ with $\ell\equiv1\pmod3$. There are $\gg_\alpha H/\log X$ by the prime number theorem in the fixed progression modulo 3. Choose one primitive cube root $t_\ell\bmod\ell$ and impose $p\equiv1$, $q\equiv t_\ell\pmod\ell$. Bombieri–Vinogradov, applied at the four interval endpoints with the maximum over reduced residue classes, bounds the sum of the absolute counting errors by $O_A(X/(\log X)^A)$ for any fixed $A$. Its modulus range includes $2H$ for each fixed $\alpha<1/2$ and large $X$. Removing moduli with endpoint error exceeding a sufficiently small fixed multiple of $X/(H\log X)$ costs $O_A(H/(\log X)^{A-1})$ moduli. For each remaining $\ell$, both progressions contain $\gg X/(H\log X)$ primes in their respective intervals. Every resulting pair has $\ell\mid p-1$ and $\ell\mid q^2+q+1$. [Bombieri–Vinogradov statement, equation (1.1)](https://doi.org/10.1112/S0010437X2500747X)

To eliminate large signed gcds, put $T=(\log X)^4$. For each fixed $\ell$ and choice of signs, count integer pairs for which some $d>T$ divides both shifted values. Since $q\equiv t_\ell\not\equiv\pm1\pmod\ell$, we have $\ell\nmid d$. The Chinese remainder theorem gives at most $(X/(\ell d)+1)^2$ pairs at a given $d$. Summing for $T<d\le X/\ell$ gives $O(X^2/(\ell^2 T)+(X/\ell)\log X)$. For $d>X/\ell$, write $p\pm1=du$ and $q\pm1=dv$, so $0<u,v<\ell$ for sufficiently large $X$. The minus sign at $p$ would force $\ell\mid u$ and is impossible. For the plus sign, $du\equiv2$ and $dv\equiv t_\ell\pm1$. Thus $u$ determines $v$ modulo $\ell$ and determines $d$ modulo $\ell$. On a dyadic range $D<d\le2D$, there are $O((X/D)(1+D/\ell))=O(X/\ell)$ choices because $D\ge X/\ell>\ell$. The total over all ranges and signs is $o(X^2/(H^2\log^2 X))$ per modulus. Finally each product is counted for $O_\alpha(1)$ moduli, since their product divides $p-1<X$ and each is at least $X^\alpha$. The disjoint intervals fix the orientation of the factors.

The same lower bound survives if every pair of positive integer weights $a,b\le X^{1/32}$ must satisfy $|ap-bq|>X^{7/8}$. At fixed $\ell,a,b$ there are $O(X/\ell)$ choices of the first integer in its progression. For each, the second lies in an interval of length at most $2X^{7/8}/b$ whenever the inequality fails, yielding $O(X^{7/8}/\ell+1)$ candidates in its progression. Summing this product over at most $X^{1/16}$ weights costs $o(X^2/(\ell^2\log^2 X))$ per modulus. For each of the surviving products, the excess of the midpoint of the intended weighted factors over their geometric mean is

$$\frac{ap+bq}{2}-\sqrt{abN}=\frac{(ap-bq)^2}{2(\sqrt{ap}+\sqrt{bq})^2}\gg X^{23/32}.$$

This rules out a close intended weighted pair in that range. It makes no claim about other factorizations of $abN$.

## Reproducing the table comparison

The 73-bit fixture has product 5516692220419344918319. Using base 2 and six entries per table, the degrees 1 and 2 exhaust all six batch gcds. Degree 3 recovers 58000000523 in the second batch gcd. Its partner is 95115382253. The local residual at the first factor drops from 29000000261 for each of the first two degrees to 29 for the cubic exponent. The four signed gcds are 2, 2, 4, and 6. The first ordinary Fermat midpoint yielding the split is 2283252848 increments above the initial midpoint.

The synthetic 2048-bit fixture uses $U=7637617\cdot 2^{479}$, $c=10101U-101$, and $L=10101U^2-201U+1$, so $c^2+c+1=10101L$. Its two 1024-bit primes are $p=106L+1$ and $q=102L+c$. Because $L\mid p-1$ and $q\equiv c\pmod L$, the cubic exponent removes $L$ from the period at $p$. The residual becomes 53, compared with $(p-1)/2$ at degrees 1 and 2. All four signed gcds are at most 12. With eight entries per table the first two degrees exhaust their searches and degree 3 finds $p$. Ordinary Fermat search needs more than $2^{1011}$ increments. This particular construction also gives a short weighted Fermat route. The midpoint of $51p$ and $53q$ is 656 increments above $\lceil\sqrt{51\cdot53N}\rceil$. The abundance proof above can exclude that additional structure in its own family.

The small identity control $15=3\cdot5$ at $k=2$ exercises the halving branch with base 2 and zero table entries.

The wrapper gives the original Lean search the base $u^{(N^k-1)/(N-1)}\bmod N$. Its internal power $N-1$ therefore produces $u^{N^k-1}$. Each trial uses only the product, base, degree and table width. After a split, the replay uses the stored decimal factors to check the result, generates the prime certificates, and invokes Lean to verify them. The seven benchmark rows record table entries allocated, giant probes, batch and halving gcds, and unresolved cofactors. Discovery time includes exponent construction, modular powering and the halving branch. Interpreter startup is included in the wall time.
