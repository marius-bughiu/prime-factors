# How many full-size products do 2,016 first square tests find?

Consider the 64 integers from 512 through 575. For each unordered pair of distinct weights $a,b$, take $S=\lceil\sqrt{4abN}\rceil$ and check whether $S^2-4abN$ is a square. This fixes the entire search before seeing any factors. Classical unconditional prime-counting inequalities imply that at least $2^{1504}$ distinct 2,048-bit products yield a prime on this list. For an unordered pair of uniformly sampled distinct 1,024-bit primes conditioned on the product having 2,048 bits, the chance that this list succeeds is less than one in $2^{460}$. The large number of successes and the small chance describe the same search over a much larger population. Lean takes the two published prime-count estimates below as premises for its lower bound. ## Why count many weights together?

Write $U=2^{1024}$. Consider all primes $p$ from $3U/4$ to $7U/8$. Dusart's estimates imply that their count $P$ is at least $2^{1011}$. For each such prime and every weight $a$, place the integer $ap$ into a bin of width $H=2^{516}$, starting the first bin at $384U$. All $64P$ labeled points fit in $2^{515}$ bins. We count ordered pairs of points in the same bin, including self-pairs for this intermediate step. If the bin occupancies are $c_i$, the number of these pairs is $\sum_i c_i^2$, at least $(64P)^2/2^{515}$ by Cauchy–Schwarz. [Dusart, equation (6.6)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1002.0442#page=9)

Two points with the same weight in a bin have prime coordinates at most $H/512=2^{507}$ apart. Start at the smallest of those primes. Montgomery and Vaughan's short-interval upper bound shows that the inclusive window of length $2^{507}$ contains at most $2^{500}$ primes. Thus at most $64P\,2^{500}$ ordered pairs have the same weight. The mean occupancy of all points is at least $2^{502}$. Subtracting the same-weight pairs leaves at least $2^{1517}$ ordered pairs with distinct weights. This subtraction is what gives a guarantee for different factor ratios without assuming how primes in two nearby intervals are correlated. [Montgomery and Vaughan, Theorem 2](https://personal.science.psu.edu/rcv4/personal/Publications/large_sieve.pdf#page=3)

## From a bin to an actual factor

Take two of the remaining points $x=ap$ and $y=bq$. Their distance is less than $H$, and both are at least $384U$. They have distinct primes because $|ap-bp|\ge p>H$ for distinct integer weights. These bounds give $(x+y-1)^2<4xy\le(x+y)^2$, so the first possible integer trace for multiplier $ab$ equals $x+y$. The discriminant is $(x-y)^2$. Its square root gives $\max(x,y)=(S+\sqrt{S^2-4abN})/2$. Taking its gcd with $N=pq$ returns one prime. The weights are smaller than either prime.

A given product can account for at most $2\cdot64^2$ ordered labeled pairs, since its two primes are unique up to exchange. Dividing the count by this upper bound gives at least $2^{1504}$ distinct successful products. Every product has 2,048 bits because its two primes belong to the stated interval. Running the complete menu costs at most 2,016 starting square roots and 2,016 discriminant square roots. The count allows duplication between weights and makes no assumption that different tests are independent. [First-trace recovery](../PrimeFactors/WeightedTracePrefix.lean)

## What did the search find?

On a deliberately constructed 2,048-bit product, pair 567 of 2,016 gave a square with root 36 and recovered both primes. To generate it we used $p=521\,2^{512}k+1$ and $q=557\,2^{512}k+1$, so $557p-521q=36$. The search received only the product and used the same fixed list as on the other inputs. Lean verified both primes, their product, the first trace and the placement in the same bin. On the fresh and crossed 2,048-bit comparisons, each run spent 4,032 square roots and found zero squares. For the probability upper bound, even allowing both orderings gives at most $64^2$ choices of multiplier. The earlier catalogue theorem grants all divisor allocations of each multiplier and all traces up to $2^{32}$ above the minimum. It bounds the success fraction by the catalogue size divided by $2^{472}$. Our one-label search lies inside that grant.
