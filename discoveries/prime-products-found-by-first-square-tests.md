# What can ten first square tests find?

Ten public square tests were enough for a new **deliberately constructed** 2,048-bit example. Test six found its two 1,024-bit primes. The same ten tests found no square on either full-size comparison product. The larger catalogue below explains the counting argument. Keeping tighter constants lets us reduce the list from 2,016 tests to ten. [Inputs, search budgets and verified outcomes](../benchmarks/weighted-crowding.json)

Use weights 512, 513, 514, 515 and 516. For each of the ten unordered pairs $a<b$, try $S=\lceil\sqrt{4abN}\rceil$ and check whether $S^2-4abN$ is a square. Our successful example was constructed as $p=513\,2^{512}k+1$ and $q=515\,2^{512}k+1$, so $515p-513q=2$. The search saw only $N$ and found the square $2^2$ with weights 513 and 515. It spent 12 integer square roots. We gave ordinary Fermat search the full allowance of 20 integer roots, enough for 19 consecutive midpoint tests. It missed. On the two controls both methods spent their full allowance. [Benchmark implementation and deterministic generator](../scripts/weighted_crowding_benchmark.py)

Classical unconditional prime estimates imply that **more than $2^{1503}$ distinct 2,048-bit products** split under this ten-test rule. For each product counted by the proof, ordinary Fermat misses even its first $2^{1001}$ consecutive midpoints. A random eligible product still has a success chance below one in $2^{460}$, since all ten tests belong to the larger catalogue with that upper bound. [Ten-test count and factor recovery](../PrimeFactors/FiveWeightMenu.lean) · [Ordinary square exclusion](../PrimeFactors/CrowdingOrdinarySeparation.lean)

The sharper count keeps the prime pool and bin width from the argument below. The five weights occupy at most $135\,2^{507}$ bins. Dusart's estimates give at least $2U/12425$ primes in the pool. Montgomery and Vaughan's short-interval bound limits each single-weight group in one bin to $\lfloor2^{512}/5600\rfloor+1$ primes. The mean occupancy of all five weights exceeds this cap by more than $2^{495}$. Even allowing 50 ordered weight and prime assignments per product leaves the stated count. The Lean formalization takes the two published analytic inequalities as explicit premises. [Constants and arithmetic](../PrimeFactors/FiveWeightEstimates.lean)

## The larger catalogue

Consider the 64 integers from 512 through 575. For each unordered pair of distinct weights $a,b$, take $S=\lceil\sqrt{4abN}\rceil$ and check whether $S^2-4abN$ is a square. This fixes the entire search before seeing any factors. Classical unconditional prime-counting inequalities imply that at least $2^{1504}$ distinct 2,048-bit products yield a prime on this list. For an unordered pair of uniformly sampled distinct 1,024-bit primes conditioned on the product having 2,048 bits, the chance that this list succeeds is less than one in $2^{460}$. The large number of successes and the small chance describe the same search over a much larger population. Lean takes the two published prime-count estimates below as premises for its lower bound. ## Why count many weights together?

Write $U=2^{1024}$. Consider all primes $p$ from $3U/4$ to $7U/8$. Dusart's estimates imply that their count $P$ is at least $2^{1011}$. For each such prime and every weight $a$, place the integer $ap$ into a bin of width $H=2^{516}$, starting the first bin at $384U$. All $64P$ labeled points fit in $2^{515}$ bins. We count ordered pairs of points in the same bin, including self-pairs for this intermediate step. If the bin occupancies are $c_i$, the number of these pairs is $\sum_i c_i^2$, at least $(64P)^2/2^{515}$ by Cauchy–Schwarz. [Dusart, equation (6.6)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1002.0442#page=9)

Two points with the same weight in a bin have prime coordinates at most $H/512=2^{507}$ apart. Start at the smallest of those primes. Montgomery and Vaughan's short-interval upper bound shows that the inclusive window of length $2^{507}$ contains at most $2^{500}$ primes. Thus at most $64P\,2^{500}$ ordered pairs have the same weight. The mean occupancy of all points is at least $2^{502}$. Subtracting the same-weight pairs leaves at least $2^{1517}$ ordered pairs with distinct weights. This subtraction is what gives a guarantee for different factor ratios without assuming how primes in two nearby intervals are correlated. [Montgomery and Vaughan, Theorem 2](https://personal.science.psu.edu/rcv4/personal/Publications/large_sieve.pdf#page=3)

## From a bin to an actual factor

Take two of the remaining points $x=ap$ and $y=bq$. Their distance is less than $H$, and both are at least $384U$. They have distinct primes because $|ap-bp|\ge p>H$ for distinct integer weights. These bounds give $(x+y-1)^2<4xy\le(x+y)^2$, so the first possible integer trace for multiplier $ab$ equals $x+y$. The discriminant is $(x-y)^2$. Its square root gives $\max(x,y)=(S+\sqrt{S^2-4abN})/2$. Taking its gcd with $N=pq$ returns one prime. The weights are smaller than either prime.

A given product can account for at most $2\cdot64^2$ ordered labeled pairs, since its two primes are unique up to exchange. Dividing the count by this upper bound gives at least $2^{1504}$ distinct successful products. Every product has 2,048 bits because its two primes belong to the stated interval. Running the complete menu costs at most 2,016 starting square roots and 2,016 discriminant square roots. The count allows duplication between weights and makes no assumption that different tests are independent. [First-trace recovery](../PrimeFactors/WeightedTracePrefix.lean)

## What did the search find?

On a deliberately constructed 2,048-bit product, pair 567 of 2,016 gave a square with root 36 and recovered both primes. To generate it we used $p=521\,2^{512}k+1$ and $q=557\,2^{512}k+1$, so $557p-521q=36$. The search received only the product and used the same fixed list as on the other inputs. Lean verified both primes, their product, the first trace and the placement in the same bin. On the fresh and crossed 2,048-bit comparisons, each run spent 4,032 square roots and found zero squares. [Decimal inputs, expected outcomes and construction](../benchmarks/weighted-crowding.json) · [Search and generator](../scripts/weighted_crowding_benchmark.py)

For the probability upper bound, even allowing both orderings gives at most $64^2$ choices of multiplier. The earlier catalogue theorem grants all divisor allocations of each multiplier and all traces up to $2^{32}$ above the minimum. It bounds the success fraction by the catalogue size divided by $2^{472}$. Our one-label search lies inside that grant.
