# What Dirichlet characters reveal about the factors

Let $N=pq$ with distinct primes $p<q\le2p$. We tested whether a value of a Dirichlet L-function reveals which half of an interval contains $q$, and how few primes suffice to recognize the correct factor sum $p+q$ in its known interval.

## Comparing a factor with a threshold

In the Riemann repository, the proof that removes extra Euler factors uses the strict inequality $\operatorname{Re}(s)>0$. This suggested looking at its boundary. Write $L_N$ for the principal Dirichlet L-function modulo $N$. At $s=it$ with $t>0$ its factorization is

$$L_N(it)=\zeta(it)\prod_{r\mid N,\ r\ \mathrm{prime}}(1-r^{-it}).$$

Zeta is nonzero there. Each factor vanishes at the positive multiples of $2\pi/\log r$. Thus the **first positive boundary zero** has height $t_0=2\pi/\log q$, giving $q=\exp(2\pi/t_0)$. Lean proves the classification for every $N>1$ and its largest prime divisor $q$. Multiplicity has no effect on the zero locations. The Euler factors are classical. The local proofs use mathlib 4.19 and are unconditional. [Original strict-half-plane lemma](https://github.com/leanprover-community/riemann-hypothesis/blob/40e56bab/src/Development/AutomorphicArithmetic/CharacterBoxes/ClaimStrip.lean#L5-L31) · [Lean boundary proofs](../PrimeFactors/DivisorSignal.lean)

**A factor comparison.** Choose $x>\sqrt N$ and set $t=2\pi/\log x$. From the product alone we can define

$$B_N(x)=\operatorname{Re}\!\left(e^{it\log(N)/2}\,L_N(it)/\zeta(it)\right).$$

Lean proves that its sign equals the sign of $q-x$. Expanding the Euler factors gives $-4\sin(\pi\log p/\log x)\sin(\pi\log q/\log x)$. The first sine is positive because $p<x$. The second changes sign at $q=x$, since $q<x^2$. For example, $34387=137\cdot251$ gives positive sign at $x=250$ and negative sign at $x=252$. Bisection of the balanced integer interval between $\sqrt N$ and $\sqrt{2N}$ takes $O(\log N)$ such comparisons. [Lean sign theorem](../PrimeFactors/DivisorSignal.lean)

**Required accuracy.** At an integer query $4\le x\le\sqrt{2N}$ above $\sqrt N$, test divisibility by $x$ first. Otherwise each $r\in\{p,q\}$ is an integer distinct from $x$, with $x/2\le r\le2x$. Integrating $1/z$ between $r$ and $x$ gives $1/(2x)\le|\log(r/x)|\le\log 2$. The chord bound for sine on $[0,\pi/2]$ then gives $|\sin(\pi\log r/\log x)|\ge1/(x\log x)$. Consequently

$$|B_N(x)|\ \ge\ \frac{4}{x^2(\log x)^2}\ \ge\ \frac{2}{N(\log N)^2}.$$

Lean checks the first inequality, including the balance and divisibility conditions. A certified absolute error below $1/[N(\log N)^2]$ in the normalized real value suffices. This is $O(\log N)$ fractional bits. **Computing them affordably from $N$ is the open step.** The Euler product through the unknown primes already requires the factors. The principal character's coefficients at positive indices below $p$ are all one. [Definitions and coefficient obstruction](../PrimeFactors/DivisorSignal.lean)

**A shortcut that loses the signal.** At heights $T_k=2\pi k/\log N$, the two progressions contribute exactly $k-1$ positive zeros up to $T_k$ for every $k\ge1$. The two floor arguments add to $k$ and neither is integral, by unique prime factorization. For distinct primes the positive progressions cannot overlap for the same reason. The cumulative count on this grid is therefore independent of the split. [Lean grid identity](../PrimeFactors/DivisorSignal.lean)

## How many primes suffice for all discriminants?

In the balanced sum interval, a false sum $s$ has positive nonsquare discriminant $D=s^2-4N\le N/2$. Call a list of odd primes *universal through $2^b$* if every positive nonsquare up to that bound fails at least one modular square test. Zero counts as a square modulo a prime.

**Paper deduction using Dirichlet GRH.** For all sufficiently large $b$, draw $2b$ primes independently and uniformly, with replacement, from $(b^3/2,b^3]$. With probability at least $1-(8/9)^b$ the list is universal. The same draw works for every balanced input whose discriminants are in range. We have no numerical starting width for this asymptotic result.

Fix a nonsquare $D\le2^b$. Its squarefree part induces a nonprincipal quadratic character modulo $8D$ which agrees with $(D/\ell)$ at odd primes $\ell\nmid D$. This follows by quadratic reciprocity, including for squarefree part 2. Put $X=b^3$. Under Dirichlet GRH the unweighted character sum over primes through $X$ has absolute value $O(\sqrt X\log(8DX))$ with an absolute implied constant. Subtract the estimate at $X/2$. The interval contains $P\asymp X/\log X$ primes by the prime number theorem, and at most $b+3$ divide $8D$. The fraction rejecting $D$ is therefore $1/2+O(\log b/\sqrt b)$ uniformly in $D$. Eventually the passing fraction is at most $2/3$. Independent draws miss this $D$ with probability at most $(2/3)^{2b}$. A union bound over at most $2^b$ nonsquares proves the claim. Dependence between different discriminants is allowed. Lean verifies the finite counting bound. The analytic estimate and its asymptotic application are on paper. [Original Dirichlet statements](https://github.com/leanprover-community/riemann-hypothesis/blob/40e56bab/src/Statements.lean) · [Taheri–Saleh, Lemma 4.1](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.19610#page=5) · [Finite sampling bound](../PrimeFactors/SampledSieve.lean)

**The order of growth is necessary.** Lean proves that a universal detector needs at least $\lfloor b/2\rfloor-2$ distinct prime tests. For $Y=2^{\lfloor b/2\rfloor}$ record one bit per prime for each squarefree integer up to $Y$. Two distinct such integers with the same bits would give a nonsquare product through $2^b$ that passes every test. If either marker vanishes at a given prime, its product is zero there. At least $Y/4$ of the integers are squarefree, by bounding the multiples of squares. Hence $2^{\#\mathrm{tests}}\ge Y/4$. At 2,048 bits this means at least 1,022 tests **for universal detection**. The upper and lower bounds determine the asymptotic order $\Theta(b)$ of the number of tests. Drawing the list costs polynomial work in $b$, for example by sieving through $b^3$. Recovering the unique passing factor sum without enumerating the interval remains open. [Lean counting lower bound](../PrimeFactors/SignatureCount.lean) · [Earlier perfect-power sieves](https://butler.elsevierpure.com/en/publications/sieve-algorithms-for-perfect-power-testing/) · [Pseudosquare literature](https://arxiv.org/abs/0712.1081)

**A tighter conjecture.** For all sufficiently large $b$, do at most $b$ primes in $(b^2/2,b^2]$ detect every positive nonsquare below $2^b$? We enumerated every nonsquare at widths 4 through 20. At each step we chose the prime eliminating the most remaining values, breaking ties by the smaller prime. Widths 4, 5, 6 and 8 have nonsquares passing *every* permitted prime. At width 8, 122 survives. Width 7 and every width from 9 through 20 admit lists of at most $b$ tests. At width 20, 17 primes covered all 1,047,552 nonsquares. These finite results leave the asymptotic conjecture open. Separately, Lean kernel-checks a universal list below 256 using five primes from a wider range, namely 3, 23, 37, 47 and 89. [Reproducible experiment and command](../experiments/SquareWindows.lean) · [Five-prime proof](../PrimeFactors/SampledSieve.lean)

## Can one prime reject a whole candidate block?

At $N=43657=149\cdot293$, the consecutive parity-compatible false sums $422,426,430$ have discriminants $3456,6848,10272$. Their product is $493056^2$. At any prime dividing this product, some discriminant is zero. At any other odd prime, three quadratic nonresidues would have product with Legendre symbol $-1$, impossible for a square. This disproves the conjecture that one odd prime always excludes three such candidates. Lean proves the odd-product obstruction in general and checks that 5 and 7 together reject this block. Wright characterized simultaneous nonresidues of arbitrary finite sets. [Lean obstruction and example](../PrimeFactors/BlockCharacters.lean) · [Wright](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0022314X06001338)

Inspired by the Riemann repository's finite character averages, we also asked how two candidate sums correlate. For $\ell\equiv3\bmod4$ prime, fix $s,t$ with $s^2\ne t^2\bmod\ell$. Over uniformly chosen nonzero product residues, the single-sum pass probability is $1/2$ and the joint probability is $(\ell-3)/(4(\ell-1))$. Lean checks the counts and the corresponding second moment. For a *fixed* unit product residue, exactly $(\ell+1)/4$ distinct squared-sum classes fail. Several candidates can occupy the same square class. [Original finite averages](https://github.com/leanprover-community/riemann-hypothesis/blob/40e56bab/src/Development/AutomorphicArithmetic/DedekindLFunctions/LandauTheorems/ChCore.lean#L24-L67) · [Lean correlation and rejection count](../PrimeFactors/ShiftedResidues.lean)

One can also deliberately choose arbitrarily many auxiliary primes that pass *every* candidate in a fixed interval. Quadratic reciprocity permits each nonzero discriminant to be a square at the same primes. CRT combines them into a period larger than the interval with arbitrarily small full-period density. For example, all nine candidates from 140 through 148 at $4897=59\cdot83$ pass modulo 97, 239 and 577. A long period and low whole-period density alone therefore imply no saving in that interval. The random-sampling result above specifies a different choice of primes. [Lean construction and table-size obstruction](../PrimeFactors/LongPeriod.lean)
