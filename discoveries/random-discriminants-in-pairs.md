# Covering four discriminant signs with random pairs

Suppose the only input is a product $N=pq$ of two distinct odd primes. A discriminant coprime to $N$ can be a square or a nonsquare at each prime, giving four sign pairs. Once we have a number $a$ whose public Jacobi symbol is $-1$, **four discriminants cover all four signs with probability $1/2$**. Draw a uniformly random unit $b$ modulo $N$ and use $1,a,b,ab\bmod N$. On an arbitrary 2,048-bit product, that probability describes which kinds of discriminants we try. The subsequent group calculation still has to expose a factor. [Deterministic sign coverage and the factoring step](four-sign-choices-from-a-public-anchor.md)

The signs of $a$ are opposite. At the prime where $a$ is a square, both $1$ and $a$ have positive sign. If $b$ is also a square there, so is $ab$, so the menu misses both negative choices there. If $b$ is a nonsquare there, $b$ and $ab$ supply both missing choices because multiplication by $a$ reverses their signs at the other prime. Exactly half the units are squares at the first prime. The Chinese remainder theorem lifts those units evenly to units of $N$. The computation needs neither prime. [Checked counts](../PrimeFactors/RandomSignMenu.lean) · [Quadratic characters of finite fields](https://github.com/leanprover-community/mathlib4/blob/v4.19.0/Mathlib/NumberTheory/LegendreSymbol/QuadraticChar/Basic.lean)

For example, $N=77$ has 60 units and $a=2$ has negative Jacobi symbol. Exactly 30 choices of $b$ cover all four signs. With two draws, 900 of the 3,600 ordered choices miss a sign. These small counts can also be checked by listing squares modulo 7 and 11.

## What independent trials buy

Keep the same $1,a$ and draw $k$ units independently with replacement. Append $b_i,ab_i\bmod N$ for each draw. The failure probability is **exactly $2^{-k}$**. A failure means every draw was square at the same hidden prime. With 16 draws, the list has at most 34 entries and misses a sign on one in 65,536 lists. The probability is over the draws for each fixed pair of primes. The factorization remains unknown, so the generator cannot recognize when all four signs first appeared. [Lean proof](../PrimeFactors/RandomSignMenu.lean)

For comparison, keep the same two anchors and append $m=2k$ independent units. Each of the two missing patterns is absent with probability $(3/4)^m$, and both are absent with probability $(1/2)^m$. Inclusion–exclusion gives failure probability $2(3/4)^m-(1/2)^m$. Even the chance the independent list misses one particular pattern is $(9/16)^k$, greater than the paired list’s total failure probability $(8/16)^k$ for every $k>0$. With four total entries, the anchored independent list succeeds with probability $1/8$ and the paired list succeeds with probability $1/2$. For a list of $L>0$ entirely independent units with no fixed entries, the classical four-coupon count gives success probability $1-4(3/4)^L+6(1/2)^L-4(1/4)^L$. At $L=4$ this is $4!/4^4=3/32$. These comparisons budget discriminant entries. The paired construction uses one random draw and one public modular multiplication per pair. ## When only one Jacobi sign is needed

The signs of $b$ and $ab$ have opposite products. To cover the two hidden patterns of one prescribed Jacobi sign, keep the one of $1,a$ with that sign, then keep just the matching member of each pair. The Jacobi symbol of $b$ tells us which member to use. A draw that covered the missing pattern of this sign also covered the missing pattern of the other sign in the full menu. Thus $k+1$ downstream discriminant tests suffice for the chosen sign, with the same $2^{-k}$ failure probability. Covering all four at once uses both signs. [Selection theorem](../PrimeFactors/RandomSignMenu.lean)

Uniform units can be drawn by sampling residues and checking their gcd with $N$. If an anchor has not yet been found, exactly half of the units have negative Jacobi symbol, so a random anchor search takes two unit draws on average. That search is separate from the $k$ draws above. The open factoring question is how frequently a covered sign yields a group element whose local orders a feasible exponent or bounded search can separate. The sign count alone supplies no estimate of that frequency.
