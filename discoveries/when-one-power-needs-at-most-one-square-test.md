# One square test per choice of power

We can reject a proposed power using just a division, and check at most one candidate for the factor sum when the two primes have the same known bit width. Across 12,994 small products, adding these rules reduced the number of square-root tests from 16,287 to **797**, keeping all **130** factorizations found by this method. The five powers tried on our fresh 2,048-bit comparison all fail the remainder rule. The open problem is choosing a power that reveals the factor sum on a large input. [Census with exact counts](../research/power-denominator-census.json) · [What a good power would tell us](what-a-successful-power-would-reveal.md)

For example, take $N=2,585,873$ and the publicly specified power $E=720,720$, the least common multiple of 1 through 16. The sixth continued-fraction convergent of $E/N$ is $12/43$. If it is the true ratio $E/F$, then $F=720720\cdot43/12=2,582,580$ and the prime sum is $N+1-F=3,294$. Its discriminant is $712^2$, giving **1,291 and 2,003**. Four fractions gave an integer candidate for $F$, and only one fit the size of the primes. This product was selected retrospectively from an earlier independently generated panel. It was the only one of the 24 original panel products to split with this exponent. The same fixed choice was rejected immediately on the fresh 2,048-bit product. ## Discarding a power before searching

Assume $1\le E\le N$ and write $N=dE+r$, $0\le r<E$. Every positive continued-fraction convergent of $E/N$ is at most $1/d$. If the exact ratio $E/F$ occurs, it follows that $F\ge dE=N-r$, hence $p+q-1=N-F\le r$. Since $(p+q)^2\ge4N$, a choice with $(r+1)^2<4N$ cannot work. Testing this rule cut 346,994 visits to continued fractions down to 137,208 in the census. On its own the rule saved no square-root tests because the skipped candidates already have negative discriminants. [Proof for Mathlib convergents](../PrimeFactors/PowerRatioRemainder.lean)

## Which fraction can give the sum?

Take a positive upper convergent $a/b$ and write its positive remainder as $R=Na-Eb$. If the fraction represents $E/F$, the difference $N-F$ is $R/a$. Moving to the next positive upper convergent at least doubles $a$ and strictly decreases $R$. The differences therefore decrease by more than a factor of two. For distinct $k$-bit primes their true difference $N-F=p+q-1$ lies in $[2^k,2^{k+1})$. That interval can contain the difference for at most one upper convergent. The scan uses integer comparisons before taking a square root. [Finite Euclidean scan and uniqueness proof](../PrimeFactors/UpperConvergentDeficit.lean) · [Agreement with Mathlib’s convergents](../PrimeFactors/UpperConvergentAgreement.lean)

The census used all distinct same-width prime pairs at widths 9, 10 and 11. For each product it tried the powers $1$, $N-1$, $(N-1)/2$, $840$, and $720,720$ when they were at most $N$. The three widths respectively required 153, 26, and 618 square roots after both rules. They gave 60, 14, and 56 factorizations. All sixty 9-bit results already follow from the single candidate $F=840\lfloor N/840\rfloor$. The larger two widths found their successes with $720,720$. These counts concern only factorizations accessible to the specified convergent search. [Results by width and exponent](../research/power-denominator-census.json)
