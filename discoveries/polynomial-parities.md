# Which negative quadratic-sieve rows guarantee a factor?

For a quadratic-sieve polynomial $F(x)=Ax^2+2Bx+C$, with $A>0$ and $B^2-AC=N$, put $t=|Ax+B|$. Then $AF(x)=t^2-N$. A negative row has $0<t<\sqrt N$. On $77$, the polynomial with $A=13$, $B=5$, $C=-4$ gives the reduced values $-4,-1$ at $x=0,-1$. Their product is square and $\gcd(5\cdot8+13\cdot2,77)=11$. The following theorem explains why any such match within a common nontrivial coefficient works. [Lean proofs and examples](../PrimeFactors/PolynomialRelations.lean)

## A complete classification for pairs

Take two distinct positive midpoints $t<s<\sqrt N$, put $D=N-t^2$, $E=N-s^2$ and suppose $DE=z^2$. A proper factor follows unless $t^2+s^2=N$. The identities
$$
(N-ts)^2-z^2=N(s-t)^2,\qquad (ts)^2-z^2=N(t^2+s^2-N)
$$
give $ts+z<N$ and $ts=z$ exactly in the stated exception. In all other cases $ts$ and $z$ are different square roots modulo $N$, even up to sign, and $\gcd(ts+z,N)$ is a proper divisor. At the exception the two integer roots coincide. If $N$ is odd and $ts$ is a unit then the two ordinary gcd tests give $1$ and $N$.

If coefficients of the two rows have a common divisor $C>1$ coprime to $N$, then $C\mid D,E$. The exception would yield $C\mid t^2,s^2$, hence $C\mid N$, which is impossible. For a common coefficient $A>1$ it suffices that the two reduced values multiply to $w^2$. The full square root is $Aw$. Polynomials with $B$ replaced by $B+jA$ shift the index by $j$. Negating $B$ reflects the index. The same absolute midpoint obtained twice gives algebraically identical rows. A bounded shifted interval can still contribute new midpoints at its ends. For general dependencies the product of the coefficients and the product of the reduced magnitudes must have the same valuation parity at each prime. A selected coefficient prime occurring just once, and absent from the reduced values, rules out that dependency. [Checked identities](../PrimeFactors/PolynomialRelations.lean) · [Silverman’s multiple-polynomial sieve](https://doi.org/10.1090/S0025-5718-1987-0866119-8)

Two distinct unordered exceptional pairs together suffice. Write them as $N=a^2+b^2=c^2+d^2$, with the smaller coordinate first in each and $a<c$. Then $a^2+c^2<N$. Crossing the two representations gives the square dependency $(N-a^2)(N-c^2)=(bd)^2$ and therefore a factor. For example $221=5^2+14^2=10^2+11^2$, and $\gcd(5\cdot10+14\cdot11,221)=17$. Thus every two different unordered negative-row matches on distinct absolute midpoints, even if their square classes differ, suffice to obtain a factor.

## How many matching partners can a midpoint have?

Suppose $N=pq$ with distinct odd primes. An input midpoint sharing a prime with $N$ gives a direct gcd. For unit midpoints write $N=t^2+d a^2$ with $d$ positive squarefree and $a>0$. Both $d$ and $a$ are units. The ratio $t/a$ is one of at most four square roots of $-d$ modulo $N$, by CRT. Distinct positive representations of this fixed $d$ give distinct root ratios. Indeed, if $t<s$ and $N=s^2+d b^2$, we have $a>b$ and $0<sa-tb<N$. The latter follows since $s,a<\sqrt N$, and both products are positive. So $tb$ and $sa$ differ modulo $N$.

If $d>1$, the ratios also differ up to sign. The identity
$$d(tb+sa)^2+(ts-dab)^2=N^2$$
gives $tb+sa<N$. Consequently $d>1$ has at most two positive midpoints, one from each of the two pairs of opposite modular roots. At $d=1$ there are at most four positive midpoints. Finding roots first and recovering norm representations by Euclidean reduction is the classical setting of Cornacchia’s algorithm. Obtaining a second root up to sign gives a factor directly. [Lean bound for the two directions](../PrimeFactors/PolynomialRelations.lean) · [Morain and Nicolas](https://www.lix.polytechnique.fr/Labo/Francois.Morain/Articles/cornac.pdf)

For $L$ distinct unit midpoints in any subset below $\sqrt N$, the number of ordered matching pairs of different midpoints is at most $L+8$. Outside $d=1$ each midpoint has at most one partner. The at most four midpoints inside $d=1$ have at most three each. In the full interval on a balanced 2,048-bit product $L\ge2^{1023}$, since only the smaller prime can occur as a nonunit and it occurs just once. With $2^{48}$ independent uniform draws from this interval of units the union bound gives probability below $2^{-927}$ of even one such match. This count concerns only two negative rows. Product-guided samples and combinations of more rows have different distributions.

## Nine fields in which matches exist

There is also a positive existence statement for the target input distribution. Take
$$d\in\{1,2,3,7,11,19,43,67,163\}.$$
When both odd primes $p,q$ split in $\mathbb Q(\sqrt{-d})$, there are two representations $x^2+d y^2=4N$ whose unit ratios $x/y$ differ up to global sign modulo $N$. For $d=1,2$ one can use $N$ itself. We exclude primes dividing $2d$, which is automatic at the target width. With these two representations a gcd of their cross products splits $N$.

Here is the reason. Each of the nine full rings of integers has class number one. One can check the particular list by Gauss reduction. For discriminant $-D$ every primitive reduced positive form has $|b|\le a\le c$ and $4ac=D+b^2$, so $3a^2\le D\le163$. For the nine fundamental $D$ the finite check through $a=7$ always yields $a=1$, the principal class. Lean verifies this finite check. For split prime ideals $P,\bar P$ over $p$ and $Q,\bar Q$ over $q$, each of $PQ$ and $P\bar Q$ is principal. Its generator has norm $N$ and the form $(x+y\sqrt{-d})/2$ for integers $x,y$. For $d=1,2$ the coordinates in the integral basis already give norm $N$. Each coordinate is invertible modulo $N$, since divisibility of $y$ by $p$ or $q$ would also make $x$ divisible by it and its square would divide $4N$. The same argument applies in the other direction because $d$ is coprime to $N$. The generator's ratio specifies which of the two prime ideals above each rational prime divides it. The two generators agree at $p$ and differ at $q$, even after using positive coordinates, up to a simultaneous change of sign. This proves the split. Conversely, any unit representations give a root of $-d$ at both primes. [Finite reduced-form proof](../PrimeFactors/PolynomialRelations.lean) · [Class-number-one list](https://doi.org/10.5802/jtnb.1141)

The nine quadratic characters on prime residues are independent. The characters of $-1$ and $-2$ give all four sign pairs modulo eight. For each odd $d$ in the list, quadratic reciprocity makes the character of $-d$ the Legendre symbol on residues modulo $d$. CRT therefore gives all $2^9$ sign vectors with equal multiplicity modulo $Q=8\cdot3\cdot7\cdot11\cdot19\cdot43\cdot67\cdot163$. For two independent uniform unit residues the proportion splitting together in at least one of the nine fields is
$$1-(3/4)^9=242461/262144\approx92.4915\%.$$
Conditional on the product residue $a\pmod Q$, let $e$ count the nine positive Jacobi symbols of the product. In each such coordinate the first prime's sign determines both primes' signs. The conditional proportion is $1-2^{-e}$. The product alone thus excludes every field with negative Jacobi symbol. The prime number theorem in fixed progressions gives these limits for uniformly chosen distinct primes of equal bit length with a full-width product. At 2,048 bits the probability differs from this limit by less than $2^{-410}$ under Dirichlet GRH.

### A finite error bound from Dirichlet GRH

Set $X=2^{1023}$ and count ordered distinct $p,q\in(X,2X)$ with $pq\ge2X^2$. Symmetry gives identical proportions after forgetting the order. Write $H=\varphi(Q)=3,879,912,960<2^{32}$. Under Dirichlet GRH, Theorem 1 of Ernvall-Hytönen and Palojärvi gives $|\pi(t;Q,a)-\operatorname{li}(t)/H|<E=2^{520}$ on $X\le t\le2X$ in every unit class. Indeed $Q=16,488,700,536<X$, $700<\log t<710$, $\log Q<24$ and $\sqrt t\le2^{512}$. After division by $\sqrt t$, their five terms of order $\sqrt t$ are bounded respectively by $70,13,1,1,1$. The two terms involving $t^{1/4}$ contribute less than $1$ combined after division because $t^{-1/4}<2^{-255}$. The theorem uses the ordinary logarithmic integral $\operatorname{li}$, whose derivative is $1/\log t$. This application of the 2026 Dirichlet release concerns the distribution of inputs. [Ernvall-Hytönen and Palojärvi, Theorem 1](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2003.01925#page=2) · [Dirichlet statement and independent verification](https://math.mit.edu/~drew/grh/)

Put $w(t)=\operatorname{li}(2X)-\operatorname{li}(2X^2/t)$ and $I=\int_X^{2X}w\,d\operatorname{li}$. For each first prime the number of eligible partners in a fixed residue class differs from $w(p)/H$ by at most $2E$. The lower threshold is never an integer since $p\nmid2X^2$. There are at most $X$ first primes. Summing over the first primes of one residue class and applying partial summation to $w$, which increases from zero to at most $X$, gives an additional error at most $2EX/H$. Thus each ordered residue-pair count, before excluding the diagonal, differs from $I/H^2$ by at most $4EX$. At most $X$ diagonal pairs are removed overall.

Restrict to any set of cells, either among all $H^2$ residue pairs or within the $H$ pairs yielding a given product residue. For the total and for any subevent, the error divided by the respective reference population ($I$ or $I/H$) is at most $(4EXH^2+XH)/I$. Integrating only over the square $[3X/2,2X]^2$ gives $I\ge[X/(2\log(2X))]^2>2^{2024}$. The relative error is less than $2^{-414}$. Dividing event count by total count perturbs the stated proportion by less than $2^{-410}$. This uses the analytic and ideal-class results on paper, with the finite reduced-form and factor-extraction arithmetic checked in Lean.

These are probabilities of existence on the specified input population. A product-only scan for the representations may take order $\sqrt N$ positions for each of nine fields. The CRT proof uses the unknown primes to establish existence. Locating these midpoints from the product within a short polynomial walk remains open.
