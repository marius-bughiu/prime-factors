# Which residues can the sum of two factors occupy?

Let $N=pq$ for distinct odd primes $p<q\le 2p$. The balance assumption gives an interval computable from $N$ for the unknown sum $S=p+q$.

$$2\sqrt N\le S\le 3\sqrt{N/2}.$$

Indeed, $S/\sqrt N=\sqrt{q/p}+\sqrt{p/q}$ increases with $q/p$ on $[1,2]$. For an integer $s$ in the interval, set $D_s=s^2-4N$. If $D_s=d^2$, reducing $s^2-d^2=4N$ modulo 4 shows that $s$ and $d$ have the same parity. Thus $(s-d)/2$ and $(s+d)/2$ are positive integral factors. The pair $(1,N)$ has sum $N+1$ outside the interval, so $S$ is the unique passing sum. The interval can contain order $\sqrt N$ candidates even when both primes have the same bit length. ## Exact local information

For an odd prime $\ell\nmid N$, the admissible sums modulo $\ell$ form the image $H_\ell=\{x+N/x:x\in\mathbb F_\ell^\times\}$. A residue $s$ lies in $H_\ell$ exactly when $s^2-4N$ is a square modulo $\ell$, including zero. The two roots of $X^2-sX+N$ explain both directions. The involution $x\mapsto N/x$ has $1+(\frac N\ell)$ fixed points, and each orbit yields one sum. Thus

$$|H_\ell|=\frac{\ell+(\frac N\ell)}{2}.$$

Every nonzero residue of a single factor is locally possible because $N/x$ supplies a partner. It is the **sum** that occupies about half the classes. For $M$ a product of distinct such primes, the Chinese remainder theorem combines the two factor residues independently, so $H_M$ corresponds to the Cartesian product of the $H_\ell$. Its exact density is $2^{-\omega(M)}\prod_{\ell\mid M}\bigl(1+(\frac{N}{\ell})/\ell\bigr)$. For the midpoint $S/2$, multiplication by $2^{-1}$ preserves these cardinalities. [Local discriminant equivalence](../PrimeFactors/FactorSum.lean) · [Lean fibre count](../PrimeFactors/ResidueCount.lean) · [Lean CRT product](../PrimeFactors/FactorSumCRT.lean) · [Hittmeir, §3](https://arxiv.org/html/2205.10074v2#S3)

For $N=143$, the admissible sums modulo 35 are $4,11,14,21,24,31$, including the true sum 24. A second example shows what happens in a short interval. For $N=34387$, balance permits the eleven even sums from 372 through 392. Modulo 3, 5 and 7 the admissible sets are respectively $\{1,2\},\{2,3\},\{0,3,4\}$. Only 382, 388 and 392 satisfy all three tests. The extra condition $S\equiv N+1\bmod 4$ for odd factors eliminates 382. The value 392 still survives, although $392^2-4N=16116$ lies between $126^2$ and $127^2$. At 388 the discriminant is $114^2$, giving the factors 137 and 251.

## How much work can whole-period sieving save?

In any interval of $W$ consecutive midpoints, each complete block of $M$ contains exactly $|H_M|$ survivors. If $M\le W$ and $t=\omega(M)$, the number surviving is at least

$$\left\lfloor W/M\right\rfloor |H_M|\ \ge\ (W/2)\,3^{-t}.$$

Indeed each odd-prime density is at least $1/3$, and $\lfloor W/M\rfloor\ge W/(2M)$. Since $M\ge(t+1)!$, we have $t=O(\log W/\log\log W)$ as $W\to\infty$. Consequently a method that explicitly visits **every** survivor of one such squarefree modulus must visit at least $W\exp[-O(\log W/\log\log W)]=W^{1-o(1)}$ values. This restriction applies to full interval enumeration with $M\le W$. Early success, short intervals inside a much larger period, and algorithms operating on implicit candidate sets require their own analyses. [Lean lower bound for every finite interval](../PrimeFactors/SieveInterval.lean)

Hittmeir gives a deterministic improvement for finding a proper factor in Theorem 4.6. For a semiprime, his parameter is $\Delta=(p-q)^2/(4\sqrt N)$, an upper bound on $S-2\sqrt N$. Up to factors polynomial in $\log N$ and $\log\Delta$, the bit bound for growing $\Delta$ is $\Delta\exp[-(\log 2+o(1))\log\Delta/\log\log\Delta]$. In §5 the stronger prediction (5.1) on the **number of survivors in a short interval** is explicitly heuristic. On multiples of $N$, Lehman gives a deterministic exponent $1/3$ and Hittmeir’s time-space tradeoff gives $2/9+o(1)$. McKee’s $N^{1/4+\epsilon}$ estimate for a rational difference-of-squares method is heuristic. These are bounds for specific algorithms. [Hittmeir, §§4–6](https://arxiv.org/html/2205.10074v2#S4) · [Lehman](https://doi.org/10.1090/S0025-5718-1974-0340163-2) · [Lawrence–Lehman tradeoff](https://arxiv.org/abs/2006.16729v2) · [McKee](https://doi.org/10.1090/S0025-5718-99-01133-3)

## A uniform set of primes that isolates the sum

**Paper deduction from Dirichlet GRH.** Suppose $N\ge3000$ and $b$ is its bit length. Apply the square test for $D_s$ at every odd prime $\ell\le324b^2$ coprime to $N$. The only integer sum surviving in the balanced interval is $S$. The cutoff is deliberately conservative and depends only on the input.

To see this, take a false $s$. Then $0<D=D_s\le N/2$ is a nonsquare. Write $D=du^2$ with $d>1$ squarefree, and put $Q=8ND$. On units modulo $Q$, quadratic reciprocity makes the Jacobi symbol $\chi(a)=(d/a)$ a character. The sign and the factor at 2 depend on $a\bmod 8$, and each odd prime factor $r$ of $d$ contributes a Legendre symbol in $a\bmod r$. This character is nonprincipal. If some odd $r\mid d$, choose $a\equiv1\bmod8$, a nonsquare modulo $r$, and 1 modulo all other odd prime divisors of $Q$. CRT gives a unit with $\chi(a)=-1$. If $d=2$, use $a\equiv5\bmod8$ and 1 at the odd primes. Hence $\ker\chi$ is a proper subgroup of $(\mathbb Z/Q)^\times$. The squarefree decomposition is used only to prove existence.

Theorem 1.1(1) of Lamzouri, Li and Soundararajan, under Dirichlet GRH, gives a prime $\ell\nmid Q$ outside this kernel with $\ell\le (L+B(Q))^2$, $L=\ln Q$. Their explicit $B(Q)$ is at most $2\ln L+3+2\omega(Q)(\ln L)^2/L$. Here $Q\ge3000$, $\omega(Q)\le L/\ln 2$, $2\ln L\le L$, $3\le L$ and $(\ln L)^2\le L$. Thus $B(Q)\le5L$. Since $Q\le4N^2$ and $N\ge4$, $\ell\le36L^2\le324(\ln N)^2\le324b^2$. At this odd prime, $\ell\nmid u$ and $(D/\ell)=(d/\ell)=-1$. It excludes $s$, while $S$ has square discriminant at every prime. The same cutoff therefore works simultaneously for all the false sums. [Theorem 1.1(1)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1309.3595v3#page=2) · [Verified Dirichlet GRH release](https://math.mit.edu/~drew/grh/) · [Related work on pseudosquares](https://cs.uwaterloo.ca/~shallit/Papers/pseudo.pdf)

**Two 1,024-bit prime factors.** Every such pair satisfies $p<q<2p$ when ordered and distinct, and $N<2^{2048}$. For each false $s$ in the interval above, $0<D\le N/2$ is a nonsquare. Use $Q=4096D$ in the character argument. It is a multiple of $8D$ with $3000<Q<2^{2059}$. Now $L<1442$, $\ln L<8$, and $1/\ln 2<1.5$. The same bound on $\omega(Q)$ gives $B(Q)<2\cdot8+3+3\cdot8^2=211$. Thus the witnessing prime satisfies $\ell<1653^2=2732409<3000000$. It is odd and smaller than $2^{1023}\le p$, hence coprime to $N$. The true sum is therefore the unique integer in the balanced interval whose discriminant is a square modulo every odd prime below three million. The bound also covers false candidates with small $D$. This deduces an explicit finite constraint system for that input size. [Finite isolation theorem](../PrimeFactors/Isolation.lean) · [LLS, Theorem 1.1(1)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1309.3595v3#page=2) · [Dirichlet verification](https://math.mit.edu/~drew/grh/)

**Research target.** Given $s$, one integer square root already decides the test unconditionally. What the GRH deduction supplies is a single family of small modular constraints with a unique solution in the interval. For cutoff $B=324b^2$, the local constraints occupy at most $B$ tables of at most $B$ residues each. Retrieving the solution without enumerating a large CRT product is the missing algorithmic step. One sufficient result would be an affordable test for existence of a survivor in a given subinterval. Bisection would then locate the unique sum in $O(b)$ such tests. The cost of each existence test, including how it handles the CRT combinations, is the open part of that approach. Lean establishes the exact local and two-modulus counts, the finite complete-block bound and isolation given a separating prime for every nonsquare discriminant. The asymptotic full-period obstruction and GRH isolation argument here are mathematical derivations on paper.
