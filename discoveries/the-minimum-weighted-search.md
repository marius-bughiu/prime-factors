# How many blocks does a weighted factor search really need?

We recovered both primes of an 89-bit number using **33,010 search blocks**. The previous best schedule in the project used 34,264 with the same list of weights and the same table of 53,130 starting powers. We now have a schedule and a matching lower bound. Anyone using these same windows to cover every possible balanced factor ratio needs at least 33,010 blocks. The search found **20,895,494,959,567 × 21,017,622,445,783**. Choosing the schedule took 155 seconds locally. Running it took about one second. For comparison, an earlier fast planner and factor search took 1.14 seconds end to end using 35,344 blocks. [Checked factors](https://marius-bughiu.github.io/prime-factors/#trace-optimal-89-a) · [Measurements and certificates for all four comparisons](../research/trace-cover-comparison.json)

The input is a product $N=pq$ of primes greater than three with $p\le q\le2p$. The proof guarantees coverage even if nobody knows the ratio $q/p$. It prices every ratio from 1 to 2, including ratios that cannot occur at that specific product. A method capable of locating the true ratio or detecting an unrelated modular collision can spend less.

## The schedule and its lower bound

Choose coprime weights $a,b$ with $b\le a\le2b$ and $1\le b\le K$. The weighted sum $ap+bq$ is at least $2\sqrt{abN}$. The product tells us its residue modulo $s=\gcd(24,2(a+bN))$. Starting at the first permitted integer at or above that lower bound, a block of length $m$ buys the next $m$ permitted values. Across all weights we can reuse one table of $m$ powers to test the resulting blocks. This is the collision method of Harvey, specialized to the balanced interval in the earlier project search. A proper gcd or an exact square gives a candidate split, and Lean checks any reported prime factors. [Starting powers and collisions](farey-weighted-sums.md) · [Harvey, Algorithms 4.1–4.2](https://arxiv.org/abs/2010.05450)

Give a weight pair $g$ blocks and write $T=\mathrm{start}+smg$ for its first omitted value. At ratio $x=q/p$ the trace lies below $T$ precisely when
$$N(a+bx)^2<T^2x.$$
For each schedule, the smallest of the lines $(a+bx)/T$ chooses a row at every ratio. The lines meet at rational numbers. On a segment controlled by one line, the expression $N(a+bx)^2-T^2x$ is convex, so checking its two ends proves strict coverage throughout, including irrational ratios. This permits any number of skipped slopes and segments whose center lies outside them. [Coverage theorem](../PrimeFactors/TraceCoverCertificate.lean) · [Why the true trace occurs at a searched index](../PrimeFactors/TraceProgression.lean)

To prove a schedule is cheapest, give nonnegative integer weights to a finite list of test ratios. Verify that **every** window bought with $g$ blocks covers at most $g$ units of test weight. Any schedule covering the interval must then pay at least the sum of all the test weights. As soon as that sum equals the verified cost of a complete schedule, the minimum is exact. These test ratios are chosen from the public input. They are independent of the factor labels.

To find the two certificates, solve the problem on a sorted sample of ratios. A window covers consecutive sample positions, so a shortest-path calculation prices all prefixes. The increases in prefix cost give the test weights. Check the resulting plan on the whole interval using the rational intersections. Add uncovered intersections and repeat. In the larger implementation, temporarily omit expensive windows, then use the exact test weights to find any omitted window whose cost is too low. If a window of $g$ blocks covers weight $v\le g$, all budgets from $v$ through $g$ pass the lower-bound check on that slope. Sorted test ratios and sums of their weights make that check inexpensive. The final verification prices arbitrary nonnegative budgets. The method uses the classical consecutive-ones structure of interval coverage. ## What happens at 2,048 bits?

With the two weights $(1,1)$ and $(2,1)$ and **one candidate per block**, we proved the exact minimum on both full-size comparison products. On each it exceeds $2^{1017}$ candidates. For a trial total, assign $u$ candidates to the first slope and the remainder to the second. If neither window spans the entire interval on its own, overlap depends on $(2T-U)(U-T)>N$, where $T,U$ are their first omitted labels at the two slopes. This expression is a concave quadratic in $u$. Testing the integer allocations nearest its maximum and the endpoint cases settles a trial budget. Binary search found the exact minima in 1,018 and 1,019 decisions. Lean checked certificates for the winning allocation and every cheaper alternative. [Two-slope budget theorem](../PrimeFactors/TwoTraceBudget.lean) · [Both full decimal minima](../PrimeFactors/TwoTraceExamples.lean)

At $K=8$ there are 23 weight pairs. We priced a different tradeoff by setting the block size to $\lfloor\sqrt N\rfloor$ divided by 8,192, rounded down. The exact minimum to cover $[1,2]$ was **23 blocks on the first product and 25 on the second**. If both primes have exactly 1,024 bits, the product gives the stronger upper bound $q/p\le (2^{1024}-1)^2/N$. On those smaller intervals the exact minima drop to **10 and 21**. Each plan still calls for **more than $2^{1010}$ starting powers**. We checked the plans and their lower bounds without building that table or finding either pair. [Fresh equal-width proof](https://marius-bughiu.github.io/prime-factors/proofs/trace-cover-fresh-equal-2048.lean) · [Crossed equal-width proof](https://marius-bughiu.github.io/prime-factors/proofs/trace-cover-crossed-equal-2048.lean)

The limits also reach genuine prime pairs. For gaps between $2^{975}$ and $2^{990}$, a table of $2^{24}$ starting powers still falls short of the true weighted sum after $2^{894}$ blocks per slope for every denominator up to $2^{32}$. We bounded how many prime pairs have those gaps. [Proof and population model](prime-pairs-outside-weighted-prefixes.md)

A separate theorem determines the best common bound on the *unrounded excess* $ap+bq-2\sqrt{abN}$ for every balanced ratio and every denominator cutoff. The worst ratio is the crossing between slopes $1/1$ and $(K+1)/K$. At $K=32$ the bound improves from $\sqrt N/4356$ to below $\sqrt N/5833$. This prices the width of the worst search band. The schedule optimizer also accounts for integer rounding, strides, and the cost across all bands. [Sharp bound and its attaining real ratio](../PrimeFactors/WeightedRatioCover.lean)

Neighboring slopes also reveal why two good guesses would be valuable. If $ad-bc=\pm1$ and we know both true traces $S=ap+bq$, $T=cp+dq$, the gcd of $a+bN-S$ and $c+dN-T$ equals $p-1$. But guessing both traces requires respecting their joint equation. Their allowed pairs form exactly $\varphi(M)$ labels modulo any $M$ coprime to $N$. For the weights $(1,1)$ and $(2,1)$ and a concrete 620-bit combined modulus, covering those labels by independent, always-valid pairs of lists needs more than $2^{531}$ such rectangles. This result prices that list representation. The polynomial collision search can process many labels together. [Exact gcd and joint-label theorem](../PrimeFactors/WeightedCollisionReuse.lean)
