# How many primes can rule out a whole group of quartics?

The fresh comparison at 600 bits had 23,967,979 possible values for a trace. A fixed list of 51 primes ruled out all of them in the experiment. There is a reason a short list can suffice. For **any** collection of this many nonzero quartics with no nonzero rational roots, at most **60 suitably chosen primes** reject them all on the nonzero residues. The primes in this existence result may be large. The experiment used primes at most 251. ## Which quartics eventually fail a prime test?

At level $j$, a factor $x$ must solve $F_j(x)=F(x)-jMx^2=0$, with $x$ a nonzero residue at each auxiliary prime not dividing the product. Remove powers of $X$ and take the primitive squarefree part of $F_j$. Exclude the finite set of primes dividing the original content, the remaining constant or leading coefficient, its discriminant, or the public product and modulus. At every other prime the test asks whether the remaining polynomial has a root. A nonzero constant already excludes the level away from its prime divisors.

If the remaining polynomial has a rational root $a/b$, that root supplies a nonzero modular root at every good prime outside the prime divisors of $ab$. It can do this even when it supplies no integer factor in the allowed interval. Integer-root isolation and checking divisibility of the original product settle these cases.

Suppose it has no rational root. If the squarefree part is irreducible of degree $d=2,3,4$, its splitting-field group acts transitively on its roots. For a uniform element of the group, let $f$ count its fixed roots. The orbit-counting lemma gives $\mathbb E f=1$ and $\mathbb E[f(f-1)]\ge1$, since the ordered pairs of distinct roots form at least one orbit. Now $f(f-1)\le d(f-1)_+$, and $\mathbb E[(f-1)_+]=\Pr(f=0)$. Thus at least a fraction $1/d$ of group elements have no fixed root. For two irreducible quadratics with the same splitting field, half the group swaps both pairs of roots. With different quadratic fields, a quarter of the biquadratic group swaps both. These cases exhaust degree at most four. By Chebotarev's theorem, the set of rejecting primes therefore has natural density at least $1/4$ for each fixed polynomial. [Cameron and Cohen](https://ir.cwi.nl/pub/1563/1563D.pdf) · [Frobenius densities](https://doi.org/10.1112/blms.13002)

## One list for many levels

Form the compositum of the splitting fields of a finite collection of $m$ such polynomials. Restriction onto each splitting-field Galois group is surjective, with fibers of equal size. On the common group, each polynomial therefore passes at most three quarters of the elements. Of all $k$-tuples of group elements, at most a fraction $m(3/4)^k$ fail to reject at least one polynomial, by the union bound. When this number is less than one, a covering tuple exists. Chebotarev supplies a prime of the specified conjugacy class at each position, avoiding any finite list of exceptions. This allows correlations between different polynomials. The finite counting step and the numerical inequalities are proved in Lean. The number-field argument here uses the cited theorems. [Tuple bound and finite constants](../PrimeFactors/QuarticPrimeCover.lean)

At 25,487 levels the same bound gives 36 primes. These counts say how few primes can suffice when each level has no nonzero rational root. They give neither a way to select the small primes in advance nor a bound on the number of trace values that the bitset must visit. For any prescribed list of primes with product $A$, the polynomial $X^4-(A^4+1)$ has the root 1 modulo each of them. It has no rational root, since it is monic and $A^4+1$ lies strictly between consecutive fourth powers. This example concerns general quartics. ## What the Riemann hypothesis contributes

For one root-free level, let $L$ be the splitting field. Put every excluded prime into a squarefree product $B$ and set $E=L(\sqrt B)$. An effective Chebotarev theorem under Dedekind GRH supplies a good detecting prime at most $(4\log |D_E|+2.5[E:\mathbb Q]+5)^2$. Here $D_E$ is the field discriminant and $[E:\mathbb Q]\le48$ for one quartic. Using the compositum of an entire collection in this bound would change the degree and discriminant. The pinned Riemann repository states results for Dirichlet characters. In the abelian case the relevant Dedekind zeta function factors into Dirichlet L-functions. A general quartic can have a nonabelian splitting field and needs an additional hypothesis for this effective bound. The observed 51-prime calculation and the qualitative covering result are unconditional.
