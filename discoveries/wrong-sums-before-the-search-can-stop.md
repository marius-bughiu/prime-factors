# Wrong sums before the search can stop

Suppose we check candidate sums in the repository’s order and, for each candidate, can ask whether its discriminant is a square modulo any power of any prime through 643. **More than $2^{765}$ wrong sums pass every one of these checks before the true sum is reached**, for all but fewer than one in $2^{35}$ eligible inputs. The inputs are all products of two distinct 1,024-bit primes whose product has 2,048 bits, counted once per unordered pair. The result is unconditional. [Lean population theorem](../PrimeFactors/PersistentScorePrefix.lean)

For a product $N=pq$, the true sum $s=p+q$ satisfies $s^2-4N=(q-p)^2$. Given an even guess $s=2h$, we can equivalently test whether $h^2-N$ is a square integer. The earlier whole-interval count left open whether most wrong survivors came *after* the true sum, where the search would never visit them. The new proof puts the wrong guesses strictly ahead of the first successful square test. [Earlier whole-interval count](persistent-local-survivors.md) · [Search order](using-both-the-location-and-remainder-of-a-sum.md)

1. Let $L=\lceil\sqrt{4N}\rceil$. The target sum is at least $L+2^{900}$ for all but fewer than one in $2^{35}$ eligible inputs. For these inputs, start at the midpoint $\lfloor\sqrt N\rfloor+1$ and take $2^{896}$ consecutive midpoints. Even the largest score weight on one of these earlier sums gives a better score than the smallest weight on the true sum. [Score comparison and rounding at the endpoints](../PrimeFactors/EarlyScoreWindow.lean) · [Population bound](../PrimeFactors/WithinResidueSearchFloor.lean)
2. This early interval contains a complete period of classes that guarantee square roots at every power of the 117 primes through 643. The period is between $2^{895}$ and $2^{896}$, and contains more than $2^{765}$ such classes for every eligible product. Each gives a different candidate sum. [Exact count](../PrimeFactors/EarlyPersistentMidpoints.lean)
3. Each candidate also passes the search’s original admission tests modulo 4 and 25. If $r^2=h^2-N$ modulo a tested prime power, then $h-r$ and $h+r$ have sum $2h$ and product $N$ there. In ordinary integers the discriminant fails the square test at every one of the counted sums. [Local factor pairs and integer-square failure](../PrimeFactors/PersistentScorePrefix.lean)

For a small example, take $145{,}684{,}811=8{,}999\times16{,}189$. The eighth candidate is 24,180. The true sum, 25,188, is thirtieth. For the eighth candidate, $12{,}090^2-N=483{,}289$ lies strictly between $695^2$ and $696^2$. This number has a square root at every power of the ten primes through 29. Of the first thirty candidates, exactly these two survive all ten primes. The prime 31 rejects the wrong sum because the remainder is $-1$ and 31 is 3 modulo 4. This particular example was selected to illustrate an early false survivor. [Reproduce the ordered prefix and all ten root checks](../experiments/early_persistent_score_example.py)

The bound concerns this ordering and rejection by these 117 primes. It remains valid if the search chooses the prime and precision separately for each candidate. A different way of skipping whole groups, or a constraint involving other information about the product, requires its own analysis.
