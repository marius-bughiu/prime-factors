# How many square tests survive a factor-interval sweep?

For a 2,048-bit product of two distinct odd primes, **more than $2^{635}$ different positive integers still need a square decision** if we sweep the balanced interval using the chord bounds and the full modulo-24 filter. This count already shares every repeated test, even across different blocks and different weights. When the larger prime is at most five quarters of the smaller one, the bound already holds strictly before the block containing the smaller prime. It explains why reducing each block to a handful of guesses still leaves an impractical search. [Distinct-test theorem](../PrimeFactors/QuotientStripDistinctTests.lean) · [How one block reduces to five labels](covering-a-factor-interval-with-five-lines.md)

A small example shows why counting the blocks alone would be misleading. For $346597=571\times607$, the consecutive blocks $[465,480]$ and $[481,496]$ both choose the weighted sum $3x+2y$. Each block has possible labels 2884 and 2885. The product residues modulo 24 leave only **2885** in both. Its discriminant is $2885^2-4\cdot3\cdot2\cdot346597=4897$, between $69^2$ and $70^2$. One failed square test clears both blocks. This product is entry 57 in the pre-existing, zero-indexed holdout sample. ## Which search intervals do the bounds cover?

Let $N=pq$ with $p<q$, and let $r$ be the nearest integer to the sixth root of $N$. The ordinary balanced search starts at $A=\lceil\sqrt{N/2}\rceil$, divides the interval into consecutive blocks of $2r$ integers and stops at $\lfloor\sqrt N\rfloor$. For a block with weights $u,v$, each possible integer label $z=ux+vy$ gives the test $\Delta=z^2-4uvN$. A surviving block in the table contains at least one label that passes the residue filter and has $\Delta>0$. Every counted block is complete and ends **strictly below** its stated cutoff. | Ordinary start $A$, cutoff $p$ | $4q\le5p$ | $>2^{654}$ | $>2^{635}$ |
| Restart for two 1,024-bit primes, cutoff $p$ | $4q\le5p$ and $400N\le289(2^{1024}-4)^2$ | $>2^{654}$ | $>2^{635}$ |

Knowing the primes have 1,024 bits allows a later start. With partner cap $D=2^{1024}-1$, the restart is $\max(A,2^{1023},\lceil N/D\rceil)$, and a new block begins there. The general proof applies to origins at most $0.85\sqrt N$ and cutoffs $C$ satisfying $4N\le5C^2$. On the fresh control, $q/p\approx1.03753$ and the restart is about $0.8807234\sqrt N$. Half of the proof's weight family still fits above this later origin, giving the last row. The ordinary before-$p$ row gives the fresh input its $>2^{635}$ distinct-test bound. The before-$p$ bounds use the ascending search that accepts a divisor inside the block being examined. ## Why so many tests remain

One direction already explains part of the cost. Near the square root the continued fraction chooses $u=v=1$, so the label is an ordinary sum. Across the overlapping ranges of these blocks, at least $2^{328}$ different labels in the class $1+N$ modulo 24 give different nonnegative discriminants. This holds for every 2,048-bit integer. The blocks occur near the end of the sweep, which is why a separate argument is needed to count the work before the smaller factor. [Single-direction proof with rounded endpoints](../PrimeFactors/QuotientStripTailSweep.lean)

Choose coprime integers $1\le i,j\le2^{327}$ and set $(u,v)=(4i+5j,3i+4j)$. This gives more than $2^{653}$ distinct reduced directions. Near $x=\sqrt{vN/u}$, the function $ux+vN/x$ reaches its minimum. On a short interval to its left, the chord approximation forces that same direction on every full block. Consecutive blocks' label ranges overlap and together cover two labels 24 apart, both above the minimum. Both pass the modulo-24 test because their residue is $u+vN$, realized by the local pair $(1,N)$. Each individual range is narrower than two, so the labels occupy different blocks. Each block has only one certified direction. [Direction count](../PrimeFactors/QuotientStripSlopePopulation.lean) · [Two different blocks per direction](../PrimeFactors/QuotientStripWideSlopeSeparation.lean) · [Direction and integer-label certificates](../PrimeFactors/QuotientStripForcedLabels.lean)

To count different square tests, fix $\Delta$. Every candidate label satisfies $z^2\equiv\Delta\pmod N$, with $0<z<N$. There are at most four roots modulo two distinct primes. Complementary roots $z$ and $N-z$ have opposite parity, and a label's parity must match $\Delta$, leaving at most two possible labels. For each such label, $uv=(z^2-\Delta)/(4N)$ is fixed. A separate count bounds how often different pairs of weights in the allowed rectangle give the same product. Combining these bounds gives the $2^{635}$ result after sharing. ## What the experiments measured

The first experiment accepts inputs coprime to six, which includes every product of two 1,024-bit primes. It ran complete searches on 99 ratio-selected products, 64 holdout products, and two larger controls. On the 41-bit control, switching from modulo 8 to the exact modulo-24 filter reduced integer square-root calls from 958 to 584 in a complete sweep and from 76 to 48 through the first factor. On the 46-bit control the corresponding counts fell from 2,881 to 1,720 and from 2,020 to 1,189. These are actual calls, including repeated discriminants. The experiment also measures the cost of updating a direction across neighboring blocks. Replay it with `python3 -m experiments.quotient_strip_farey`. [Work counters and inputs](../benchmarks/quotient-strip-farey.json) · [Farey direction updates](../PrimeFactors/QuotientStripFareyRuns.lean) · [Exact modulo-24 filter](../PrimeFactors/QuotientStripLevelMod24.lean)

The second experiment located **ten blocks per 2,048-bit input** directly from the product. It used five predefined weight pairs, chose two residue-compatible labels per pair and solved the real quadratic to locate each block. On the fresh product it used the ordinary start. On a separate product constructed with a power of five in the prime difference it used the equal-width restart. Each of the twenty blocks contained just one integer label, and all twenty discriminants were positive nonsquares. The certified factors confirmed afterward that each block ended before $p$. Replay it with `python3 -m experiments.quotient_strip_stationary`. [Selection rule and results](../benchmarks/quotient-strip-stationary.json) · [Product-only locator](../experiments/quotient_strip_stationary.py) · [Constructed input](../benchmarks/gap-stride.json)

The remaining question is whether information computed cheaply from $N$ can reject whole families of these positive discriminants at once, or select a block containing a genuine factor. The lower bound applies to the candidates left by the chord and modulo-24 tests. A stronger test, a different order of search, or a way to share work among unequal discriminants could change the computational cost. [Current search results](searching-the-balanced-interval.md)
