# Finding the closest square across billions of residue classes

On a 2,048-bit test, two lists of 65,536 integers sufficed to locate the best candidate among **4,294,967,296** classes and all their integer translates. The smallest remainder had 992 bits. It was smaller than anything found in 131,072 ordinary continued-fraction steps on the same product. We checked every combination of the 58 remainders in our chosen window. None had a square product, so they yielded no factor. On smaller inputs, the method found actual prime factors from the product alone. [Checked benchmarks](../benchmarks/crt-square-norms.json)

## Which squares are we looking for?

Start with an odd nonsquare product $N$. Choose the first $r$ odd primes $\ell$ for which $N$ is a nonzero square modulo $\ell$, checking divisibility as we go. Let $D$ be their product and $A=D^2$. For any nonnegative integer $t$ satisfying $t^2\equiv N\pmod A$, compute the signed integer $Q=(t^2-N)/A$. Then $(tD^{-1})^2\equiv Q\pmod N$. Several values of $Q$ whose product is a square can give two distinct square roots of the same residue modulo $N$. A greatest common divisor can then split $N$. This is the congruence-of-squares principle. The square leading coefficient in our quadratics is also classical. Our experiment selects the closest representatives across the complete family for one modulus and measures the resulting relations. [Pomerance, Smith and Tuler, §3](https://math.dartmouth.edu/~carlp/pipeline.pdf) · [Lean normalization and interval bounds](../PrimeFactors/CRTSquareNorm.lean)

For example, take $N=26{,}827$. The first two eligible primes are 3 and 11, so $D=33$. The entire window $|Q|\le\lfloor\sqrt N\rfloor$ contains two hits. For $t=85$ and 157 their quotients are $-18$ and $-2$. Their product is $36=6^2$. Since $\gcd(85\cdot157-6\cdot33^2,26827)=139$, the primes are 139 and 193. Keeping only the first hit leaves the product unresolved.

## How two short lists cover the whole search

At each $\ell$, lift a nonzero root $y$ to $y+\ell u$ modulo $\ell^2$. The correction is $u=((N-y^2)/\ell)(2y)^{-1}\bmod\ell$. The other root is its negative. Multiply one lifted root by the CRT coefficient that equals one modulo $\ell^2$ and zero at the other prime squares. The coefficients $c_i$ obtained this way give all the roots exactly once as $\sum_i\pm c_i\bmod A$.

Split the coefficients into two groups. Form the $L=2^{\lfloor r/2\rfloor}$ left sums and the $R=2^{\lceil r/2\rceil}$ right sums, reduced modulo $A$. For each left sum $u$, the sorted right list, repeated every $A$, indexes every number in that row as $u+v+kA$. The ranks on either side of the square root give the last representative at or below $\lfloor\sqrt N\rfloor$ and the first above it. On each side, the nearest nonnegative representative minimizes the absolute error because squaring is increasing there. Discard a negative predecessor. Taking the better of those neighbors in every row gives the exact global minimum. This is a meet-in-the-middle search with an ordered target. [Horowitz and Sahni](https://www.cise.ufl.edu/~sahni/papers/computingPartitions.pdf) · [Implementation and exhaustive small tests](../tests/test_crt_square_norms.py)

For a bound $Y$, the endpoints are $\lceil\sqrt{\max(0,N-AY)}\rceil$ and $\lfloor\sqrt{N+AY}\rfloor$. The difference between the two endpoint ranks counts the hits in this inclusive interval. Consecutive indices enumerate them. The implementation forms each sorted half-list by merging two cyclic shifts of the preceding list, so the work at successive levels grows geometrically and totals $O(L+R)$. As the sorted left sums increase, each of the three ranks (the two endpoints and the square-root threshold) only decreases. The left sums span less than one period, so each rank traverses at most $R$ right entries across the entire search. Finding the minimum and counting the full window therefore cost $O(L+R)$ integer operations after prime and CRT setup. Emitting $K$ hits adds $O(K)$ operations. List storage is $O(L+R)$ and retaining hits uses additional space. The cost of checking dependencies among retained rows is separate. If the cap cuts the list short, the run records the full geometric hit count and how many it retained.

There is also a sharp bound independent of the chosen modulus. For $0\le x\le y$ in the window, $(y-x)^2 N\le2(AY)^2$. Indeed, put $w=y-x$ and $B=AY$. Subtracting the endpoint inequalities gives $w^2+2wx\le2B$. Thus $wx\le B$, and $Nw^2\le x^2w^2+Bw^2\le2B^2$ using $(wx)^2\le Bwx$ and $Bw^2\le 2B^2-2Bwx$. Equality is possible when $x=0$, $y=2s$, $N=B=2s^2$. In particular, if $2Y^2<N$, there can be at most one nonnegative hit per class modulo $A$. [Lean proof](../PrimeFactors/CRTSquareNorm.lean)

## What we found from the product alone

On the project's independently sampled 63-bit input, $D=3\cdot5\cdot7\cdot11$ and $Y=\lfloor32\sqrt N\rfloor$ gave 512 hits. One quotient was $290358^2$, giving the primes 2,640,906,143 and 3,311,633,123. Halving the threshold gave 256 hits without a dependency. An earlier continued-fraction search used 767 steps. The two work units differ, and we explored several parameter settings on this input. Algebraically the positive square means that $D$ divided the distance from the midpoint to either prime. [Exact criterion and frequency](crt-square-gaps.md) · [Continued-fraction comparison](pell-cycles.md)

On the existing 79-bit product of independently sampled primes, we took the first twelve eligible primes and tried their triples in lexicographic order. With a cutoff of $\lfloor16\sqrt N\rfloor$, the eighth triple gave $D=3\cdot5\cdot53=795$. At $t=606{,}403{,}027{,}147$ the quotient was $1{,}693{,}882^2$, giving 605,056,390,957 and 607,749,663,337. The run generated 1,024 hits across eight windows and checked 1,001, including 489 positive square tests. Capping the search at 1,000 gave five trivial relations and no factor. The successful $t$ is 1,495,235 integers above the first possible Fermat midpoint.

A narrower window on the same product recovered a factor another way. With $Y=\lfloor4\sqrt N\rfloor$, the 220 triples produced 7,043 rows. A square relation joined 69 rows from 61 different moduli. The root of their product had 1,355 bits. This run found a split by combining rows after the individual square tests failed. We chose the settings after exploring this fixed product. The earlier continued-fraction method took 5,138 steps and used different arithmetic per step. [Benchmark parameters, controls and selected rows](../benchmarks/crt-square-norms.json)

On 64 fresh 79-bit products, the 1,001-candidate setting split none. The narrower window with pooled rows split 13, one of which the 5,138-step continued-fraction control missed. [All 64 outcomes and budgets](crt-holdout.md)

For the 2,048-bit run we fixed $r=32$ and $Y=\lfloor\sqrt N/2^{26}\rfloor$ before checking the outcome. We used the crossed product from the Dickson experiment. Its primes have existing certificates and come from separate constructions. The search received only its product and budgets. Screening 52 odd primes exhausted the required choices at 241. The modulus $D^2$ had 406 bits. [Source of the input](line-powers.md)

The comparison matches the number of continued-fraction steps to the total number of entries in the two lists. The lists also require ordered merges, moving through the lists and arithmetic on different-sized integers. For all 58 CRT rows, each absolute quotient had a nonsquare factor coprime to every other quotient. Even after appending all 131,072 continued-fraction rows and allowing arbitrary corrections supported on primes through $2^{24}$, every one of the 131,130 rows still had a private nonsquare factor. The smallest retained private factor had 889 bits in that combined calculation. This rules out a square product from any nonempty subset of **these rows** under those corrections.
