# Reading the factors from a multiplication circuit

Let the only input be a product $N=pq$ of two distinct primes. An arithmetic circuit that counts the satisfying assignments of a **tagged** multiplier can return the exact factor sum in one forward and one reverse traversal. Every gate in those traversals can operate on a three-element field. This removes the sequence of adaptive prefix questions used in an existing deterministic factoring construction. The cost of constructing and contracting the multiplier is still the main question.

## The polynomial to compute

Use a box $0\le x,y<B$ that includes both factors and excludes $N$. With a known factor width $b$, one can take $B=2^b$ provided $q<B\le N$. Impose $xy=N$ by local multiplication constraints with one internal wire assignment per accepted input pair. Attach a variable $z_i$ to bit $i$ of the **first** input when that bit is one. The resulting sum of products is
$$
P_N(z)=\sum_{xy=N,\ x,y<B}\ \prod_{i=0}^{b-1} z_i^{\mathrm{bit}_i(x)}.
$$
Only $(p,q)$ and $(q,p)$ contribute. At all tags equal to one, the value is always 2, but the partial derivative at tag $i$ is $p_i+q_i$. Its possible integer values 0, 1, 2 are distinct in the field of three elements. Multiply each canonical residue by $2^i$ and add over the **ordinary integers**. The result is $S=p+q$, and the factors are $(S\pm\sqrt{S^2-4N})/2$. [Box and digit proofs](../PrimeFactors/CountingReadout.lean) · [Marginal and full-width proofs](../PrimeFactors/BitMarginals.lean)

The construction also works without knowing either factor width. Use the bit length of $N$ for both inputs. The two additional witnesses are $(1,N)$ and $(N,1)$. Subtract the public bits of 1 and $N$ from each derivative in the field before reading its canonical residue. The same $0,1,2$ argument then recovers $S$. This works for every product of two distinct primes, regardless of the ratio of their sizes. The command given below exercises this case on the unbalanced product $51=3\cdot17$.

## The work needed for all derivatives

Suppose a division-free straight-line circuit with $L$ operations represents $P_N$ as a formal polynomial. Evaluate it once on unit tags, saving the forward values. Seed the output with weight 1 and walk the circuit backwards. An addition sends its weight to both operands. A product of $u,v$ with weight $w$ adds $wv$ to the weight of $u$ and $wu$ to the weight of $v$. Contributions to repeated operands accumulate. When the walk reaches the inputs, their weights are all the required derivatives. The arithmetic work is $O(L+b)$ and storing the gate records and weights takes $O(L+b)$ field elements and indices. This is the classical reverse differentiation principle applied to the two orientations of a factor pair. [Baur and Strassen](https://doi.org/10.1016/0304-3975(83)90110-X) · [First-order weighted counting](https://aclanthology.org/D09-1005/) · [Lean proof for circuits with shared gates](../PrimeFactors/ReverseSweep.lean)

Lean connects the reverse pass, the formal polynomial, and the exact integer sum. The theorem also accepts a known nonzero scalar multiple of the partition polynomial and divides out that scalar. Agreement only on field-valued points does not suffice for that proof. For instance, $X^3-X$ vanishes at every point in the three-element field while its derivative is $-1$. Likewise, an intermediate value that happens to vanish at unit tags can carry a nonzero derivative. [Circuit-to-sum theorem](../PrimeFactors/FactorCircuit.lean)

There are two other readouts. In characteristic two, a first reverse sweep finds a bit where $p$ and $q$ differ. Set its tag to zero and run the sweep again. The surviving orientation supplies all the other bits of one factor. Alternatively, a scalar counter can assign each digit contribution $c_i$ the ordinary weight $1+Tc_i$ modulo $T^2$, with $T=2B+1$. Products of those weights are $1+Tx$ modulo $T^2$ and the partition value is exactly $2+T(p+q)<T^2$. The latter identity works for positional digits of any radix and needs one modular count with a wider modulus. [Binary and ternary readouts](../PrimeFactors/BitMarginals.lean) · [Scalar readout](../PrimeFactors/CountingReadout.lean)

## What a small contraction actually costs

The experiment builds a Boolean schoolbook multiplier from AND gates, full adders and fixed product bits. It joins local constraint tables and sums out wires, sharing equal arithmetic gates. Its input is the product and a public width or balance bound. After one forward and one reverse pass modulo three, it checks the discriminant and product. The tests separately ask Lean to check the prime factors of each successful fixture. [Multiplier and elimination order](../experiments/bit_marginals.py) · [Decimal fixtures](../benchmarks/bit-marginals.json) · [Independent checks](../tests/test_bit_marginals.py)

For 143 the bit counts, starting at the least significant bit, are $2,1,1,2$, giving $2+2+4+16=24$. The counted joins include work already spent before the small output circuit is available. Among the negative controls, 36 has five accepted assignments in a four-bit box. Its assignment count reduces to two modulo three, yet the final discriminant check rejects the proposed sum. Run the fixtures with the following command.

~~~sh
python3 experiments/bit_marginals.py --fixtures
python3 experiments/bit_marginals.py 51 --full-width
~~~

There is a precise obstruction to compressing a partial multiplication constraint. Consider the table with all first-input bits on one side and all second-input bits on the other. For odd $N$, the relation $xy=N\bmod 2^k$ on the odd residues is a permutation: each $x$ has the unique partner $Nx^{-1}$. Its matrix over the three-element field has rank $2^{k-1}$ for $k\ge1$. Expressing it as $\sum_{j=1}^r f_j(x)g_j(y)$ therefore requires at least that many terms. After imposing the **entire** product in the semiprime box, the rank drops to exactly two. This lower bound applies to the indicated split of the partial constraint. Other variable orders and circuits that combine low and high product bits can behave differently. [Lean rank and separation bounds](../PrimeFactors/LowProductRank.lean)

## How this relates to deterministic subexponential factoring

The follow-up derives a subexponential tagged-circuit construction on paper and a polynomial-space contraction for the divisor sum. A separate unweighted construction needs only a quarter-width output for distinct semiprimes. [Circuit and tape costs](tagged-contraction-cost.md) · [Quarter-width count](quarter-width-count.md)

Isaac Rudich and Louis-Martin Rousseau published a Lean construction of a single deterministic machine that, for every fixed $k\ge1$, finds the smallest prime factor on $n$-bit inputs within $C_k 2^{\lfloor n/k\rfloor}$ steps. Their driver determines the factor by a sequence of divisor-prefix counts. Their multiplication network gives each accepted input a unique filling, and its first-factor input gates already store the digits needed to attach the tags. The ring-valued grouping and single-level transformations can express polynomial weights. This project inspected their Apache-2.0 sources at revision `c91cc3f` and independently wrote its readout proofs against the local Lean toolchain. The upstream theorem is attributed to its authors and has not been rebuilt here. [Paper](https://hexagonmath.org/2610.00600v1) · [Pinned repository and license](https://github.com/IsaacRudich/SubexponentialDeterministicFactoring/tree/c91cc3f4947caca8977e81a5d14244f73b5ae2b1) · [Unique fillings and divisor count](https://github.com/IsaacRudich/SubexponentialDeterministicFactoring/blob/c91cc3f4947caca8977e81a5d14244f73b5ae2b1/FactoringAux/MulCount.lean)

Two implementation details matter for using that result. First, the compiled driver returns a prefix decision after a modular sweep. Its recursive transformations compute a known integer multiplier $D$ times the network value. If $3\mid D$, the scaled output can lose every derivative in characteristic three. One can use any prime characteristic greater than two coprime to $D$ and divide by $D$ there, or use a contraction that preserves the unscaled polynomial. Second, arithmetic gates must express the tagged polynomial or its coordinate slices. Running the published decision machine on a scalar input gives neither a derivative tape nor the missing representation proof. A size bound for a compiled tagged circuit, together with the cost of storing and addressing its tape, is required to translate the readout into a machine-time bound. [Ring-valued level identity](https://github.com/IsaacRudich/SubexponentialDeterministicFactoring/blob/c91cc3f4947caca8977e81a5d14244f73b5ae2b1/FactoringAux/Level.lean) · [Recursive scaling factor](https://github.com/IsaacRudich/SubexponentialDeterministicFactoring/blob/c91cc3f4947caca8977e81a5d14244f73b5ae2b1/FactoringAux/EngineGroup.lean) · [Compiled driver's contract](https://github.com/IsaacRudich/SubexponentialDeterministicFactoring/blob/c91cc3f4947caca8977e81a5d14244f73b5ae2b1/FactoringAux/UniValSpec.lean)

At 2048 input bits the pinned uniform construction selects **zero** recursive acceleration levels. It selects the first positive level only when the input length is at least $(9\cdot48^{16})^{40}$, an integer with 1115 decimal digits. The multiplication representation already uses eight 128-bit digits per input gate at that width. An unconstrained input gate thus has $2^{1024}$ possible settings. This is a table-size illustration, and a different implicit representation can avoid materializing that table. The published asymptotic theorem supplies no practical runtime bound at 2048 bits.
