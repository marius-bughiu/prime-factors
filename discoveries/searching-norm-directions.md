# Finding factors from two points on an ellipse

The product alone lets us choose a short list of equations. For almost every product of two random 1,024-bit primes, the list contains two solutions whose coordinates reveal the factors. The probability that the list misses is below $2^{-119}$ for the population defined below. **Finding those two solutions is still expensive.** Our complete search reduces the exponent of a direct coordinate scan from $1/2$ to $1/3$. At 2,048 bits even one full ellipse can take on the order of $2^{683}$ blocks.

For a small example, $65=1^2+8^2=4^2+7^2$. Cross-multiplying the coordinates gives $4\cdot8=32$ and $1\cdot7=7$. The difference gives $\gcd(32-7,65)=5$ and the sum gives $\gcd(32+7,65)=13$. The research question is how to locate such pairs for a product whose factors we do not know. [Lean example and factor theorem](../PrimeFactors/NormDirections.lean)

## What the search needs

Let $N=pq$ for distinct odd primes. Choose a positive coefficient $d$ coprime to $N$ and look for positive integer coordinates satisfying $t^2+d a^2=kN$ for a small positive integer $k$. If $a$ is invertible modulo $N$, the fraction $t/a$ gives a square root of $-d$ modulo $N$. Two solutions can use different $k$. If their roots differ even after allowing a simultaneous sign reversal, the roots agree up to one sign at $p$ and the opposite sign at $q$. The gcd of their difference with $N$ gives a factor. Equivalently, one can use the difference of their integer cross products.

To prove the search complete for a chosen $d$, suppose $-d$ is a square modulo both primes. CRT gives four roots modulo $N$, in two pairs $\{z,-z\}$. Write $s=\lceil\sqrt d\rceil$ and $L=\lfloor\sqrt{N/s}\rfloor$, with $1\le s\le N$. For each root, Dirichlet approximation gives $1\le a\le L$ and an integer $t\equiv az\pmod N$ with $|t|(L+1)\le N$. Then $t^2<sN$, $da^2\le sN$ and $N\mid t^2+da^2$. Thus each root pair has a representative on one of the ellipses
$$t^2+da^2=kN,\qquad 1\le k<2s.$$
For $N>1$, $a<N$ and $t\ne0$ because $z$ is invertible. If $a$ shares a divisor with $N$, its gcd gives a proper factor immediately. Otherwise replacing $t$ by $|t|$ preserves the root up to sign. One need store only the first root and compare subsequent roots with it and its negative. [Lean proof of the multiplier bound and gcd](../PrimeFactors/NormDirections.lean)

There is a sharper cutoff. The lattice $\{(t,a): t\equiv az\pmod N\}$ has determinant $N$. Reduce its basis in the squared length $t^2+da^2$. The first reduced vector has length squared $kN$ with $3k^2\le4d$. Indeed, if the squared lengths of the two reduced vectors are $V\le W$ and their weighted inner product is $u$, reduction gives $2|u|\le V$. The determinant identity $VW-u^2=dN^2$ gives $3V^2\le4dN^2$. Subtracting the nearest integer multiple of the first vector and swapping when the second becomes shorter reaches such a basis, since each swap decreases a positive integer length. At the target widths $d\ll N$, so the bound excludes vectors on either coordinate axis and gives $|a|<N$. At these widths $k<\min(p,q)$ as well. If either prime divided $a$, it would divide $t$ and its square would divide $kN$, which is impossible. The same reasoning makes $t$ a unit because $d$ is coprime to $N$. The implementation uses this shorter multiplier range. The Lean module checks the reduced-basis inequality. [Formalized bounds](../PrimeFactors/NormDirections.lean)

## How the product chooses the coefficients

Take $m$ small primes $\ell_1,\ldots,\ell_m$, including 2. If one divides $N$ the list already gives a factor. For any unit $u$, write $v(u)$ for the two Legendre signs at $p$ and $q$, recording $+1$ as 0 and $-1$ as 1. Multiplication adds these two-bit vectors modulo two. We seek $v(d)=v(-1)$, since this is exactly when $-d$ is a square at both primes. The Jacobi symbol $(u/N)$, computable from the product, gives the parity of the two bits.

Let $J=(-1/N)$. If one of the primes has Jacobi sign $-1$, choose the first such prime $r$ as a pivot.

- Start with $1$ if $J=+1$, or with $r$ if $J=-1$.
- For every other prime $\ell$, include $\ell$ if $(\ell/N)=J$. Otherwise include $r\ell$.

Every entry has the desired parity. Subtracting the starting vector from each other entry produces exactly $v(\ell)$ or $v(\ell)+v(r)$, whichever has even parity. Each difference is either $00$ or $11$. These differences span the entire even-parity part of the original span: one can cancel the odd-parity contributions in any sum by adding the pivot to each odd generator. This part has dimension at most one. If a nonzero difference is attainable, one listed entry already realizes it relative to the start. Thus the target appears precisely when it is in the span of the original prime vectors.

If there is no pivot, all the available vectors are $00$ or $11$. With $J=-1$ the target is unreachable. With $J=+1$ use $1$ and every individual prime. This also reaches every vector in the span. The list has $m$ entries with a pivot and at most $m+1$ in total. With all 131 primes through 739, $d\le733\cdot739=541687$. The reduced-basis bound leaves at most 849 multipliers for any one coefficient. The implementation also discards $k$ if $kN$ cannot equal a square plus $d$ times a square modulo 8, or if it is a quadratic nonresidue modulo an odd prime dividing $d$. Both tests are necessary conditions for an integer point. ## How often the list works at 2,048 bits

Choose distinct primes uniformly from $[X,2X)$, $X=2^{1023}$, conditioned on $pq\ge2X^2$. This gives two 1,024-bit factors and a 2,048-bit product. Ordered and unordered pairs have the same failure proportion. The following upper bound is **unconditional** and concerns existence somewhere on the listed ellipses.

If the target is outside the span, there is a nonzero linear functional on the two-bit vectors which vanishes on all the generators and takes value one on the target. There are only three such functionals. They read the first bit, the second bit, or the sum of the two. For the first two cases one of $p,q$ must have positive signs at all $m$ generators and negative sign at $-1$. In the third case $p,q$ must have matching signs at all generators and opposite signs at $-1$.

Put $M=4\prod_{i=1}^m\ell_i$. Among units modulo $M$, the $m+1$ signs of $-1,2$ and the odd $\ell_i$ take every pattern equally often. Indeed the first two signs distinguish the four odd residues modulo 8. Once that residue is fixed, quadratic reciprocity lets us set each remaining sign independently by choosing a quadratic residue or nonresidue modulo the corresponding odd prime. Each pattern therefore occupies $C=\varphi(M)/2^{m+1}$ residue classes. For any fixed first prime the required partner pattern in the third case occupies at most $T=C\lceil X/M\rceil$ integers of $[X,2X)$. For the first two cases fix the unrestricted prime and use the same bound for the restricted one.

Write $U=X/\log X$. Explicit prime-count bounds give fewer than $1.511U$ choices for an unrestricted prime. The subinterval $[\sqrt2X,2X)$ contributes more than $(0.22U)^2$ eligible ordered pairs of distinct primes. Dividing the union bound $3(1.511U)T$ by this denominator and using $\log X<710$ gives
$$\Pr(\text{miss})\le \frac{3(1.511)(710)}{0.22^2}\frac{\varphi(M)}{2^{m+1}X}\left\lceil\frac{X}{M}\right\rceil.$$
With $m=131$, $M$ has 1,021 bits and $\lceil X/M\rceil=6$. Exact rational substitution gives a bound below $2^{-119}$ (approximately $1.078\cdot10^{-36}$ rounded upward). This argument counts every integer in the required residue classes, so it needs no equidistribution assumption at this width. [Rosser and Schoenfeld, Corollary 1](https://doi.org/10.1215/ijm/1255631807) · [Explicit prime-pair denominator](stride-budget.md#an-explicit-bound-at-exactly-2048-bits) · [Executable rational bound](../experiments/norm_directions.py)

## The limiting frequency and an all-input guarantee

For a fixed list, the prime number theorem in progressions makes the complete sign patterns of $p,q$ independent and uniform as their common bit width grows. The full-width product restriction preserves the limit. Partition the fixed scaled square $[1,2]^2$ into a grid, apply the theorem on each rectangle and then shrink the rectangles meeting $pq/X^2=2$. The diagonal contributes a vanishing proportion. Consequently the $m$ prime vectors and the target are independent uniform two-bit vectors in the limit.

Conditional on the public Jacobi signs, the limiting miss probabilities are:

| Public information | Limiting miss probability |
|---|---:|
| A pivot exists | $2^{-m}$ |
| No pivot, $J=-1$ | $1$ |
| No pivot, $J=+1$ | $2^{-(m+1)}$ |

With a pivot, the generators span only a line if the first prime's $m$ bits are either all zero or exactly the public parity vector. These are two of $2^m$ possibilities. Only then can the target miss, with probability $1/2$ conditional on its parity. With no pivot, the diagonal line is the only possible nonzero direction. When $J=+1$, the span misses the target only when all generators vanish and the target is $11$. Averaging the table gives $(3/2)2^{-m}-(3/4)2^{-2m}$. The finite bound also allows a growing list without using a prime number theorem at growing moduli. Taking every prime up to $c\log X$ for fixed $0<c<1$ gives $M=X^{c+o(1)}$ and $m\sim c\log X/\log\log X$. The same integer count bounds failure by $O((\log X)2^{-m})$.

The Dirichlet GRH from the 2026 Lean release gives a stronger guarantee for every target semiprime. The map from units modulo $N$ to the two Legendre bits is onto by CRT. Each of the three functionals above therefore has a proper index-two kernel. Lamzouri, Li and Soundararajan show that for $N\ge3000$ with no prime divisor below $(\log N)^2$, every proper subgroup omits some prime at most $(\log N)^2$. The two 1,024-bit prime factors satisfy that hypothesis. Supplying every prime up to this cutoff makes the generator span the full two-bit space. It suffices at 2,048 bits to use the universal integer cutoff $\lceil(2048\log2)^2\rceil=2,015,166$. Selection takes a polynomial number of operations in the bit length, and every selected $d$ is at most the square of the prime cutoff. The analytic theorem enters this argument on paper. [Lamzouri, Li and Soundararajan, Theorem 1.1(2)](https://arxiv.org/html/1309.3595v3#S1.SS1) · [Independent verification of the Dirichlet statements](https://math.mit.edu/~drew/grh/)

## What knowing the factors would buy

On a covered input, recovering the factors and finding a revealing pair of solutions reduce to one another. Given the pair, one gcd recovers a factor in polynomial time. Conversely, given $p,q$, evaluate both Legendre symbols for each of at most 132 coefficients to select a suitable $d$. Compute a square root of $-d$ at each prime by Tonelli–Shanks. Its auxiliary nonresidue can be found in two random nonzero trials on average, since exactly half the units at an odd prime are nonresidues. Combine the roots by CRT, holding the sign at $p$ fixed and taking each sign at $q$. Reduce the associated two-dimensional lattices as above. This produces the two required solutions in expected time polynomial in the bit length. The point of the coverage proof is to identify equations where the search will succeed if completed. The hard step from the product alone is locating a revealing pair. [Prime square-root complexity](https://arxiv.org/abs/1105.1456) · [Two-dimensional lattice reduction](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/019667749190033U)

## Cost of locating the solutions

A coordinate-by-coordinate search on even $x^2+y^2=N$ uses order $\sqrt N$ positions. The implementation tests groups of positions together. For $T=kN$ it writes each ellipse as $Ax^2+By^2=T$ in two ways, using $(A,B)=(1,d)$ and $(d,1)$. In each chart it scans $Ax^2\le T/2$. The two charts cover all positive solutions, including the boundary. Let $f(x)=\sqrt{(T-Ax^2)/B}$. On this arc,
$$|f'|\le\sqrt{A/B},\qquad |f''|=\frac{AT}{\sqrt B(T-Ax^2)^{3/2}}\le\frac{3A}{\sqrt{BT}}.$$
Use blocks of $W=\max(1,\lfloor(\sqrt{BT}/(8A))^{1/3}\rfloor)$ consecutive integer positions. At the left endpoint approximate a rational lower bound $u$ on the slope magnitude by $h/v$ with $1\le v\le W$ and $|h-vu|\le 1/W$. For $x=x_0+j$ in a block the integer $vy+hj$ stays in an interval of constant length. The slope error contributes at most one, and the curvature contributes $O(AW^3/\sqrt{BT})=O(1)$ for $W>1$. A width-one block has zero displacement.

The implementation forms rational bounds from the two endpoint heights rounded down to multiples of $1/S$, where $S=4(A+B+1)W^2$. The magnitude of the secant slope from the left endpoint increases along this concave arc. Its endpoint value is $A(x_0+x_1)/(B(f(x_0)+f(x_1)))$. Rounding the denominator down bounds this slope from above. Using $f(x_0)$ rounded up in the left derivative bounds it from below. On the target ellipses the heights in each chart are at least one. The extra slope enclosure is therefore $O(\sqrt{A/B}/S)$. After multiplying by $vW$ this adds $O(1)$ to the intercept interval. The rounded left height adds at most $v/S$. For each integer intercept an exact quadratic discriminant, perfect-square test and divisibility check recover every integer solution on that line. [Integer arc and line tests](../experiments/norm_directions.py) · [Lean proofs of slope, intercept and discriminant bounds](../PrimeFactors/NormArc.lean)

Both charts together need $O(1+T^{1/3}/(AB)^{1/6})$ blocks. This follows by dividing their respective coordinate lengths $O(\sqrt{T/A})$ by their respective block widths. For $k<2\lceil\sqrt d\rceil$ this is $O(1+N^{1/3})$ per ellipse. Summing over the multipliers and coefficients gives $O(N^{1/3}\sum_d\lceil\sqrt d\rceil)$ blocks, with polynomial bit cost per block for the coefficient ranges above. Even for $k=d=1$ a full chart has order $N^{1/3}$ blocks. Harvey and Hittmeir already give a stronger general deterministic factorization bound of $N^{1/5+o(1)}$ bit operations. Progress toward practical 2,048-bit recovery here would require a faster way to locate one point from each pair of opposite roots. [Harvey and Hittmeir](https://arxiv.org/abs/2105.11105)

The product-only search split $3599=59\cdot61$ using two different multiples. The equations are $57^2+14\cdot5^2=3599$ and $32^2+14\cdot21^2=2\cdot3599$. It eliminated three multipliers by congruences, visited three ellipses, and tested eight candidate lines in 73 blocks. For $1{,}900{,}014{,}700{,}027=1{,}000{,}003\cdot1{,}900{,}009$, it collected two solutions at $d=2$, covering 657,200 coordinate positions in 10,600 blocks with 4,522 line tests. On the 2,048-bit control the budget of 128 blocks expired with zero solutions and the full cofactor unresolved. [Inputs, work counts and results](../benchmarks/norm-directions.json)
