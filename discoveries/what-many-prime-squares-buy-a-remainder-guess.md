# What many prime squares buy a remainder guess

If two unknown factors multiply to 1 modulo some modulus, how many guesses does it take to name their **sum remainder**? Give each possible ordered pair of factor remainders equal weight. A guess counts once, and the correct guess counts too. A guess of a remainder can identify a whole group of possible sums. Finding which integer in that group is the true sum is a separate problem.

For modulus **7,239,457,225**, there are **41,767,264** possible sum remainders. Guessing them in a uniformly random order takes **20,883,632.5** guesses on average. The best order takes **470,695,147/48**, about **9.81 million**, a **53.04% saving**. The modulus is the product of the squares of 5, 7, 11, 13 and 17. Lean checks the optimum on the actual sum remainders modulo that one number. [Exact minimum, support and rank mass](../PrimeFactors/FiveSquareGuessRanks.lean)

| 5² | 7 | 20 | 13/4 | 4 |
| 5², 7² | 112 | 840 | 112/3 | 113/2 |
| Add 11² | 5,152 | 92,400 | 44,521/30 | 5,153/2 |

For the first three rows we also enumerated every possible first factor remainder and its inverse and counted the sums directly. For five squares there are only 32 groups to count by frequency. A real semiprime can share the stated product remainder. We deliberately selected $139{,}649{,}129{,}870{,}251=11{,}809{,}331\times11{,}825{,}321$ with that property and close factors. Its Fermat benchmark takes three midpoints and checks the factorization in Lean. This particular input supplies no estimate of the probability of a sum for actual prime products. ## Can the relative saving keep growing?

Yes in this model. **For every positive fraction, there is a modulus for which the mean number of guesses is below that fraction of the random-order mean.** The witness modulus uses distinct prime squares and the fixed product remainder 1. Both the number of labels and the number of required guesses can grow. [Statement on actual modular sum labels](../PrimeFactors/ArbitrarilyBiasedSumGuesses.lean)

The reason can be seen one prime at a time. At each odd prime square $p^2$, there are two particularly frequent sums with $p$ ordered pairs each. Every other attainable sum has just two. The Chinese remainder theorem makes the joint multiplicities multiply. Guessing in descending order of these weights minimizes the mean. [Local weights and their effect](../PrimeFactors/PrimeSquareGuessAffinity.lean) · [Transferring the weights to one modulus](../PrimeFactors/CoprimeGuessTables.lean)

For a table with $A$ labels and normalized probabilities $P_s$, put $F=(\sum_s\sqrt{P_s})^2/A$. A uniform table has $F=1$. For independent tables the values of $F$ multiply. A classical guesswork inequality says that twice the optimal mean is at most $1+AF$. Lean checks the finite version used here. For every prime $p\ge11$, our local $F$ is at most $1-1/(2p)$. The sum of reciprocal primes diverges, so multiplying enough of these local factors makes their product arbitrarily small. The deduction uses unconditional elementary number theory formalized in Mathlib. [Finite probability inequality](../PrimeFactors/IndependentGuessRanks.lean) · [Sason and Verdú, Theorem 5(a), equation 73](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1801.01265) · [Product bound](../PrimeFactors/PrimeGuessProducts.lean)

The conclusion also holds for **every fixed positive integer $n$**, even when the selected primes must exceed a given cutoff. Prime numbers congruent to 1 modulo $8n$ make $n$ a nonzero square. Their reciprocals have divergent sum, so a finite selection can again make the relative mean as small as desired. Lean proves this using quadratic reciprocity and Mathlib’s theorem on primes in arithmetic progressions. These primes can be larger than $n$. The experiment being predicted still draws a random ordered *residue pair* with product congruent to $n$. [Unconditional supply of splitting primes](../PrimeFactors/SplittingGuessPrimes.lean) · [Result for any fixed positive product](../PrimeFactors/FixedProductSumGuesses.lean)

## What remains expensive?

For distinct prime squares and a fixed unit product that is square at every prime $p$, define $H=\prod_p(p-1)$. No single sum label has probability above $1/H$. Even the best list needs at least $\lceil H/2\rceil$ labels to cover half the pairs. Every possible order has mean rank at least $(H+1)/2$. For the five primes above, $H=46{,}080$, so even half coverage takes at least 23,040 labels. As more primes enter the modulus, this absolute lower bound increases. The apparent improvement against random order reflects how quickly that comparison grows. [Absolute bounds for lists and guessing orders](../PrimeFactors/JointSumGuessFloor.lean)

The model takes a random pair of factor **remainders** as its sample. These results supply neither the true remainder of a given semiprime nor an inexpensive test for a proposed remainder. For actual 2,048-bit prime products the project has a separate guarantee on how many exact square tests an elementary search saves, and a lower bound on the work it still has to do. ## Do the same guesses work for actual primes?

Consider all products of two **distinct 1,024-bit primes** that have 2,048 bits and leave remainder **one modulo 7,239,457,225**. Give each eligible product equal weight. Under a published Dirichlet-GRH prime-count estimate, the best fixed order for guessing the remainder of the sum takes **9,806,148.896 guesses on average**, within 0.001 of the exact modular value $470695147/48$. The random-order mean is exactly 20,883,632.5. The comparison concerns the sum remainder across this specified population. Testing whether a particular guess is correct and recovering an integer factor are separate problems. [Bound for every order and the optimum](../PrimeFactors/FiveSquareActualRanks.lean)

To count the products, take an ordered pair of unit remainders $(a,b)$ with $ab=1$ modulo the chosen modulus. Count the distinct primes $p,q$ with these remainders, both of the required width, and $pq$ above the full-width threshold. The prime-count estimate bounds the ratio of the largest of these counts to the smallest by $1+2^{-380}$. Giving the pair $(q,p)$ the same rank as $(p,q)$ then counts each product twice, so the same ratio controls the mean over distinct products. The bound applies to **every fixed ordering**, including choices that perform poorly. Lean takes the published inequalities and the logarithmic-integral endpoint identity as hypotheses and checks the rest of the deduction. [Residue-cell counts](../PrimeFactors/FiveSquarePrimeSupply.lean) · [From ordered pairs to distinct products](../PrimeFactors/PrimeResidueRankTransfer.lean) · [Ernvall-Hytönen and Palojärvi, Corollary 2](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2003.01925#page=2) · [Verification of the upstream Dirichlet-GRH statement](https://math.mit.edu/~drew/shortgrh/)

All **32 frequency groups keep their order** on this full-size population. For example, every sum remainder with 280 modular pairs occurs more often than every remainder with 272. Those two groups realize the smallest ratio between unequal weights, $280/272=35/34$. Cell counts differing by a factor strictly below $35/34$ already suffice to preserve the order. Labels tied in the modular model may differ in frequency on the prime population. [Exact groups and ordering theorem](../PrimeFactors/RankWeightSeparation.lean)

At smaller widths the picture changes. We enumerated every distinct prime pair of each width, requiring a full-width product and the same product congruence. For 22-bit primes there were **zero** eligible products. For 23-bit primes there were **six**, occupying five weight groups. For 24-bit primes there were **23**, occupying thirteen groups. Among the last 23 we deliberately selected the pair requiring the fewest Fermat midpoints, $15{,}385{,}987$ and $15{,}456{,}673$. The checked factorization took 41 midpoints. These complete small censuses illustrate why a finite modular table alone cannot establish frequencies for actual primes. ## What if the modulus must fit a 2,048-bit budget?

Suppose the product of the chosen distinct odd prime squares is at most $2^{2048}$ and none of the primes divides the input. Fix any product remainder and sample uniformly over the ordered factor remainders with that product. The average cost of **every** sum-guess order exceeds **7.581413% of the random-order mean**. A reduction by a factor of 14 is impossible in this model. The earlier guarantee of an arbitrarily small fraction allowed the modulus to keep growing. [Bound on the mean at a finite modulus budget](../PrimeFactors/BudgetedSumGuesses.lean)

The same argument bounds the length of a list. To include the correct sum remainder for **at least 99.9%** of the possible factor pairs, **more than 98.68%** of all attainable sum remainders must appear on the list. Choosing the modulus and the list with knowledge of the fixed product remainder is allowed. A probability statement about the true factors of a particular integer would require additional information.

For the example with five prime squares and product remainder one, the minimum list length is exactly **41,644,742 of 41,767,264** sum remainders, or **99.7067%**. The least frequent labels have 32 ordered factor pairs each. There are enough such labels to drop 122,522 of them, but dropping one more would exceed the allowed failure rate. The same table that cuts the *mean* number of guesses by 53.04% requires a nearly complete list to reach this success rate. [Sharp minimum and witness](../PrimeFactors/FiveSquareCoverageTail.lean) · [Exact small-table reproductions](../experiments/joint_sum_guess_counts.py)

For the proof, at any prime $p\ge5$ each attainable sum has at least $(p^2-3p+4)/(p(p-1))$ of the probability it would have if the sum remainders themselves were uniform. At $p=3$ the attained sums are uniform for every unit product. The fractions multiply under the Chinese remainder theorem. The smallest product of these guaranteed fractions within the budget is attained at the 129 primes from 5 through 739 and lies between 0.07581413 and 0.07581415. Their squares multiply to a 2,032-bit number. The next prime, 743, would take that choice to 2,051 bits. This is the exact minimum of the probability bound. The best achievable mean rank can be higher. [Local and joint bounds](../PrimeFactors/PrimeSquareUniformFloor.lean) · [Finite optimization and checked integers](../PrimeFactors/GuessModulusBudget.lean)
