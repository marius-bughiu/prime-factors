# How often can a smooth reduced order expose a large prime?

The search that split our constructed 2,048-bit product in under a second depends on the orders of modular powers. Choose two distinct 1,024-bit primes uniformly, conditioned on a 2,048-bit product. The probability of finding a proper divisor during a power sweep using primes up to 1,000 is **below one in $2^{677}$** per uniformly chosen surviving group element. The estimate even allows the best of four choices involving $p-1$, $p+1$, $q-1$ and $q+1$ for each product. It includes accidental successes when the whole reduced group size has a large prime divisor. The randomness here is over the product and the group element. The earlier full-size success used primes chosen specifically to make the search work. ## Counting the possible orders

For one choice of signs, put $P=p\pm1$, $Q=q\pm1$, $g=\gcd(P,Q)$ and $r=P/g$. After the public first power, the local cyclic group at $p$ has size $r$. If $E$ is the product of all subsequent exponents, a uniform local element becomes the identity with probability $\gcd(r,E)/r$. To see it, write a cyclic group element as a generator raised to an exponent $x$ chosen uniformly modulo $r$. The condition is $r\mid Ex$, which has exactly $\gcd(r,E)$ solutions. A factor from a checkpoint requires the identity at one of the two primes. A uniform global element conditioned on surviving the initial projection increases the bound by at most two when the signed sizes differ. Equal signed sizes leave no surviving element and require twin primes. [Projection, group sizes and survival](jacobi-separation.md) · [Local probability](../PrimeFactors/SmoothSignedPairs.lean)

Write $d=\gcd(r,E)$ and $k=r/d$. The local success probability is $1/k$. The number $d$ has only prime divisors up to 1,000. Let $U=2^{1024}$ and let $S$ be the number of such positive integers at most $U$. Fix $Q$. The triple $(g,d,k)$ determines $P=gdk$. There are at most $\tau(Q)$ possible $g$, one for each divisor of $Q$, and at most $S$ possible $d$. Summing $1/k$ over the possible $k$ contributes at most the harmonic sum $H_U=1+1/2+\cdots+1/U$. Summing over $Q$ adds at most $U H_U$ because each possible divisor $i$ occurs $\lfloor U/i\rfloor$ times. Hence the sum of local success probabilities over **all integer group sizes** $1\le P,Q\le U$ is at most $US H_U^2$. Restricting to actual shifted primes can only reduce that sum. This step allows arbitrarily large common divisors $g$. [Finite encoding and divisor-sum proof](../PrimeFactors/SmoothSignedPairs.lean)

The finite Euler-product estimate gives $S<2^{297}$. It uses exponent $5/32$ and checks outward rational bounds for each of the 168 primes through 1,000. Also $H_U\le1+\log U<711$. We count four choices of signs, either prime as the one where the power vanishes, and the factor of two for a surviving base. This gives $16US H_U^2$ before dividing by the number of eligible prime pairs. The population contains at least $2^{2021}$ unordered pairs by the explicit prime-count inequalities of Rosser and Schoenfeld. The Lean population theorem takes this last numerical lower bound as a hypothesis. Together these give the stated $2^{-677}$ bound. [Smooth-number certificate](../PrimeFactors/BoundedSmoothCount.lean) · [Population estimate](stride-budget.md#an-explicit-bound-at-exactly-2048-bits) · [Rosser and Schoenfeld, Corollary 1](https://doi.org/10.1215/ijm/1255631807)

An exponent whose largest prime divisor is 1,000 or less cannot improve on the full smooth schedule at this cutoff by increasing its powers. Every possible local order already divides a number at most $U$. The union bound puts even a trillion such uniform trials below $2^{-637}$ averaged over eligible prime pairs. This estimate concerns factor readouts from the power sweep. The benchmark's deterministic choice of an integer base or seed has a different distribution. ## How common is the pattern we deliberately built?

Fewer than one in $2^{689}$ eligible products have even one *entire* reduced group size with only prime divisors up to 1,000, across all four sign choices. For a fixed $Q$ such a $P$ is determined by $g\mid Q$ and a smooth $r$ with $P=gr$. Both group sizes are even, so we can halve them before counting. Each unordered pair of primes is counted with the smaller prime first. Here the randomness comes only from choosing the input product. This bound quantifies the rarity of the pattern used in our fast constructed example. [Structural count and its connection to the exponent](../PrimeFactors/SmoothSignedStructure.lean)

A particular element can get lucky outside that family. For $1{,}577=19\times83$, every signed pair of reduced group sizes contains two numbers with odd prime factors. A chosen quadratic-group element nevertheless reveals 19 in one doubling after the public first power. Lean checks both the group-size statement and the actual gcd. [Checked example at cutoff two](../PrimeFactors/SmoothSignedStructure.lean)

For the ordinary predecessor method, there is also a bound for deterministic starting values. Even using every integer from 2 through $2^{21}$ and any 1,000-smooth exponent can reach fewer than one in $2^{177}$ eligible products.
