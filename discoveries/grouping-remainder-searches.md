# What happens if we stop choosing remainders early?

On 502,005,281,288,939, the previous search attempted to reconstruct a factor test from each of 1,368,000 combinations of remainders. We can stop two primes earlier and group them into 4,800 lattices. Checking the points in those lattices recovered **17,838,631 × 28,141,469**. An exhaustive run took 0.59 seconds locally, compared with 7.39 seconds for the previous complete reconstruction. An earlier weighted-sum search completed all its groups on this same input in 0.0062 seconds. These are separate measurements of three algorithms. [New checked fixture](../benchmarks/offset-lattice-batches.json) · [Earlier comparison](../research/midpoint-weight-holdout.json)

When both searches stopped as soon as they found a factor, the old method tried 1,173,282 combinations and took 6.49 seconds. The two-prime grouping reached that same factor in its 4,117th group after 918,597 point visits and 0.52 seconds. These are medians of three locally timed runs in rotating order, with primality certification timed separately. Omitting `--exhaust` in the single-input commands reproduces the stopping rule.

The grouping preserves every successful test for two distinct 1,024-bit primes. A separate result tells us why merely choosing the next remainder more cleverly cannot make the old method practical. Suppose the search keeps every slope allowed by these square and factor-sum tests at primes coprime to the input. If every branch continues to the reconstruction threshold, a complete search takes **more than $2^{889}$ branches** on every nonsquare 2,048-bit target. This holds even when we allow prime powers and choose a different next test on each branch. On our two full-size comparisons, eight lattice groups each yielded no factor. [Coverage and work proof](../PrimeFactors/OffsetLatticeBatches.lean) · [Adaptive lower bound](../PrimeFactors/OffsetAdaptiveFloor.lean)

## Recovering all candidates in one group

Given only $N$, compute $T=\lfloor\sqrt N\rfloor$, $h=N-T^2$, $J=\lfloor\sqrt{4h/3}\rfloor$, and $K=\lfloor TJ/h\rfloor+1$. The original search considers positive coprime pairs $j\le J$, $k\le\lfloor Tj/h\rfloor+1$. A winner has an integer square discriminant $D(j,k)=j^2+4k(Tj-hk)$. The factor is the gcd of $N$ and $(j+2Tk-\sqrt D)/2$. One or two positions succeed on every target product. [Guaranteed region](complete-offset-search.md) · [Why there are at most two](one-or-two-winning-tests.md)

At an odd prime $\ell$ coprime to $N$, a true square allows certain values of the fraction $j/k$ modulo $\ell$, plus an infinity label for $\ell\mid k$. Keep the labels at only some of the primes. Write $I$ for the product of the infinity primes, $m$ for the product of the others, and $R$ for the Chinese remainder of their finite labels. The conditions $I\mid k$ and $j\equiv Rk\bmod m$ describe a lattice with basis $(m,0)$ and $(RI\bmod m,I)$ and determinant $M=Im$. We can enumerate its points even when $M$ is too small for one-label rational reconstruction. When a deferred prime would have rejected a point, we can still test that prime before taking a square root. [Original single-position reconstruction](reconstructing-winning-positions.md) · [Lattice search](../experiments/offset_lattice_batches.py)

Scale coordinates by $(K,J)$, reduce the lattice basis, and write the resulting unscaled basis as $x=(a,b), y=(c,d)$ with positive determinant $M$. Every compatible position equals $w x+u y$ at integer indices, with $Mu=ak-bj$. Bounds on $j,k$ give an interval for $u$. For fixed $u$, the rectangle, positivity and the sharper condition $hk\le Tj+h$ give an interval for $w$ by linear inequalities. On the row $u=0$, only $w=\pm1$ could give a primitive point. On every row where a primitive point gives a square, $D(a,b)$ is a square modulo every divisor of $u$. Indeed, a common divisor of $w,u$ would divide $j,k$, and $D(j,k)\equiv w^2D(a,b)\pmod u$. This can reject a row before any positions in it are visited. [Coordinate, interval and row-test proofs](../PrimeFactors/OffsetLatticeBatches.lean)

Put $L=JK$. With $M\le L$ the reduced first vector bounds the number of rows by $2L/M+1$ and the number of point visits by $4L/M+2$. If restoring one deferred prime $\ell$ would make $M\ell>2L$, and that prime had $g$ labels, the whole group costs at most $6g$ row and point visits combined, including the axis exception. This last bound also applies when $M>L$. It replaces $g$ individual reconstructions with one basis reduction. The bound counts visits. Reducing the basis, computing the obstructions and checking an integer at a visit each cost additional arithmetic. [Finite work bounds](../PrimeFactors/OffsetLatticeBatches.lean)

## Choosing higher prime powers

A prime power can beat the best choice among *all* distinct odd primes. For $N=3,376,036,549$, the threshold is 155,204. Every distinct-prime list above it costs at least 12,180 combinations. Using $3^2\cdot7\cdot11\cdot13\cdot19=171,171$ costs exactly 10,500. The same strict comparison holds on both 2,048-bit inputs when several primes are lifted, as shown below. [Odd-power search and lower-bound calculation](../experiments/offset_prime_power_slopes.py) · [Distinct-prime lower bound](reconstructing-winning-positions.md)

For $p^e$, $p$ odd and $p\nmid N$, use one of two normalized representatives for a primitive pair. If the denominator is a unit, it is $(r,1)$ with $r=x+N/x-2T$ for a unit $x$. Otherwise it is $(1,pb)$ with $b\bmod p^{e-1}$. On the second chart every discriminant is 1 modulo $p$ and has a square root through every power. On the first chart apply the known count for sums of coordinates on a modular hyperbola. Write $G$ for the total number of labels. At a nonsquare $N\bmod p$, $2G=(p+1)p^{e-1}$. At a square,
$$2(p+1)G=(p^2+1)p^{e-1}+2c,\qquad
c=\begin{cases}2p+1 & e\ \text{odd},\\p+2 & e\ \text{even}.\end{cases}$$
In particular $G/p^e\ge5/12$. Powers of 3 approach this limit from above for square $N\bmod3$. For general directions $(a_i,b_i)$ at pairwise coprime moduli $q_i$, let $I=\prod_i\gcd(b_i,q_i)$ and write $k=Iv$. Dividing $b_i j\equiv a_i k\bmod q_i$ by $\gcd(b_i,q_i)$ gives ordinary finite congruences for $j$ in terms of $v$ at moduli $q_i/\gcd(b_i,q_i)$. Their product is $M/I$, so the previous convergent reconstruction applies when $M>2JK$. [Local Lean proofs](../PrimeFactors/OffsetPrimePowerSlopes.lean) · [Bower, Evans, Luo and Miller](https://arxiv.org/abs/1212.2930)

At $2^a$, separate the cases $k$ odd and $k$ even. In the first case, use $j/k=x+N/x-2T$ for odd $x$. This follows from the two integer roots whose product is $Nk^2$, before reducing modulo $2^a$. In the second case $j$ is odd, and *every* even value of $k/j\bmod 2^a$ survives the discriminant test at arbitrary precision. We can therefore choose higher binary precision on the first chart and choose the odd moduli independently on the two charts. Doing so reduced the complete search on 117,449 from 168 to 120 combinations. On the eight small comparisons, there were 58,258 combinations, 3,815 square tests, and the same nine winners. The capped full-size runs tried 32 combinations per chart and reached one square test per input. ## Why adaptive local tests still leave so much work

Suppose a search chooses a new prime, or a higher precision at an existing prime, after seeing the labels on the current branch. Each chosen prime is coprime to the input. The search retains every slope allowed by these local square and factor-sum tests and continues each retained branch until its modulus exceeds $2JK$. We count the leaves needed to exhaust this search. A lucky early hit can still give a factor.

At a fresh odd prime, keep only labels with a *nonzero* square discriminant, and also keep the infinity label. If $N$ is a quadratic residue there are $(p-1)/2$ such labels, otherwise there are $(p+1)/2$. Every one of their $p$ lifts through another digit survives. Restrict to exactly $(p-1)/2$ of the first labels. At 2, keep just the even-denominator chart, which has one label at the first digit and both lifts at each further digit. This defines a subtree of locally consistent choices, even when different children choose different next primes. [Odd regular labels and lifts](../PrimeFactors/OffsetPrimePowerSlopes.lean) · [Permanent binary labels](../PrimeFactors/OffsetDyadicSlopes.lean)

Let $S$ contain the distinct odd primes along a path. Write $m$ for the product of all additional precision factors after their first digits, including further binary digits. Giving the first binary digit to every branch for free, its modulus is at most $2m\prod_{p\in S}p$. Its *path price*, the product of the numbers of retained children, is $m\prod_{p\in S}(p-1)/2$. The rectangle threshold exceeds $2^{1023}$ on every target. If there are at most 130 odd primes, Lean verifies $\prod p\le8\prod(p-1)$ for any such set. The finite check uses the odd primes through 743. Each later prime has $p/(p-1)\le744/743$. These inequalities give a path price greater than $2^{889}$. If there are at least 131 distinct odd primes, the product of the 131 cheapest first-level prices already exceeds $2^{893}$.

To convert the path prices into a bound on the whole tree, distribute unit mass equally among retained children at every node. The mass reaching a leaf is the reciprocal of its path price. Each leaf gets less than $2^{-889}$ and their masses sum to one. Lean proves the equivalent integer inequality by induction on the decision tree. It also proves the fixed-modulus bound directly for the actual finite mask sizes, including an optional binary power. The argument stops applying when a method discards a locally admissible branch using the bounds on the actual integers, or searches a lattice inside a partial branch. This is exactly the distinction exploited by the grouping method. [Finite bounds and adaptive tree theorem](../PrimeFactors/OffsetAdaptiveFloor.lean)

Under Dirichlet GRH the previous research found a prime cutoff that can detect every nonsquare once its integer discriminant is known. Here the obstruction concerns lists of local possibilities before an integer pair has been recovered. The finite lower bound, the grouping method and its coverage use unconditional arithmetic. [Analytic assumptions and cutoff](reconstructing-winning-positions.md#how-far-can-pair-dependent-prime-tests-go)
