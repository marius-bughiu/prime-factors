# Which subsets have a square product?

On each of the two 2,048-bit controls, 16 small primes suffice to prove that **none of the 65,535 nonempty subsets** of the first 16 weighted remainders has a square product. The largest primes used were 137 and 139. This conclusion uses finite arithmetic alone. The Dirichlet GRH release bounds how far a deterministic prime search can ever need to go on an arbitrary list of positive remainders. All inputs to the search are public. The certificate gives information about the chosen remainders and leaves the two full-size factorizations unresolved. [Weighted rows and their exact definition](../experiments/weighted_square_triples.py) · [Public inputs](../benchmarks/weighted-square-triples.json)

## A matrix that certifies every subset

For positive integers $D_1,\ldots,D_m$, put $Q=\operatorname{lcm}(8,D_1,\ldots,D_m)$. For every odd prime $\ell\nmid Q$, form a binary row with entry $0$ if $D_i$ is a square modulo $\ell$ and $1$ otherwise. A subset is represented by its binary mask. Multiplicativity of the Legendre symbol makes the dot product of the row with that mask zero whenever the integer product is square. The true masks form a subspace over $\mathbb F_2$, since repeated factors in the product of two subsets contribute squares.

One can certify a complete answer at any point. Find a basis of the nullspace of the sign matrix by binary elimination. For every basis mask, check by integer square root that its product is an exact square. If every check passes, the nullspace equals the true subspace. At full column rank the empty basis passes immediately. A proper factor is a separate readout requiring a congruence of squares and a proper gcd. Zero remainders need their own case, since every subset containing one has product zero.

## A deterministic stopping bound from Dirichlet GRH

For a nonsquare subset write its product as $s v^2$, where $s>1$ is squarefree. The fundamental discriminant of $\mathbb Q(\sqrt s)$ is $d=s$ for $s\equiv1\pmod4$ and $d=4s$ otherwise. Its conductor $d$ divides $Q$ because each odd prime in $s$ occurs in some $D_i$, and the 2-part of $d$ divides 8. On units $a$ modulo $Q$ the Kronecker character $(d/a)$ therefore gives a quadratic character, and on odd positive unit representatives it equals the Jacobi symbol $(s/a)$. Its value at a test prime is exactly the product of the selected Legendre signs. This uses the squarefree decomposition only to prove the theorem. Computing the tests needs no factorization of any $D_i$.

The induced character really is nontrivial on the full unit group, including when $Q$ has extra prime factors. If an odd prime divides $s$, take $a\equiv1\pmod8$ and a nonsquare residue at that one prime, with residue $1$ at every other odd prime dividing $Q$. The Chinese remainder theorem supplies a unit with those residues, and quadratic reciprocity makes the character $-1$. If $s=2$, use $a\equiv5\pmod8$ and $a\equiv1$ at all odd prime divisors of $Q$. Thus each false mask gives a proper subgroup of $(\mathbb Z/Q\mathbb Z)^\times$, its character kernel.

Under Dirichlet GRH, Bach's bound gives a **prime coprime to $Q$** outside any such subgroup below $3(\log Q)^2$. Therefore the single list of eligible primes up to $\lceil3(\log Q)^2\rceil$ eliminates every false mask simultaneously. The smaller $2(\log Q)^2$ form permits a prime dividing the modulus, which gives no sign in this matrix. Lamzouri, Li and Soundararajan's Theorem 1.1(1) also enforces coprimality and improves the cutoff at large moduli. Part (2) has an additional small-prime-divisor condition that fails here because $8\mid Q$. The pinned Lean release supplies the zero-location statements for Dirichlet L-functions. Their application through the published least-prime theorem here is a paper deduction. For a sharper public ceiling on these examples, $1800!$ has 16,875 bits and exceeds either lcm, so $\omega(Q)<1800$. Both satisfy $\log Q>11360$ and $\log\log Q<9.35$. Dropping the nonpositive correction in Theorem 1.1(1) gives $B(Q)\le 2(9.35)+3+2(1800)(9.35)^2/11360<50$ in the authors' notation. As $\log Q<11367$ and $11363$ respectively, the two $(\log Q+B(Q))^2$ ceilings can safely be replaced by the integers 130,347,889 and 130,256,569. These are worst-case search limits. The smaller example $N=2{,}100{,}271$ shows how the same stopping test handles a relation that exists. After 19 eligible primes through 109, the matrix has rank 15. The only nonzero remaining mask is `0x1060`, taking bit $0$ as the first row. Its three excesses give $57{,}424\cdot873\cdot21{,}312=1{,}033{,}632^2$. ## Cost and earlier methods

Let $b$ be the total input bit length of the remainders, including at least one bit per row. The lcm has $O(b)$ bits. The GRH bound costs at most $O(b^2)$ trial moduli and $O(m b^2)$ symbol evaluations, plus prime generation, reductions and binary elimination. All operand lengths and all these operations are polynomial in $b$. The computation outputs a basis, which can represent exponentially many masks. Listing them individually incurs the output cost. An unconditional route already available here refines the remainders by gcd into pairwise coprime bases, tests those bases for squareness and eliminates the exponent-parity columns belonging to nonsquare bases. Bernstein gives an essentially linear-time coprime-refinement algorithm in the size of the input; the repository uses a simpler gcd implementation. [Existing exact dependency computation](../experiments/pell_relations.py) · [Bernstein, *Factoring into coprimes in essentially linear time*](https://cr.yp.to/lineartime/dcba-20040404.pdf)

There is also an unconditional randomized version with an exact stopping test. Draw a uniform unit $a\bmod Q$ by gcd rejection and use the Jacobi signs $(D_i/a)$, allowing odd composite denominators. The sign map is a group homomorphism. Its image is exactly the orthogonal complement of the true masks. Every true mask annihilates every unit, and the CRT argument supplies a separating unit for each false mask. Each image vector has equally many preimages. For image dimension $\rho$, independent draws therefore require on average $\sum_{j=1}^{\rho}(1-2^{-j})^{-1}<\rho+2$ accepted units to span it. The integer square checks on a nullspace basis certify when to stop. Each accepted unit costs $Q/\varphi(Q)$ raw draws on average. Each draw uses a gcd, and each accepted unit needs $m$ Jacobi symbols on up to $O(b)$-bit arguments.

Quadratic character columns and the expected spanning count are classical. Buhler, Lenstra and Pomerance explicitly describe the rational-integer sign test, the dual-space criterion, and the expected number of uniform draws, and credit Adleman for using quadratic characters in the number field sieve. The application here gives these particular weighted row sets small, independently reproducible certificates and links the deterministic worst-case scan to the released Dirichlet theorem. [Buhler, Lenstra and Pomerance, §8, pp. 67–68](https://www.cs.umd.edu/~gasarch/TOPICS/factoring/1993_Book_TheDevelopmentOfTheNumberField.pdf)
