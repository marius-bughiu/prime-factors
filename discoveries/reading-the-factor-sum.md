# Can two checks pick out the sum of the primes?

For a product of two distinct primes of similar size, two random checks have a better than **39 in 40** chance of distinguishing the true sum from every other number in the standard search interval. On a fresh 52-bit product we found that sum and recovered **52,902,449 × 55,986,167**. On each 2,048-bit comparison we stopped before building the search tables. Even the smaller of our two plans needed more than $2^{449}$ entries in total. [Checked probability](../PrimeFactors/SumSignatureProbability.lean) · [Search fixtures](../benchmarks/sum-signature.json)

A subsequent result shows when one base suffices. For random eligible prime pairs and one uniformly sampled unit, the probability of any repeat in the interval is below $2^{-972}$. On the 52-bit example, reusing digit powers reduced the original 866 modular exponentiations to 78 with the same two bases. [Single-base proof, full-size certificate and work comparison](one-base-for-the-factor-sum.md)

## How the product tells us what to look for

Write $N=pq$ with distinct primes $64\le p<q\le2p$, and let $S=p+q$. For any base $g$ coprime to $N$, the two powers $g^S$ and $g^{N+1}$ have the same remainder modulo $N$. This follows because their exponents differ by $(p-1)(q-1)$. The target on the right is computable from $N$. Once a candidate sum $s$ is known, we check whether $s^2-4N=d^2$, then test $(s-d)/2$ and $(s+d)/2$. The advantage of using powers is that a lookup can compare many sums at once. Hittmeir gives both the classical power identity and the residue-list construction. His estimate about survivor density in a short interval is a separate heuristic. [Hittmeir, §6](https://arxiv.org/html/2205.10074v2#S6) · [Product-and-sum proof](../PrimeFactors/PowerSignature.lean)

Let $I=[\lceil2\sqrt N\rceil,\lfloor3\sqrt{N/2}\rfloor]$ and let $W$ be the difference between its endpoints. Suppose two bases are sampled independently and uniformly from the units modulo this fixed $N$. For a positive gap $e\le W$ to repeat both powers, its two local projections must each lie in a short-period subgroup. More precisely, at $p$ both bases must lie in the unique subgroup of index $(p-1)/\gcd(p-1,e)$. The width of $I$ makes this index at least 6. At $q$ the corresponding index is at least 9. In a cyclic group the fraction of ordered base pairs that lie in the subgroup of index $d$ is $1/d^2$. The Chinese remainder theorem makes the two projected samples independent. Summing over the permitted indices gives

$$\Pr(\text{any repeated signature in } I)<\frac15\cdot\frac18=\frac1{40}.$$

Three independently sampled units give a fraction below $1/6400$ by the same calculation with cubes. These statements hold for each fixed eligible product, with probability over the bases. They improve the project's earlier guarantee of more than one half for two bases. ## Finding the match and paying for it

The search collects the possible remainders of $S$ modulo 4 and successive odd primes until their product $M$ exceeds $W$. It divides the moduli between two lists. A pair of entries $a,b$ represents the sum $L+a+b-kM$, where $L$ is the lower endpoint and $k$ is 0 or 1. A sorted table groups the $b$ values according to their modular powers. For each $a$, the code looks up the target powers and uses binary search within that group to keep exactly the $b$ values in the allowed interval. Prefix sums give both the number and total of all matching sums, including when several share the same powers. The local-choice tuples have distinct residues modulo $M$, so a sum in the interval is counted once. On the 52-bit example the interval spanned 6,602,552 steps. The search did 866 modular exponentiations, 289 table lookups and one integer square test. Trying the first prime with negative Jacobi symbol (3, after testing 2 and 3) on this same input gave a unique match with 433 modular exponentiations. Both searches still constructed all 432 entries. A direct Fermat search beginning at the rounded-up square root took 21,838 square tests. Those operations have different costs. The two full-size searches priced their lists using 44,553 local residues per input and stopped at the cap of 65,536 entries per list. Neither ran a modular power lookup. [Measured work and certificate inputs](../benchmarks/sum-signature.json)

For the fresh example, each prime is the first odd prime at or above $3\cdot2^{24}+H\bmod2^{24}$, where $H$ is the big-endian integer SHA-256 digest of the UTF-8 text `prime-factors, sum-join v1, left` or `prime-factors, sum-join v1, right`. The construction and two independent labels were fixed before testing. The search receives the product and derives its reproducible bases from a separate hash stream. The sampling theorem assumes genuinely uniform, independent draws. [Reproducible base selection](../experiments/sum_signature_join.py)

The $2^{449}$ figures are per-input measurements. We also proved a lower bound for this type of construction on **every** 2,048-bit input. The standard balanced interval has diameter at least $2^{1020}$. Suppose a method chooses any distinct odd primes coprime to $N$, grants itself a stride of at most four, takes its total period beyond that diameter, and builds both complete half-lists of local sum classes. At least one list has **more than $2^{442}$ entries**, even if the prime selection and list split depend on $N$. The proof uses that a prime $\ell$ supplies at least $(\ell-1)/2$ local choices, then applies a bound on the product of those counts. It applies before clipping pairs to the interval. Compressing the lists, pruning partial choices before building them, or finding a narrower interval directly from an input would require a different analysis. [Lean table-size bound](../PrimeFactors/SumSignatureCost.lean)

## When two sums match

For $N=109{,}969$ and base 35, both 674 and 686 pass the power test. Their count and total give the exponent $2(N+1)-(674+686)=218{,}580$. Each matching sum $s$ contributes $N+1-s$, an exponent for which the base raised to that power is 1 modulo $N$. Their sum has the same property. Removing its factor of four leaves 54,645. The remainder of $35^{54645}$ is 43,272. Squaring once gives 23,821, whose square is 1 modulo $N$. Now $\gcd(23820,N)=397$ and $\gcd(23822,N)=277$. Taking gcds of $N$ with the base or its immediate neighbors at the outset gives only 1. [Lean replay of candidates, powers, gcds and primality](../PrimeFactors/AmbiguousSignatureSplit.lean)

If $p-1$ and $q-1$ contain the same power of two and the base has Jacobi symbol $-1$, the squaring chain for any positive exponent annihilating that base is guaranteed to split the product. The matching table supplies such an exponent from the count and total. The extraction by repeated squaring is classical. [Aggregate and splitting theorems](../PrimeFactors/AmbiguousSignatureSplit.lean) · [Miller, 1976](https://www.cs.cmu.edu/~glmiller/Publications/Papers/Mi76.pdf) · [Leander's analysis of Jacobi-negative bases](https://arxiv.org/abs/quant-ph/0208183)

For a uniformly sampled unit with Jacobi symbol $-1$, Leander computed the exact worst-case failure probability of the halving step given a positive annihilating exponent. Write $a=v_2(p-1)$ and $b=v_2(q-1)$, exchanging the primes if necessary so $a\le b$. When $a=b$ the failure probability is zero. When $a<b$ it is $2^{a-b-1}\le1/4$. We formalized the exact count in the two cyclic groups, the Jacobi sign condition, and the criterion for every halving step to miss. In a completed table search the true sum always matches, and the aggregate supplies a positive annihilator even if several sums match. Consequently a single uniformly sampled Jacobi-negative base gives at least a three-in-four chance to finish on each eligible input if the tables are fully constructed. Choosing the first small prime with that sign is deterministic and its success fraction does not follow from uniform sampling. [Lean formalization](../PrimeFactors/JacobiHalvingCount.lean) · [Leander, Theorem 4 and proof](https://arxiv.org/pdf/quant-ph/0208183#page=2)
