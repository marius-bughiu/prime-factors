# Can the product predict the value of a factor clue?

Suppose a helper could supply the lowest 160 bits of a prime factor. **Look at the last three bits of the product first.** Endings `011` and `111` guarantee that the clue fixes exactly 161 low bits of the sum. Ending `101` guarantees exactly 162. Ending `001` guarantees at least 163, with about 164 fixed on average even after conditioning on any one specified 161-bit product suffix with that ending. The average for actual prime pairs uses the stated Dirichlet-GRH estimate. The three exact cases and the 163-bit minimum are elementary and apply to each such input. The actual value of the sum remainder still requires the clue. ## How the three visible bits distinguish the outcomes

Every odd square is 1 modulo 8. For an odd candidate factor remainder $a$ modulo $2^k$, the number of extra low sum bits is at least $j$ exactly when $j\le k+1$ and $N\equiv a^2+a2^k\pmod{2^j}$. When $k\ge3$, the public comparison modulo 8 alone settles the gain for every odd $a$ unless $N$ is 1 modulo 8. [Precision criterion](../PrimeFactors/DyadicFactorClueGain.lean) · [Three fixed outcomes](../PrimeFactors/FixedProductClueTails.lean)

For a fixed product equal to 1 modulo 8, count each odd candidate factor suffix once. Exactly $2^{k+1-j}$ suffixes gain precisely $j$ extra sum bits for $3\le j\le k$, and two suffixes gain $k+1$. The average extra gain is exactly $4-2^{2-k}$. Of the two suffixes giving the largest gain, neither allows further filtering by binary digits after its single sum congruence is imposed. Two different suffixes, corresponding to ordinary square roots of the product, do allow further filtering. [Exact histogram](../PrimeFactors/FixedProductClueTails.lean) · [Disjoint suffix pairs](../PrimeFactors/SingularDyadicClueSuffixes.lean) · [Effect on the search](when-more-binary-digits-can-reject-a-factor-sum.md)

Now count each eligible **prime pair** once. The population consists of distinct 1,024-bit primes whose product has 2,048 bits. Fix any one particular 161-bit product suffix equal to 1 modulo 8. Within that population the mean extra gain differs from $4-2^{-158}$ by less than $2^{-144}$. The chance that the true suffix gives the maximum gain differs from $2^{-158}$ by at most $2^{-304}$. The chance that the true suffix belongs to either of the two ordinary square-root classes obeys the same bound. This event is disjoint from maximum gain. The transfer to actual primes uses the published arithmetic-progression estimate of Ernvall-Hytönen and Palojärvi under Dirichlet GRH. Lean takes the estimate as an explicit hypothesis. ## Would asking for a different number of factor bits help?

Write $t$ for the exponent of two dividing $q-p$ for distinct odd factors. A clue of width $k$ gains $k$ extra bits when $k<t$, gains $t+1$ when $k=t$, and gains $t$ at every larger width. For $71\times79$, the gains at widths one through five are **1, 2, 4, 3 and 3**. Both a three-bit clue and a four-bit clue fix exactly seven sum bits. Knowing the gain at the largest width $K$ already reveals precisely $\min(t,K+1)$ and determines the entire record of smaller-width gains. This counts extra sum bits beyond the bits supplied. [Width profile and exact inverse](../PrimeFactors/FactorClueWidthProfile.lean)

Even a rule using the entire product to choose its width has a limit. If it may choose $k$ from a finite set $W\subseteq\{1,\ldots,160\}$, its chance of attaining the maximum at the chosen width is at most $(1+2^{-146})\sum_{k\in W}2^{-k}$ under the stated prime-count estimates at those widths. Distinct widths require different exact factor-gap exponents, so their maximum-gain events are disjoint. The bound estimates attainment of that particular maximum.
