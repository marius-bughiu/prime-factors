# How many sums share the actual digit?

If we knew the sum of two unknown primes, we could recover them from their product. Suppose someone gives us a correct clue about that sum for the project's fresh 2,048-bit product. Even with the clue in hand, **at least $2^{630}$ distinct sums in the actual search interval pass both the clue and the modular test described below**. On the crossed comparison product, at least $2^{633}$ pass. [Lean proof for both products](../PrimeFactors/ActualDigitMultiplicity.lean)

The clue is one cyclotomic digit. For a product $N=pq$ of distinct odd primes, its value is the remainder of $(p-1)(q-1)/2$ on division by a chosen positive integer $M$, the radix. Since $(p-1)(q-1)=N+1-(p+q)$, the digit selects a regularly spaced list of sums. The public interval comes from $N$ and the known bit width of the factors. The proof grants the correct digit for free. [Digit identity](../PrimeFactors/CyclotomicDigitPeriod.lean) · [Interval bounds](../PrimeFactors/ProductDeficitBand.lean)

For each sum $S$ on that list, the modular test asks whether some two remainders modulo $m$ add to $S$ and multiply to $N$ modulo $m$. The bounds above cover every $1\le M\le2^{128}$ and $1\le m\le2^{256}$, including a choice of $m$ made after seeing the digit. For several tests, the same bound applies if the radices have a common multiple within the first cap and the sieve moduli have a common multiple within the second. [Digit and modulus reductions](../PrimeFactors/ActualDigitMultiplicity.lean)

The test repeats as the list advances. The true factor sum passes, so each complete repetition in its list contains at least one passing sum. For a concrete count, fix $M=2^{128}$ and $m=315$. In either product's list, every 315 consecutive entries visit all 315 remainders exactly once. The fresh product accepts 18 of them and the crossed product accepts 24. This gives **at least $2^{883}$ and $2^{886}$ passing sums**, respectively, for every attainable digit value. The proof counts complete repetitions and gives an exact formula for the partial one at the end. [Local counts and full-interval bounds](../PrimeFactors/ActualDigitMultiplicity.lean)

These counts measure ambiguity left by this particular clue and sieve. To verify a surviving sum, one can test whether $S^2-4N$ is an integer square and recover the factors from its square root. A method that uses other information could distinguish candidates without testing them individually. [Exact square test](../PrimeFactors/FactorSum.lean)
