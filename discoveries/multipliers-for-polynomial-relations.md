# Choosing where to look for smooth values

On eight fresh 79-bit products, picking a multiplier from the product alone recovered all eight pairs of primes. Multiplier one recovered three at the same number of positions, and seven when given enough extra positions to match both sieve marks and prime divisions on each input. A sieve mark is one visit to a position for one prime. Across the entire 24-input comparison the corresponding counts were 12, 5 and 10. Neither the fresh nor the crossed 2,048-bit control produced even one value made entirely of primes through 2,048. [All inputs, budgets and outcomes](../research/multiplier-polynomials-panel.json)

For example the selector chose 41 on 437,734,232,099,653,621,163,699. At position 54,637 the resulting square relation gave 626,783,228,959 and 698,382,170,861. The ordinary search missed even after 111 blocks, at which point its marks and divisions both exceeded the selected search's totals. One 80-bit input went the other way. Lean verified all 13 distinct factorizations found across the comparisons. [Examples and full-size benchmarks](../benchmarks/multiplier-polynomials.json)

## How the product chooses a multiplier

The public input is the odd product $N$. We compare the 26 odd squarefree $k$ from 1 through 63. For each chosen $k$ the search constructs square leading coefficients $A=c^2$ with $c$ coprime to $N$ and chooses a root $B$ of $kN$ modulo $A$. This gives the integer polynomial
$$Q(x)=Ax^2+2Bx+(B^2-kN)/A.$$
Modulo $N$, $Q(x)$ equals the square of $(Ax+B)/c$. Several signed values with a square product give a pair of modular square roots whose difference can reveal a factor. Changing $k$ changes which primes can divide the values. The Knuth–Schroeppel multiplier and multiple-polynomial quadratic sieve are established methods. We implemented the scoring rule in the repository's existing polynomial sieve. [Silverman, §5](https://doi.org/10.1090/S0025-5718-1987-0866119-8) · [FLINT selector](https://flintlib.org/doc/qsieve/qsieve_knuth_schroeppel.html) · [Original polynomial comparison](polynomial-relations.md)

For an odd prime $p$ coprime to $AkN$, the polynomial has either two roots or none modulo each $p^e$, according as $kN$ is a square or a nonsquare modulo $p$. At a prime dividing squarefree $k$ exactly once and coprime to $AN$, one class is divisible by $p$ and none by $p^2$. At a prime dividing $A$ with $2B$ a unit there is one class at every depth. The one-variable mean valuations used to score the first two cases are $2/(p-1)$ or zero, and $1/p$. For odd $A$ the means at 2 are $2,1/2,1,1/2$ as $kN$ runs through $1,3,5,7\pmod8$. These follow by summing the fractions of solutions at successive powers of two. [Prime-power proofs, finite counts and normalization](../PrimeFactors/MultiplierPolynomialRoots.lean)

The selector uses the dyadic term and the 24 odd primes through 97. It weights the mean at $p$ by $\log_2 p$ and subtracts $\tfrac12\log_2 k$ for the increased size of the numbers being sieved. Each contribution is rounded to the nearest millionth of a bit before summation, with ties in the final score going to smaller $k$. The size charge approximates how values grow when choosing $A$ near $\sqrt{2kN}/512$. On scaled full-size polynomials a prime dividing $A$ can depart from the score. The score estimates small-prime divisibility. Finding enough entirely smooth values also depends on the rest of their arithmetic. [Selection and exact sieve](../experiments/multiplier_polynomials.py)

Each primary run used 64 blocks of 1,025 positions, with factor base through 2,048. Before running the searches we fixed the score, multiplier menu, budgets, 14 previously generated 80–96-bit inputs, the seed for eight fresh 79-bit inputs, and the two full-size controls. The primary controls included multiplier one, fixed multiplier three, multiplier one with selector setup charged, and multiplier one with one extra block of useful search. ## Measuring the score on a particular interval

Lean proves formulas for the sum and squared sum of the score over an actual finite block. A root modulo $p^e$ marks one arithmetic progression. Its contribution uses the exact number of positions in that progression. Multiplying two indicators uses the intersection of the corresponding progressions. Compatible residues give one progression modulo the least common multiple, and incompatible residues give zero. Signed weights let the same sums compare two polynomials point by point, including coincidences at different powers of the same prime. Direct division on each position independently checks the calculation. [Moment and finite-block proofs](../PrimeFactors/MultiplierPolynomialScore.lean)

On the first centered 1,025 positions for each full-size product, we compared multipliers three and one, counting prime powers through 4,096 at primes through 97. After charging the actual polynomial size at each position, multiplier three gained an average of −0.358 bits on the first product and −5.324 on the second. It still won at 480 and 154 individual positions respectively. Both multipliers yielded zero values smooth through 97 on both blocks. The full 64-block selector chose seven and one on these two products. Run `python3 -m experiments.multiplier_block_moments --check` to reproduce the finite-block comparison. [Measurements and operation counts](../research/multiplier-block-moments.json)

The released Dirichlet Riemann hypothesis gives a separate existence bound for prescribing which small primes see $kN$ as a square. The desired signs specify a coset in the group of unit residues. Lamzouri, Li and Soundararajan bound the first prime multiplier in such a coset under Dirichlet GRH. Its index doubles with each independent sign requirement. This deduction bounds the multiplier search, while the cost of producing fully smooth values remains open. All measurements and finite identities above use elementary arithmetic.
