# Choosing a three-square count that would reveal one prime

For $697=17\cdot41$, counting representations of $697^2$ as three squares gives 4,182. That is just $697$ times the six ways to represent 1. Trying $2\cdot697^2$ gives $697$ times the twelve ways to represent 2. A test using only 697 picks a more informative multiplier, **10**. There are 18,696 ordered, signed representations of $10\cdot697^2$ and 24 of 10. Their ratio is 779. Subtract 697 and halve the difference to get **41**. An independent small census obtained both counts without using the factors. ## What the product tells us before we count

Write $r_3(m)$ for the number of ordered integer triples whose squares add to $m$. Signs and zero coordinates count. For $N=pq$ with distinct odd primes, the Jacobi symbol $\left(\frac{-d}{N}\right)$ can be computed from $d$ and $N$ using Euclidean arithmetic. If it is $-1$, exactly one of the two primes sees $-d$ as a nonzero square. Call that prime $r$. A classical recurrence for counting three squares gives the exact identity

$$r_3(dN^2)=r_3(d)(N+2r).$$

The identity alone does not give an informative ratio when $r_3(d)=0$. At $N=15$ the sign for $d=23$ is negative, yet both counts vanish. In contrast, $d=1$ gives $150/6=25$ and selects 5. At the same product $d=2$ gives $252/12=21$ and selects 3. [Two-prime identity, positive controls and zero-count example](../PrimeFactors/AdaptiveThreeSquareTwist.lean) · [Hirschhorn and Sellers, equation (4)](https://www.d.umn.edu/~jsellers/p8.pdf#page=3)

## How small a multiplier suffices?

First check the sign for $d=1$, then for $d=2$. Use the first with negative sign. If both signs are positive, take a prime $\ell$ for which $\left(\frac{\ell}{N}\right)=-1$ and choose $d=2\ell$. In this case multiplying by $-2$ preserves the negative sign. Twice an odd prime is 2 modulo 4, so the classical three-square theorem guarantees a positive base count. If a particular representation is known, checking its three coordinates also establishes positivity. For 65, the prime $\ell=3$ gives $d=6$. For 697, $\ell=5$ gives $d=10$. [Product-only sign tests](../PrimeFactors/BoundedThreeSquareTwist.lean) · [Base representability](../PrimeFactors/EvenPrimeThreeSquareBase.lean) · [Ankeny’s proof of the three-square theorem](https://doi.org/10.1090/S0002-9939-1957-0085275-8)

The Dirichlet-GRH connection supplies a bound for this choice. Lamzouri, Li and Soundararajan give a small prime outside a proper subgroup of the units when the modulus has no small prime factor. Apply their result to the subgroup of positive Jacobi symbol. For two 1,024-bit primes with a 2,048-bit product, the local proof puts $\ell$ at most **2,015,165** and $d$ at most **4,030,330**. Testing an integer's Jacobi symbol here requires no sphere count. The bound concerns the small multiplier alone. For any chosen mixed-sign twist put $b=r_3(d)>0$ and call the large count $A$. Since the selected prime is odd, $A$ has the known remainder $b(N+2)$ modulo $4b$. An estimate within **strictly less than $2b$** of $A$ determines the exact prime by rounding. At 697 this allows an absolute error strictly below 48. More generally, if two candidate prime values fit an estimate within error $\varepsilon$, their distance is at most $\varepsilon/b$. The challenge is obtaining even a rough estimate whose *error bound is known* for the individual input. [Precision theorem](../PrimeFactors/AdaptiveTwistPrecision.lean)

The earlier finite-list theorem allowed the prime sizes to grow and fixed the list before choosing the product. Here the witness bound depends on the input size and the sign test selects a multiplier after reading the product. For every product in the specified range, **one exact large count would suffice**. Obtaining that count remains the bottleneck. Direct enumeration in the definition loops over pairs of possible coordinate magnitudes, with $(\lfloor\sqrt{dN^2}\rfloor+1)^2$ pairs. Even at $d=1$ this exceeds $2^{4094}$ for a 2,048-bit input. ## What Lean checks here

The local Lean 4.19 development proves how the negative sign selects a prime, how the representation count reveals it, and how the explicit bound on the multiplier follows. The full-size theorem takes the least-prime estimate, the sufficient direction of Legendre’s three-square theorem, and the Hirschhorn–Sellers recurrence as inputs. The local census agrees exactly with the cardinality of all ordered signed integer triples, including at zero. For 15, all four positive counts were also checked by independent enumeration.
