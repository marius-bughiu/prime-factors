# How much of the interval gives the prime sum?

Of the first 90 positive integers, exactly **37, 59 and 74** share a factor with 2,183. Their count, three, gives the sum of the two primes by the rule $24(3+1)=96$. The product determines the multiplier 24 before any counting begins. Measuring the count directly took 90 gcds. The 37th gcd had already found a prime, and trial division from the bottom of the balanced odd-factor interval found it on the second division. ## Where the multiplier comes from

Let $N=pq$ for distinct primes, let $m\ge2$, and count the positive integers through $\lfloor N/m\rfloor$ that share a factor with $N$. Call the result $C_m$. Every integer counted is a multiple of $p$ or of $q$. The first positive multiple of both is $N$, beyond the cutoff. Counting the two sets separately gives

$$
C_m=\lfloor q/m\rfloor+\lfloor p/m\rfloor,\qquad
p+q=m C_m+(p\bmod m)+(q\bmod m).
$$

The last two remainders are added as ordinary integers. Suppose $m$ is coprime to $N$. For each invertible residue $x$ modulo $m$, the other residue must be $N/x$ modulo $m$. Lean proves that the sum of their least representatives has a single value for **every** such pair exactly when $m$ divides both 24 and $N+1$. In that case the value is $m$. For odd $N$ the largest eligible denominator is therefore $g=\gcd(24,N+1)$, and $p+q=g(C_g+1)$. [Correction and classification](../PrimeFactors/FractionalPrefixResidues.lean) · [Greatest denominator and decoding](../PrimeFactors/FractionalPrefixReadout.lean)

The classification follows from a symmetry. Negating both unit residues changes a sum $r$ to $2m-r$, so a constant sum must be $m$. The two residues must then be opposites. Setting $x=1$ forces $N\equiv-1\pmod m$, and allowing every $x$ forces every unit to square to one. This last property holds exactly for the divisors of 24. The classification ranges over all compatible residue pairs. Additional information about the primes can exclude some of them. [Proof](../PrimeFactors/FractionalPrefixResidues.lean)

For a 2,048-bit input, even this shortest universally valid interval has **more than $2^{2042}$ entries**. That bounds the work of a scan testing every entry individually. A way to obtain the count without scanning the interval would change the cost. [Full-width interval bound](../PrimeFactors/FractionalPrefixResidues.lean)

## What happens at other denominators?

A larger denominator can give several candidate sums. For 2,183 and $m=48$, the cutoff is 45, the sole nonunit is 37, and the possible sums are 72, 96 and 120. Only 96 gives an integer factor pair. At $527=17\cdot31$ the cutoff is ten and the count is zero. The same three residue corrections 24, 48 and 72 are now the candidate sums, and only 48 succeeds. [Examples](../benchmarks/fractional-nonunit-prefix.json)

Across 57 small balanced products, testing every coprime denominator from 2 through 48 gave 2,502 cases. All decoded correctly after testing 17,533 candidate sums. In 1,945 cases the interval already contained the smaller prime. Scanning every interval cost 262,191 gcds. Stopping each scan at its first nonunit or at its end took 71,627 gcds. Running ascending balanced trial division for the same product/denominator pairs took 7,685 divisions. These are operation counts, with each denominator charged separately. [Experiment](../experiments/fractional_nonunit_prefix.py) · [Complete counts](../benchmarks/fractional-nonunit-prefix.json)

An earlier calculation used counts of coprime integers at powers of two to recover a column of the primes’ binary digits. Counting the excluded integers gives the same column and lets us ask what can be recovered if a reading is missing. [Earlier dyadic identity](dyadic-totative-masks.md) Write $B_j=C_{2^j}$ for $j\ge1$. Then $B_j-2B_{j+1}$ is the number of ones among the two bits at position $j$. If the middle reading is missing, the two neighboring readings allow two possible middle values exactly when $B_j-4B_{j+2}$ is 2 or 4 under the binary-column constraints. Its parity distinguishes them. For 7,169 and 7,313 all positive-index counts agree except the eighth-interval count, which is 21 or 20. Another balanced pair of products realizes the second ambiguity. The half-interval count $B_1$ alone already gives $p+q=2B_1+2$ for odd primes. [Dyadic readings and exact examples](../PrimeFactors/DyadicNonunitReadings.lean)

## Can sampling or a Fourier coefficient replace a full count?

For the earlier Jacobi experiment, $H_d$ counts the changed readings after using the first $d$ squares. At $N=2{,}183$ the product gives $d=16$ and $H_d=1{,}248$. Estimate $H_d/N$ using the proportion of changes in a uniform sample and convert it to an estimate of $p+q$. Root mean squared error below one requires **4,559 independent draws**, or **1,477 distinct draws** sampled uniformly without replacement. This accuracy criterion averages errors over samples. An individual sample can give a different error. [Exact thresholds](../PrimeFactors/CircleMarkUnsignedSampling.lean) · [Measured controls](../benchmarks/changed-mask-frequency.json)

For distinct odd primes $p<q<2p$ and $d=\lfloor\sqrt{N/8}\rfloor$, the one-draw variance of that estimate is $V=H_d(N-H_d)/d^2>15N/8$. Independent draws require more than $15N/8$ readings, and drawing without replacement requires more than $15N/23$ distinct readings. The proof applies to this estimator. [Variance bounds](../PrimeFactors/CircleMarkUnsignedVariance.lean) · [Sampling proof](../PrimeFactors/CircleMarkUnsignedSampling.lean)

Individual samples also carry information beyond their change flag. At 2,183, grouping the 16 coordinate differences and taking a single gcd gives 37 on 688 of the 2,183 residues and 59 on 336. Of 1,248 changed readings, 1,232 have a proper gcd at one or more individual coordinates. The 16 exceptions are the public squares themselves. A complete direct row uses 16 differences. At full size its length grows as the square root of the product. [Exact partitions and row lengths](../PrimeFactors/CircleMarkPrefixExtraction.lean)

For the single-square mask, the Fourier coefficient at zero frequency is the change count $p+q-3$. At any frequency coprime to $N$, the coefficient equals one minus a known phase times the sum of four phases indexed by the square roots of one modulo $N$, evaluated at the half-frequency $s=k/2\pmod N$. Conversion in either direction preserves absolute error. Thus estimating this coefficient would also estimate the four-root sum at its corresponding frequency. Using exact integer arithmetic, we checked the Chinese remainder formula for all 9,297 frequencies of 21 toy products and the mixed-root phase relation at all 8,508 coprime frequencies. The pinned RH statements concern the location of zeros of Dirichlet $L$-functions. An efficient evaluation of these finite readings from the product alone remains open.
