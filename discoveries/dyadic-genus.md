# A class-number divisor before the first search

The 2,048-bit comparison number leaves remainder 1 on division by both 8 and 7. These two small checks guarantee that a quadratic class number associated with the product is divisible by **16**. We can therefore skip 15 of every 16 candidate class numbers in that field. After charging for its larger discriminant and the divisor already available in the original field, the projected number of entries in the first search table falls by **3.2%**. Its length still has 516 bits. The calculation uses the product alone. ## Why sixteen divides the class number

Suppose $N=pq$ for distinct odd primes, $N\equiv1\pmod8$, and $(N/7)=+1$, where the parentheses denote a Legendre symbol. Use discriminant $\Delta=-56N$. Its four prime discriminants are $8,-7,p^*,q^*$, with $r^*=(-1)^{(r-1)/2}r$. Genus theory gives eight elements in $G[2]$, the subgroup of quadratic-form classes squaring to the identity. A class is a square in $G$ exactly when all four of its genus signs are positive. The product of the four signs is always positive, leaving three independent signs. [Stevenhagen, §§2–3](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1806.06250#page=4)

Consider the two explicitly known classes $U=[2,0,7N]$ and $V=[7,0,2N]$. The argument assumes $N>7$, as follows from the input promise. Both represent involutions. They and the identity are distinct reduced forms. Their product represents the ramified ideal of norm 14 and is a fourth distinct class. Equivalently, the eight ambiguous classes correspond to the unordered factorizations $a c=14N$, with reduced form $[a,0,c]$. In fact a reduced ambiguous form must have $b=0$, $a=b$ or $a=c$. Either of the last two with $b\ne0$ would express $14N$ as a product of two integers of the same parity, impossible since $14N\equiv2\pmod4$. [Reduced forms and inverse convention](https://flintlib.org/doc/algorithms/number-theory/binary-quadratic-forms.html)

Both public signs, at $8$ and $-7$, are positive on $U$ and $V$. To see this on $U$, use the represented integer $2+7N$. It is $1\bmod8$ and $2\bmod7$. On $V$ use $7+2N$. It is $1\bmod8$ and $2N\bmod7$. Both integers are odd and coprime to $14N$. The character at $8$ is $(2/\cdot)$, positive at 1 modulo 8. By reciprocity the character at $-7$ is $(\cdot/7)$, and $(2/7)=(N/7)=+1$.

It follows that on the four classes $1,U,V,UV$ the two hidden signs must agree. There are only two possible sign patterns. At least one of $U,V,UV$ therefore has every sign positive. Call it $S$. Then $S$ is both a nonidentity involution and a square. The square subgroup $G^2$ has even size, and the squaring map has kernel of size eight, so $16\mid h(\Delta)$. The lower bound is sharp. The product $1073=29\times37$ passes both tests and has $h(-56\times1073)=48$, whose highest power-of-two divisor is 16. This conclusion requires neither the identity of $S$ nor an actual square root. It also explains why identifying $S$ alone would leave both primes hidden. [Group-order implication in Lean](../PrimeFactors/DyadicGenus.lean)

For a class-number interval $[1,U_0]$, set $w=\lfloor U_0/16\rfloor$. The class $g^4$ of any trial form is annihilated by $h/16$, since $g^2\in G^2$ and $|G^2|=h/8$. Thus the search for an annihilator can start in $[1,w]$. An exponent $e$ that works gives $4e$ as an annihilator for $g$. The corresponding base comparison at $\Delta=-4N$ has four involutions and uses $g^2$ in the window through $\lfloor U_0/4\rfloor$. We use the same unconditional integer upper bound $U_0=\lfloor\lceil\sqrt{|\Delta|}\rceil(2+\operatorname{bitlength}|\Delta|)/3\rfloor$ in each field. The projected table length is $\lceil\sqrt w\rceil$. On the recorded 2,048-bit product the new length lies between 96.7% and 96.8% of the old length. [Checked comparison](../PrimeFactors/DyadicGenus.lean)

A factor arises from any ambiguous class outside the four public ones. For its diagonal representative $[a,0,c]$, exactly one of $p,q$ divides $a$, so $\gcd(a,N)$ splits $N$. The search must still *find* such a class. One sufficient approach is to collect split-prime forms whose genus images span $G/G^2$, project each to its power-of-two part using an annihilator, and compute a basis for the subgroup they generate. The projections then generate the whole Sylow 2-subgroup. Indeed if a subgroup $H$ surjects modulo squares in a finite abelian 2-group $P$, then $P/H=(P/H)^2$ and repeated squaring forces $P/H=1$. The last nonidentity powers of a basis generate $G[2]$, which can be enumerated in eight elements. This involves prime selection, form powering, and group-basis work. The full-size comparison measures the initial table length only. [Sutherland's algorithms for abelian prime-power groups](https://arxiv.org/abs/0809.3413)

A second choice is $\Delta=-39N$ whenever $N\equiv1\pmod4$ and $(N/3)=(N/13)=+1$. The public ramified classes at 3 and 13 have positive signs at both $-3$ and 13. Their cross signs are $(13/3)=(-3/13)=+1$. For the diagonal signs use the coprime integers $(13N+3)/4$ and $(3N+13)/4$ represented respectively by $[3,3,(13N+3)/4]$ and $[13,13,(3N+13)/4]$, or another represented coprime integer in the same residue at the relevant prime. The resulting symbols are $(13N/3)$ and $(3N/13)$, both positive. The same four-to-two argument proves $16\mid h(-39N)$. Choosing more auxiliary primes also increases the discriminant used by the analytic bound. Each proposed choice needs its own cost comparison. [Ramified classes and the genus map](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1806.06250#page=4)

## What the two untwisted dyadic choices guarantee

For $N\equiv1\pmod8$, at least one of $h(-4N)$ and $h(-8N)$ is divisible by eight. Each group has four ambiguous classes. Write $\Delta=dN$ with $d=-4$ or $-8$. Write $T$ for the ramified class at 2. For $d=-4$ it is $[2,2,(N+1)/2]$, representing the odd integer $(N+1)/2$. For $d=-8$ it is $[2,0,N]$, representing $2+N$. Both represented integers are coprime to $\Delta$. In each case the public sign $\chi_d(T)$ is positive because these integers are respectively $1\bmod4$ and $3\bmod8$.

The hidden primes have the same residue $r\bmod8$, because every odd residue modulo eight is its own inverse. If $r=1$ or $7$, the two hidden signs of $T$ are also positive. Quadratic reciprocity evaluates them as $(2/p)$ and $(2/q)$ in both fields. Thus $T$ is a nonidentity square involution. For $r=3$ choose $d=-8$. For $r=5$ choose $d=-4$. The supplementary laws give $(d/p)=(d/q)=+1$ in the chosen field. The public genus character is therefore positive on $T$ and on the ambiguous form $[p,0,|d|q/4]$. The latter represents $p+64|d|q/4$ coprime to $\Delta$ with character $(d/p)$. These two forms generate the four ambiguous classes: both are nonprincipal and they are distinct, also when $p<q$ is unrestricted. Their images under the complete genus map now have at most two possible values. A nonidentity square involution again gives the extra factor two. Both products are $209\bmod256$ and their successful choices are opposite. Lean counts the reduced primitive forms through $a\le\sqrt{|\Delta|/3}$ and checks all four primes. Trying both fields can cost more than dividing one search width by another factor of two. A different tempting shortcut is to choose an order of prime conductor $f$ in the *same* imaginary quadratic field. Its class number, for fundamental $\Delta<-4$, is $h(\Delta)(f-(\Delta/f))$. The known multiplier scales both endpoints of any inherited class-number interval. Dividing the endpoints by that multiplier recovers the original interval exactly. ## An untwisted calculation on 77

If $N\equiv5\pmod8$, the public ramified class $T=[2,2,(N+1)/2]$ at $\Delta=-4N$ instead has **negative** public sign under $\chi_{-4}$, since $(N+1)/2\equiv3\pmod4$. Thus $G=\langle T\rangle\times\ker\chi_{-4}$. The kernel has just one nonidentity involution. For each seed, choose the representative $x$ of $\{g,gT\}$ with positive public sign. Then $x^2=g^2$, and $h/4$ annihilates $x^2$ by the square-subgroup argument. When $x$ has even order, raise it to the odd part of a known annihilating exponent, then square repeatedly. The last nonidentity power is the required kernel involution. It and its product with $T$ include the factor-revealing class $[p,0,q]$. Selecting one of the two split genus patterns that survive normalization outside the principal genus is an index-four prime-coset problem, as in the earlier twist argument. This time we need no auxiliary twist prime. [Earlier projection and prime bound](twisting-ambiguous-forms.md) · [Checked normalization identity](../PrimeFactors/DyadicGenus.lean)

For 77 we have $T=[2,2,39]$. The first split prime is 3 and gives $g=[3,2,26]$, whose public sign is negative. Composition with $T$ gives $x=[6,2,13]$. To square $x$ it suffices to square $g$: its middle coefficient lifts from $2\bmod6$ to $-4\bmod18$. The squared form $[9,-4,9]$ reduces to $[9,4,9]$. The identity $77=9^2-(4/2)^2$ gives both primes, 7 and 11. Lean checks the coefficient arithmetic, the factorization, the group-order divisibility step and the finite counterexamples. The genus interpretation and the proofs applying it to quadratic forms are on paper.
