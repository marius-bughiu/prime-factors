# Finishing a factor-sum search with one witness

A quick test can certify that **one base is enough to finish the search once the two search tables have been joined**. Even two wrong guesses for the sum can then lead to a factor. Finding the matches is still far beyond reach on our two 2,048-bit controls. Base 2 passed the test on both, but the fresh and crossed inputs needed about $2^{449.03}$ and $2^{449.42}$ table entries respectively, counting both tables together. Both runs stopped before building either table and left the whole input unresolved. [Fresh control](https://marius-bughiu.github.io/prime-factors/#collision-gap-fresh-2048) · [Crossed control](https://marius-bughiu.github.io/prime-factors/#collision-gap-crossed-2048) · [Proof of recovery from a gap](../PrimeFactors/GeometricCollisionGap.lean)

## What the test tells us

Write $N=pq$ for distinct odd primes and $S=p+q$. For every base $g$ coprime to $N$,

$$g^S\equiv g^{N+1}\pmod N,$$

because $N+1-S=(p-1)(q-1)$. Earlier work used this equality to recognize a unique sum in an interval. The new result handles ties. First compute $\gcd(g,N)$, then, for a unit base, compute $v=g^{N-1}\bmod N$ and $\gcd(v-1,N)$. Either gcd can give a proper factor immediately. If $v=1$, try the next base in the witness search. If $\gcd(v-1,N)=1$, use this base for the join. This is the public **Fermat witness** case that proceeds to the tables. On both full-size controls the two gcds were 1. [Earlier recognition result](one-base-for-the-factor-sum.md) · [Preflight and join](../experiments/sum_signature_join.py)

For $p<q\le 2p$, the public interval for $S$ has endpoints $L=\lceil\sqrt{4N}\rceil$ and $U=\lfloor\sqrt{9N/2}\rfloor$. Write $W=U-L$. A small modulus rules out sums incompatible with $xy\equiv N$ at that modulus. The Chinese remainder theorem combines the remaining choices. The search splits the moduli between two tables and joins entries whose sums fall in the interval and whose powers match $g^{N+1}$. Every such test preserves the true sum. Hittmeir describes this modular sum sieve and the two-list approach. The result here concerns decoding the matches returned by the join. [Hittmeir, §§3–6](https://arxiv.org/html/2205.10074v2) · [Project table construction](reading-the-factor-sum.md)

## How a wrong sum can help

A complete join finds at least one match. A singleton must be $S$. Test $S^2-4N$ for a square and obtain the factors as $(S\pm\sqrt{S^2-4N})/2$. With several matches, the same square test can first be applied to a bounded number of saved sums. Even if all those guesses are wrong, two distinct matches $s,t$ give $g^{|s-t|}=1\pmod N$. The search can use a smaller number $h$, the gcd of all differences from one matched sum. It computes this using adjacent differences inside sorted buckets and one offset per occupied slice, without expanding every matching pair. [Range-gcd identity and implementation](../PrimeFactors/GeometricGapBudget.lean) · [Gap decoder](../experiments/collision_gap_readout.py)

Let $d_p,d_q$ be the least positive exponents giving 1 modulo $p,q$ respectively. Their least common multiple $d$ is the order modulo $N$ and divides $h$. A complete factorization of $h$ always suffices for the following tests. Starting at $h$, remove a prime factor $\ell$ whenever $g^{h/\ell}=1\pmod N$, repeating on the reduced exponent. Public power tests suffice to recover $d$. If $d_p\ne d_q$, one local order is a proper divisor of $d$. Choose a prime divisor $\ell$ of the quotient by that order. This order divides $d/\ell$ and the other cannot, since otherwise their least common multiple would also divide $d/\ell$. Trying each distinct prime in $d$ therefore finds a proper factor through $\gcd(g^{d/\ell}-1,N)$. Equal local orders would divide both $p-1$ and $q-1$, hence also $N-1$. A Fermat witness rules that out. [Checked stripping algorithm, splitting theorem and witness implication](../PrimeFactors/GeometricCollisionGap.lean)

There is another way to finish when a fixed base has equal orders $d_p=d_q=d$. Now $d^2$ divides $(p-1)(q-1)$, so $S\equiv N+1\pmod{d^2}$. If $d^2>W$, rounding up from $L$ gives the unique sum in the interval. With a smaller $d$, the actual endpoints can still leave just one candidate. [Square-modulus recovery](../PrimeFactors/GeometricOrderReadout.lean)

## Stopping the join as soon as it gives an answer

On a fresh 92-bit product, **2,999,205,669,099,453,866,424,720,319**, we stored 37,422 right-hand entries and generated left-hand entries one at a time. In reverse order, row 22,985 gave **45,058,864,713,217 × 66,561,944,873,407**. Forward order reached the 32,256-row cap without finding the match. Under the 40,000-entry storage cap the previous implementation stopped when it planned a 64,512-entry left table. The new run stored no such table. These counts exclude the tables of digit powers and the traversal stack. On this machine Python’s tracked peak allocations were 34.82 MiB with both tables and 7.07 MiB streaming in either direction. Median discovery times over five runs without allocation tracking were 0.225 seconds with both tables, 0.069 seconds in forward order with the full visit budget and 0.047 seconds in reverse order. The fixture uses the first prime on each side at 46 bits, sample zero, with the SHAKE seed `streamed-sum-join-holdout-v1`. Both traversal directions used the same caps, fixed before either run. [Fixtures](../benchmarks/streamed-sum-join.json) · [Input regeneration and tests](../tests/test_streamed_sum_join.py)

The smaller right table is indexed by powers. On the left we walk the residue choices depth first, carrying a power on the current path. The first matching sum is checked immediately. If it is wrong, any second match supplies a gap for the witness calculation above. Thus, with enough work allowed on the gap, the search finishes no later than the row containing the actual sum. Suppose that row has one-based rank $t$ among $A$ left rows. Reversing every digit order puts that same row at rank $A+1-t$. A fair choice between the two directions therefore costs at most $(A+1)/2$ left visits on average for **each fixed input**, even when its candidate sums cluster. Building the right table is a separate expense. In the 92-bit case the two actual ranks were 22,985 and 41,528. On the constructed 54-bit case, two wrong sums in the very first row sufficed in both directions. At 2,048 bits even the smaller table remained far above the cap. [Row-count proof](../PrimeFactors/StreamedSumBudget.lean) · [Streaming implementation](../experiments/streamed_sum_join.py)

## Pairing entries to halve the stored table

The right-hand table contains pairs that can share an entry. On the same 92-bit number, pairing cut the table from 37,422 entries to **18,711**. The correct sum still appeared at row 22,985. A second input from the same generator, fixed as sample one before measuring, gave **39,492,927,787,793 × 47,616,693,152,947** after 28,022 rows with 17,820 entries. Both passed a 20,000-entry cap that stopped the ordinary search before its first row. On the two 2,048-bit inputs the paired tables still required approximately $2^{446.55}$ and $2^{447.01}$ entries. In both cases the run stopped before building the table. [Reproducible comparisons](../benchmarks/trace-folded-right.json)

For the timing comparison, both searches had room for 40,000 entries and visited the same rows in reverse order. Times are medians of five local discovery runs. Peak allocations are from a separate run using Python’s allocation tracker. Carrying both a modular power and its inverse saved memory at a small time cost in these examples.

Here is the symmetry behind the saving. If $x$ and $z$ multiply to $N$ modulo a sieve modulus, then $-x$ and $-z$ also multiply to $N$. Thus the admissible local sums come in pairs $s,-s$. Write $M$ for the full sieve period and $L$ for the lower bound on the factor sum. In the right coordinates choose the CRT shift $C=-L$, with $C=0$ in the left coordinates. The right partial sums are then represented by $C\pm t$ modulo $M$ with $0\le t\le\lfloor M/2\rfloor$. Store one entry for each pair. If there are $R$ original entries and $F$ fixed points under negation, this takes $(R+F)/2$ entries. The implementation generates one representative directly by fixing the orientation at the first local digit that differs from its negative. Zero and an even midpoint each receive a single orientation. [Finite counting and trace proof](../PrimeFactors/TraceFoldedRight.lean) · [Enumeration and work counters](../experiments/trace_folded_right.py)

For a candidate left position $a$, an in-range sum has the form $L+a+C\pm t-kM$. Put $u=g^t$ and $y=g^{N+1-L-a-C+kM}$. The search indexes $u$ by $T(u)=u+u^{-1}$ modulo $N$. Since $T(u)=T(u^{-1})$, one query covers either orientation for each possible wrap $k$. An equal trace satisfies $y(T(y)-T(u))=(y-u)(y-u^{-1})=0$. If $y=u$ or $y=u^{-1}$, test the corresponding sum. Otherwise $\gcd(y-u,N)$ is a proper factor. On $372{,}221$ a single query row gave $449$ this way, and Lean verified $372{,}221=449\times829$. The ordinary run visited the same row without a match. Brent, Kruppa and Zimmermann used this reciprocal identity and symmetric CRT progressions in stage two of the $p\pm1$ methods. Galbraith, Wang and Zhang used inversion classes to shorten baby-step/giant-step tables. Here the CRT shift preserves admissible factor sums, and a collision with different orientations at the unknown primes can itself split the product. [Brent, Kruppa and Zimmermann, §8.3.1](https://maths-people.anu.edu.au/~brent/pd/rpb264.pdf#page=15) · [Galbraith, Wang and Zhang, §3](https://www.aimsciences.org/article/doi/10.3934/amc.2017038) · [Checked example and budgets](../benchmarks/trace-folded-right.json)

## Why a second sign choice can erase information

Could the same trick replace four entries with one? Suppose the lookup uses one fixed key on every power $g^x$, gives the same key to a power and its inverse, and also gives the same key to exponents $x$ and $2r-x$ for a fixed second center $r$. Composing the two reflections shows that shifting $x$ by $2r$ never changes the key. If $g$ has order $d$, shifting by $d$ also preserves the key. The key therefore has period $h=\gcd(d,2r)$, and ordinary reflection leaves at most $\lfloor h/2\rfloor+1$ different values. A small $h$ makes many unrelated candidates look identical.

For an exact example, work modulo 17 with $u=9$ and $v=3$. The four products $u^{\pm1}v^{\pm1}$ are 3, 6, 10 and 12. A key invariant under inversion and the second reflection $y\mapsto 9/y$ must give 5 the same key as 3. This lookup alone would accept 5, a false match. The theorem assumes both invariances on every power. An index designed for a particular table, multiple lookups, or a separate test for each match could still preserve the missing information. [Lean period bound and finite counterexample](../PrimeFactors/TwoReflectionKeys.lean)

## What the bounds pay for

If there are $H\ge2$ distinct matching sums, the gaps between consecutive ones are positive multiples of $h$. Hence $(H-1)h\le W$. Choose a budget $K$ with $K^3\ge W$. When $H\le K$, at most $K$ square tests cover every match. Otherwise $h\le K^2$, so trial division through $K$ is sufficient to factor $h$. This budget starts **after the join** and separately charges the range summaries and modular order tests. Classical deterministic factorization can also be applied to the smaller integer $h\le W$; a fourth-root algorithm costs $h^{1/4+o(1)}$ bit operations. The known deterministic bounds for factoring $N$ directly remain the relevant comparison for an overall complexity claim. [Packing and trial-work proofs](../PrimeFactors/GeometricGapBudget.lean) · [Harvey’s deterministic factorization bound and earlier algorithms](https://arxiv.org/abs/2010.05450)

A separate dense search bisects the full index interval using gcds of geometric products to locate the first occupied position and, when needed, its successor. Disagreement between the two primes yields a factor directly. These queries each require a geometric product. The sparse join instead pays for its two CRT lists and uses a gap that can skip any number of intermediate positions. [Dense search classification](../PrimeFactors/GeometricOrderReadout.lean) · [Measured cost of geometric products](reading-an-index-from-a-geometric-product.md)

For **each fixed pair** of distinct 1,024-bit primes, fewer than a $2^{-884}$ fraction of unit bases have equal local orders $d$ with $d^2\le W$. These are the only bases for which the completed one-base join, order tests and square-modulus step can still leave ambiguity. Some of them can succeed as well. The probability is over a uniform choice of the base for that particular product. It concerns a completed computation; the probability gives no guarantee for a run stopped at its table or trial cap. At least half of all unit bases are Fermat witnesses, and their witness status can be checked before spending anything on the join. [Exact exceptional count, half-witness bound and fixed-input estimate](../PrimeFactors/GeometricOrderFailure.lean)

## Where Dirichlet GRH enters

The bases satisfying $g^{N-1}=1\pmod N$ form a proper subgroup of the units. Under Dirichlet GRH, Theorem 1.1(2) of Lamzouri, Li and Soundararajan puts a prime outside that subgroup at or below $(\log N)^2$ whenever $N\ge3000$ has no prime divisor below that threshold. Two 1,024-bit prime factors satisfy this condition, since $(\log N)^2<(2048\log 2)^2<2{,}016{,}400$. Thus one can test a deterministic finite list of prime bases. Each preflight result is verifiable by ordinary modular arithmetic. The repository’s finite theorem takes the outside-prime conclusion as a hypothesis; the link from the independently checked Dirichlet zero-location statements to that conclusion uses the published analytic theorem. The resulting bound pays for finding a base and leaves the table sizes above in force. [Lamzouri, Li and Soundararajan, Theorem 1.1(2)](https://arxiv.org/html/1309.3595v3#S1.SS1) · [Sutherland’s independent verification of the shorter Lean release](https://math.mit.edu/~drew/shortgrh/) · [Finite subgroup deduction](../PrimeFactors/GeometricOrderFailure.lean)
