# Ruling out an interval of divisors

**On each of two 2,048-bit products, Lean certifies that a specific interval of $2^{513}+1$ consecutive integers contains no divisor.** Three small residue checks suffice on one product and four on the other, once the interval and the residue tables have been computed. The intervals lie strictly inside the possible smaller-factor ranges. They cover about one part in $2^{508}$ and $2^{509}$ of the respective ranges. Both products remain unfactored by this search. [Fresh exclusion certificate](https://marius-bughiu.github.io/prime-factors/proofs/weighted-divisor-excludes-fresh-2048.lean) · [Crossed exclusion certificate](https://marius-bughiu.github.io/prime-factors/proofs/weighted-divisor-excludes-crossed-2048.lean)

## From an interval to a short list of sums

Knowing only an odd product $N$, choose positive integer weights $a,b$. A divisor $d$ and its partner $N/d$ would give an integer sum $S=ad+bN/d$ and an integer square

$$S^2-4abN=(ad-bN/d)^2.$$

On a positive interval $[L,U]$ the sum is convex in $d$. Its maximum is at one of the two endpoints and its minimum is at least $2\sqrt{abN}$. Both factors are odd, so $S$ has the parity of $a+b$. These facts give a finite list of integers to check. If each discriminant is a nonsquare modulo some small integer, no divisor can occur anywhere in the interval. This handles all divisors in the interval and uses no advice about the prime factors. The general Lean theorem also permits intervals on one side of the minimum with an independently proved lower trace bound. [Finite-window theorem and full-size instances](../PrimeFactors/WeightedDivisorExclusion.lean)

Both experiments use $a=5$, $b=4$ and $c=\lfloor\sqrt{4N/5}\rfloor$. The interval is $[c-2^{512},c+2^{512}]$. The weights place its center inside both public ranges. Write $S_0=10c+9$. All possible sums and their discriminant remainders appear below.

| Input | Possible sums | Modulus | Remainders of $S^2-80N$ |
| --- | --- | ---: | --- |
| Fresh 2,048-bit product | $S_0,S_0+2,S_0+4$ | 23 | 5, 20, 20 |
| Crossed 2,048-bit product | $S_0,S_0+2,S_0+4,S_0+6$ | 33 | 7, 23, 14, 13 |

None of the listed remainders is a square modulo its modulus. The moduli were selected by inspecting these publicly computed remainders. Building the two tables takes 12 and 17 small squarings respectively, using symmetry about zero. Computing the center and the first possible sum also takes integer square roots. The linked certificates state the exact interval endpoints in decimal and quantify over every integer in the stated range. The benchmark retains each 2,048-bit cofactor. [Fresh computation](https://marius-bughiu.github.io/prime-factors/#weighted-divisor-excludes-fresh-2048) · [Crossed computation](https://marius-bughiu.github.io/prime-factors/#weighted-divisor-excludes-crossed-2048)

The weighted difference-of-squares method is classical. Lehman used it to cover many divisor intervals, and Hiary developed a continued-fraction method to test short intervals near arbitrary prescribed centers. The project's earlier Lean example rejected fourteen consecutive integers with one weighted sum. The added proof checks a whole window of weighted sums at once and produces explicit interior exclusions on both large controls. It gives no reason to expect the unknown factors near these particular centers. [Earlier one-sum proof](../PrimeFactors/IntervalGap.lean) · [Lehman, *Factoring Large Integers*](https://doi.org/10.1090/S0025-5718-1974-0340163-2) · [Hiary, *A deterministic algorithm for integer factorization*](https://arxiv.org/abs/1408.2608)

The same calculation can find a divisor. On $34387$ with weights $(2,1)$ and interval $[123,139]$ the only possible sum is 525. Its discriminant is $23^2$, and the oriented roots give the in-interval divisor 137. Lean certifies $34387=137\times251$ and the primality of both factors. A separate test on $122821$ stops after three of 36 sums and makes no exclusion claim. [Small recovery](https://marius-bughiu.github.io/prime-factors/#weighted-divisor-recovery-34387) · [Capped run](https://marius-bughiu.github.io/prime-factors/#weighted-divisor-cutoff-122821) · [Reproducible experiment](../experiments/weighted_divisor_exclusion.py)

## Choosing the location first

For a product of two distinct 1,024-bit primes, **any integer center in the feasible smaller-factor range** admits an interval of radius $W=2^{340}$ that needs at most one discriminant square test. The calculation first chooses weights for the requested center. This gives shorter intervals than the $5,4$ examples above, but lets us specify where to look. It applies Hiary's continued-fraction method. Our contribution is the Lean guarantee, explicit target-size count, and publicly checkable exclusions at prescribed locations. [Hiary's paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/1408.2608) · [Lean coverage and candidate theorem](../PrimeFactors/ArbitraryDivisorWindow.lean)

For a positive integer radius $W$ and a center $c$ with $c^2\le N\le2c^2$ and $4W^3<c-W$, choose positive integers $A,B$ with $B\le2W$ and $|Ac^2-BN|(2W+1)\le c^2$. Dirichlet approximation guarantees such weights. The program uses the last continued-fraction convergent of $N/c^2$ with denominator at most $2W$, and checks its error. For $d=c+x$ the exact change of the weighted sum from its value at the center is

$$Ad+BN/d-(Ac+BN/c)=\frac{(Ac^2-BN)x}{c^2}+\frac{BNx^2}{c^2d}.$$

For $|x|\le W$, the first term lies strictly between $-1/2$ and $1/2$ and the second lies between zero and one. Thus the sum lies in an open interval of width two. For an odd divisor $d$ of odd $N$, the sum is an integer of known parity. Only one such integer can fit. Its discriminant gives at most two candidates, and exact division checks them. Coefficient selection, integer arithmetic and candidate checks add to the cost of the square test. [Derivation in Lean](../PrimeFactors/ArbitraryDivisorWindow.lean) · [Integer implementation](../experiments/arbitrary_divisor_window.py)

Cover $[2^{1023},2^{1024})$ with $2^{682}$ bins of $2^{341}$ consecutive integers. Clamp each bin midpoint to the feasible range between $\max(2^{1023},\lceil\sqrt{N/2}\rceil)$ and $\lfloor\sqrt N\rfloor$. The smaller factor is within $2^{340}$ of at least one of these public centers. Hence **at most $2^{682}$ window tests suffice on every target pair**, each requiring a weight calculation and at most one discriminant square test. This is another complete search alongside the earlier $2^{692}$ signed-arc bound. A separate quotient search gives fewer than $2^{678}$ cells at 2,048 bits. These methods count different operations. The entire new list is still vastly beyond a practical run. [Complete address theorem](../PrimeFactors/ArbitraryDivisorWindow.lean) · [Earlier arc search](counting-all-factor-arcs.md) · [Cost of a quotient cell](quotient-search.md)

We picked the lower quartile of the public equal-width factor range on each full-size control before selecting the weights. The calculations took 192 and 193 continued-fraction steps, respectively. One discriminant test ruled out the complete $2^{341}+1$-integer interval on each input. Independently, the downloadable proofs check the permitted trace and its nonsquare remainder, 3 modulo 9 on the fresh input and 3 modulo 7 on the crossed input. These intervals are disjoint from the larger $5,4$ intervals on the respective products. The same quartile rule on $2501$, at center 42 and radius 2, recovered $41\times61$.
