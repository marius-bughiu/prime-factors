# Recovering two large primes when their sum is only approximately known

We recovered both 1,024-bit primes of a constructed 2,048-bit number even though their sum missed our guess by a **121-digit integer** (399 bits). The exact-sum search tried all 647 exponents without finding a factor. Given the product and an error allowance of $2^{400}$, the new search visited four candidate bands and solved four degree-four polynomial problems. It took about 2.4 seconds locally to discover the factors. Lean checked that both are prime, that their product is correct, and that their sum differs from every exact candidate. [Measured example and downloadable proof](https://marius-bughiu.github.io/prime-factors/#near-smooth-399-2048) · [Full decimal input and results](../benchmarks/near-smooth-sum.json)

The example was built to have a sum near a number of the form $2^a3^b$. That condition is still extraordinarily rare. At this tolerance, among uniformly selected unordered pairs of distinct 1,024-bit primes conditioned on a 2,048-bit product, fewer than one pair in $2^{598}$ can lie this close even to a catalog of 648 fixed even centers. The 647 in-range centers and the upper endpoint together fit this bound. A random product of two large primes remains out of reach. ## How the search uses an approximate sum

From the product $N$ we generate candidate sums $C$ and intersect each band $[C-E,C+E]$ with the possible sums for two equal-width factors. For an ordered prime pair $p\le q$, the larger factor is $(S+\sqrt{S^2-4N})/2$ at the true sum $S$. This expression increases with $S$. Evaluating it at the ends of the band and rounding toward its interior gives a whole-number interval for $q$. The smaller root decreases with $S$ and gives a corresponding interval for $p$. Close to $2\sqrt N$, the same sum error can give a much wider interval. All interval endpoints are computed from the public input. [Exact directed-root equivalence](../PrimeFactors/NearSmoothSumWindows.lean) · [Earlier estimate for an approximate totient](approximate-totient-squares.md)

For a narrow interval one weighted sum can test all possible divisors in it. For a wider interval the implementation uses lattice reduction to construct a polynomial that must vanish at the offset of any divisor there. It checks the coefficient conditions, isolates the integer roots and tries the resulting divisors. The four quartics on the 399-bit-offset example required 18 geometry square roots, 2,618 lattice swaps, 4,542 size reductions and 2,391 root-isolation nodes. There was one candidate divisor and one final factor confirmation. Scanning the same four bands in the same order, testing each allowed sum modulo 24 up to the winning sum, would take more than $10^{120}$ square tests. This count compares two ways of using the sum promise. ## Which products and centers the theory covers

A sum near the edge of the range could in principle be closer to a power product just outside it. Lean proves that for an error up to $2^{437}$ the only extra possibility is $2^{1025}$. In that case the primes are so close to each other that the first square test in Fermat’s method recovers them. The two older comparison products each rule out that extra center by an integer inequality on the product alone. Together with their downloadable proofs, this excludes **every** sum near any $2^a3^b$ at tolerance $2^{400}$ for those two inputs. [Boundary proof and first-square recovery](../PrimeFactors/NearSmoothBoundary.lean)

The 647 centers span the interval from $2^{1024}$ up to $2^{1025}$. Any two differ by more than $2^{1024}/977$. For any fixed product with balanced factors, at most 56 centers can meet the balanced sum range even if each center may err by $2^{1012}$. The spacing proof certifies an ordering of the normalized powers of 3 with integer comparisons. At tolerance $2^{435}$, for every target product, only the smallest center that survives the public filter can fail the seven-row width bound. Each other active band has an equation with at most six integer roots. The theorem proves existence. The implementation checks whether the row it actually constructs meets its height bound and records any unfinished bands. [Spacing and balanced-range theorem](../PrimeFactors/SmoothCenterSpacing.lean) · [Uniform one-exception result](../PrimeFactors/NearSmoothUniform.lean)

At $E=2^{400}$, for all except fewer than one in $2^{55}$ target prime pairs, each feasible center fits a single seven-row equation with at most six integer roots. This second bound counts products on which the interval can be too wide. It uses the same prime-count estimate as the $2^{-598}$ bound. Most products pass the width test and have no factor in any of the intervals. On the two earlier controls Lean also proves that at $E=2^{435}$ the seven-row construction gives at most 42 and 228 candidate values respectively. ## How the two positive inputs were chosen

Both examples use the same center $2^{543}3^{304}$. We added $2+2^{80}t$, choosing $t$ from a fixed hash seed for each example. We searched arithmetic progressions for two primes with that sum and with easily certified divisors of their predecessors. The two products are coprime. Their shared construction explains why both succeed. The factor search receives only $N$ and $E$. It receives neither the center, the offset, the progression, nor the factors. The displayed discovery times exclude construction and certificate checking. Run `python3 -m experiments.near_smooth_control 2 --replay` to reproduce the 399-bit-offset selection.
