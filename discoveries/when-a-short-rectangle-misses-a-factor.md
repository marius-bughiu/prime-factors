# When a short rectangle misses a factor

For $2{,}491=47\cdot53$, a rectangle of six numerators and six denominators finds a factor for 1,127 of the 1,196 eligible targets. Moving one entry from the numerator list to the denominator list gives a $5\times7$ rectangle. It tests one fewer pair and finds a factor for **1,150** targets. The trade has winners and losers. Target 13 works only in the second grid, where $1^2-13\cdot7^2=-636$ has gcd 53 with the product. Target 29 works only in the first. All these counts use targets with Jacobi symbol $-1$ at this fixed product. ## Which extra cells matter?

The test at position $(i,j)$ computes $\gcd(i^2-aj^2,N)$. For a product of two distinct odd primes and a negative-Jacobi target $a$, only one prime admits a square root of $a$. Denominators below both primes guarantee that every hit reveals precisely that prime. Write $H,D$ for the lengths of the two lists and $C=(H+1)(D+1)$ for the coverage threshold. Suppose the prime that admits a square root is $p=C+s$ with $1\le s\le\min(H,D)$. The earlier result identifies exactly one missing square for each divisor of $s$. [Original coverage theorem and assumptions](which-squares-a-rational-rectangle-misses.md)

**In column $D+1$, the necessary and sufficient extra numerators are exactly the positive divisors of $s$.** A missed root has the form $\pm(H+1)/t$ modulo $p$ for $t\mid s$. Multiplying that root by $D+1$ gives $\mp s/t$ because $C\equiv-s\pmod p$. As $t$ runs over the divisors, so does $s/t$. Distinct positive numerators through $H$ reach distinct squares in this column. If a contiguous prefix is easier to test, its minimum length is exactly $s$. At 2,491, $53=49+4$. Positions 1, 2 and 4 in column 7 each rescue 23 targets. Those three cells complete the original grid for all 1,196 targets. [Column identity, minimality and union of two rectangles](../PrimeFactors/CircleMarkRationalFringe.lean)

When the prime is hidden, the exact shortfall is hidden too. A public upper bound $L\le\min(H,D)$ still gives a correction. Take the union of the divisor sets of all offsets $s\le L$ for which $C+s$ is prime. For $C=100$ and $L=9$, the required numerators are 1, 3, 7 and 9. The same interval offers four primes for direct divisibility tests, namely 101, 103, 107 and 109. In general each candidate prime contributes its own distinct offset, so directly testing all the candidate primes takes at most as many scalar tests as the smallest guaranteed new column. Identifying which offsets are prime is work for either comparison. [Public window and cost bound](../PrimeFactors/CircleMarkRationalFringe.lean)

For **any specified prime** $p\ge5$ the formula applies at the last balanced list length before complete coverage. Put $z=\lfloor\sqrt p\rfloor$. Start with $H=D=z-1$ when $p<z(z+1)$, or $H=z-1,D=z$ otherwise. The shortfall always falls in the divisor formula’s range. No rectangle with this total list length covers every root. Adding one entry to the shorter list suffices. This way of choosing the rectangle uses the prime itself. [Last balanced budget](../PrimeFactors/CircleMarkLastBalancedRectangle.lean)

## What can the product tell us cheaply?

One public choice is the square with $H=D=h=\lfloor N^{1/4}\rfloor$. Suppose the larger prime is $q=(h+1)^2+s$ with $1\le s\le h$. The smaller prime lies below the threshold. For denominators below both primes the fraction of negative targets that succeed is exactly $1-\tau(s)/(q-1)$, where $\tau(s)$ is the number of positive divisors of $s$. At $8{,}453=79\cdot107$, the fraction is $4{,}056/4{,}134=52/53$. For an individual positive target, $as+1<q$ guarantees success. At $2{,}173=41\cdot53$, target 13 attains equality and misses. [Mixed-prime count and sharp target cutoff](../PrimeFactors/CircleMarkRationalMixedBoundary.lean)

**Ordinary Fermat factoring takes at most five square tests on every odd prime pair in this public square’s shortfall range.** Its starting point is the rounded-up square root of $N$. The proof bounds the distance from the larger prime to that public value, which puts the true midpoint $(p+q)/2$ within four increments. At $2{,}562{,}569$, the first four tests miss and the fifth gives $1{,}605^2-N=116^2$, hence the factors 1,489 and 1,721. If both primes lie within $\min(H,D)$ of the same rectangle boundary, even one chosen with knowledge of the primes, the first Fermat test already recovers them. [Public square planner and fifth-test certificate](../PrimeFactors/CircleMarkSquarePlanner.lean) · [Two-prime band proof](../PrimeFactors/CircleMarkRationalMidpoint.lean)

Among all 7,995 full-width products of distinct 6- through 11-bit primes of equal width, 1,307 meet the public square condition. Actual Fermat searches on those inputs needed one, two, three, four and five tests on 631, 394, 203, 76 and 3 products respectively. The census used the known factors to classify inputs. Fermat received the product. [Reproducible census](../experiments/rectangle_boundary_census.py)

## How often can both full-size primes be near the boundary?

Count each pair of distinct 1,024-bit primes with a 2,048-bit product once. The fraction for which **any** common rectangle can place both primes just above its threshold is between $2^{-516}$ and $2^{-496}$. The lower bound already holds for one rule that chooses its rectangle from $N$. It sets $W=3\cdot2^{510}$, $H=W-1$, and $D=\lfloor\sqrt N/W\rfloor-1$. Prime pairs in the same interval between consecutive multiples of $W$ supply the lower bound. Any rectangle fixed independently of the input covers fewer than one in $2^{999}$ pairs this way. Allowing the primes on either side of the same boundary gives an upper bound of $2^{-495}$. These counts use unconditional prime estimates. Every pair counted in these boundary bands already splits on the first Fermat test.
