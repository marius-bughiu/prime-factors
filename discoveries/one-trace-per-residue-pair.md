# One weighted sum per pair of remainders

Given only an odd number, we can find every integer factor pair whose larger member is at most twice its smaller member. Group the possible factors by their remainders. In each group the product tells us how to weight the two factors so that their weighted sum has at most **one** possible value. One quadratic equation then checks the entire group. The number of groups still grows exponentially with the bit length. [Lean arithmetic](../PrimeFactors/MidpointWeights.lean) · [Existence and four-vector search](../PrimeFactors/MidpointWeightLattice.lean)

For 34,387 choose base 18. There are four possible unordered pairs of remainders. Three yield an empty interval for their weighted sum. The remaining pair has remainders 11 and 17, and the only possible value of three times the sum of the factors is 1,164. Hence their sum is 388. The quadratic with that sum and product has roots **137 and 251**. [Checked example](../benchmarks/midpoint-weights.json)

## Why a single check suffices

Write the factors as $x=h-d$, $y=h+d$. Either order is allowed. The balance condition is $3|d|\le h$. The product $N=h^2-d^2$ then gives the integer bounds

$$h_0=\lceil\sqrt N\rceil\le h\le h_1=\lfloor\sqrt{9N/8}\rfloor,\qquad |d|\le D=\lfloor\sqrt{N/8}\rfloor.$$

Put $H=h_1-h_0$ and $G=2D$. An empty midpoint interval needs no search, and a single midpoint needs one square test. For a nonempty interval of positive width choose a base $R$ coprime to $N$ with $R^3>4HG$. Knowing $x\equiv u\pmod R$ fixes $y\equiv w=Nu^{-1}\pmod R$. Set $s=wu^{-1}\bmod R$. Choose weights $a,b$, at least one nonzero, satisfying $b\equiv sa\pmod R$. On this entire group the sum $t=ay+bx$ has one known remainder modulo $R^2$. To compute it, take $k=(N-uw)/R$, $i=u^{-1}\bmod R$, and use $a(w+Rik)+bu$. The product equality gives the congruence even when neither digit is the true factor. [Congruence and quadratic proof](../PrimeFactors/MidpointWeights.lean)

The sum equals $(a+b)h+(a-b)d$. Across the whole allowed rectangle its width is $H|a+b|+G|a-b|$. To see why suitable weights always exist, take the lattice generated by $(1,s),(0,R)$ and transform $(a,b)$ to $(H(a+b)+G(a-b),\ H(a+b)-G(a-b))$. The greatest absolute coordinate of the image is exactly the width. The transformed lattice has index $4HGR$. There are $R^4$ integer points in the square with both coordinates between 0 and $R^2-1$. Two points must be in the same lattice class. Their nonzero difference gives a width strictly below $R^2$. In Lean an explicit finite encoding proves the count. In the search, two-dimensional Gauss reduction followed by four vector choices finds weights with the guarantee. Every computed weight is also checked against the width and congruence. The threshold $4HG/N$ tends to $3-2\sqrt2$ for large $N$. [Lattice proof](../PrimeFactors/MidpointWeightLattice.lean) · [Implementation](../experiments/midpoint_weights.py)

There is room for at most one integer $t$ having the required remainder. If it exists, $b x^2-tx+aN=0$. The search handles negative, zero and repeated roots, checks divisibility and the original product, and reports each balanced pair once. A further identity shows that $R^2$ divides $t^2-4abN$ even for false candidates. Testing the quotient for a square gives the same answer. [Discriminant proof](../PrimeFactors/ReducedWeightDiscriminant.lean)

## Measurements

The first eight distinct pairs from two independent streams of 25-bit primes were fixed by the seed `midpoint-weight-holdout-v1`. Each method received just the product and searched the complete balanced interval. The times below sum per-input medians of five runs each in rotating order on an arm64 Mac using CPython 3.9.6. Lean checked every factorization. [All timings and operation counts](../research/midpoint-weight-holdout.json) · [Sampler and verification](../experiments/midpoint_weight_holdout.py)

| Method | Work across all eight products | Total of median times |
| --- | --- | ---: |
| Odd trial division | 30,640,850 divisor tests | 1.003 s |
| Previous primorial-base search | 376,320 remainder classes, 2,880,536 Euclidean steps | 1.656 s |
| One weighted sum per remainder pair | 41,280 remainder pairs, 23,956 candidate sums, 20,337 integer square roots | 0.058 s |
| Same sums, first dividing out the known square and testing small moduli | 326 integer square roots | 0.073 s |

The version doing fewer square roots took longer on these small inputs. The earlier search's 3,181 candidate pairs and the new search's 23,956 candidate sums measure different operations. The improvement here came from processing far fewer groups and avoiding recursive interval counts. [Earlier comparison](affine-radices.md)

On a separate draw, the first two 41-bit primes from the seed `one-trace-scale-v1` gave $3{,}043{,}806{,}426{,}885{,}490{,}749{,}164{,}321$. The complete search visited 7,188,480 groups and found **1,688,035,255,729 × 1,803,165,198,449** in one run of 7.75 seconds. GNU coreutils `factor` found the same factors in 0.025 seconds, including startup (median of nine runs). [Reproducible input, operation counts and factors](../benchmarks/midpoint-weights.json)

## How much work remains at 2,048 bits?

For any base satisfying $R^3>4HG$, a complete search through these groups requires **more than $2^{676}$ remainder pairs** on every odd 2,048-bit input. Pairing a unit $u$ with $N/u$ covers at most two units. The lower bound follows from the rounded interval widths, the cube condition, and a bound on the fraction of residues coprime to $R$. It applies to this method of enumerating the groups. On the specific 2,048-bit benchmark, the exact count exceeds $2^{677}$. We visited 128 groups, obtained 84 candidate sums and found neither factor. For the 69 nonnegative quadratic discriminants, removing the known square reduced the largest square-test input from 2,731 bits to 1,368. The small-modulus checks left two square roots to compute. On this input even the unvisited groups have square-test inputs of at most 1,369 bits after division. [Proof of the size bound](reduced-weight-discriminants.md) [Full-size budget theorem](../PrimeFactors/MidpointWeightBudget.lean) · [Capped fixture](../benchmarks/midpoint-weights.json)

Lenstra showed how to recover divisors efficiently once their residue class modulo a sufficiently large base is supplied. Harvey and Hittmeir use stationary linear combinations and lattice reduction in a different factoring algorithm with a better asymptotic exponent. Our calculation uses the balanced midpoint bound on both orders at once and tests what this specialization saves. It is unconditional. The code and proofs for this construction were written in the repository. [Lenstra, *Divisors in residue classes*](https://pub.math.leidenuniv.nl/~lenstrahw/PUBLICATIONS/1984b/art.pdf) · [Harvey and Hittmeir, Proposition 3.3](https://arxiv.org/abs/2105.11105)

Grouping several remainder pairs that have the same slope reduced the paired 82-bit search from 8.63 to 4.42 seconds. On the full-size control even this grouped search still needs more than $2^{585}$ distinct slopes. [Slope counts and timings](weight-slope-batches.md)
