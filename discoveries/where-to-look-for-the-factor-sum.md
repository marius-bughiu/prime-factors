# Where to look first for the factor sum

For two 1,024-bit primes whose product has 2,048 bits, the product tells us how to choose a central window occupying at most a quarter of the possible sum span. **About half of uniformly drawn eligible prime pairs have their sum in that window.** This predicts how often a shortened Fermat-style search will cover the answer. A particular product can miss. On our two full-size inputs the central windows alone still contained more than $2^{1015}$ integer positions. [Finite cutoff proof](../PrimeFactors/FactorSumQuantile.lean) · [Search and controls](../benchmarks/factor-sum-quantile.json)

## Computing the cutoff without the primes

Suppose $N=pq$, with $p\le q<B=2^b$, and $B^2/2\le N<B^2$. The known factor width puts the sum $S=p+q$ between $2\sqrt N$ and $B+N/B$. In this interval the central cutoff is

$$S_* = \sqrt{2N+(B+N/B)\sqrt N}.$$

Computing its floor takes two integer square roots and a division. The search needs only $N$ and $b$. The integer endpoint for the full search is $\lceil B+N/B\rceil-1$ because $q<B$. Lean proves that $S\le S_*$ for the actual factors exactly when $q^3\le B^2p$. It also proves $S_*-2\sqrt N\le (B+N/B-2\sqrt N)/4$ via the corresponding real inequality. The span statement concerns continuous lengths. Integer position counts include their endpoints. [Lean definitions and theorems](../PrimeFactors/FactorSumQuantile.lean) · [Integer evaluation](../experiments/factor_sum_quantile.py)

To see where the median comes from, write $n=N/B^2$, $a=\log(q/\sqrt N)$ and $H=\log(1/\sqrt n)$. Treat the factors momentarily as real coordinates. Then $(p/B,q/B)=(\sqrt n\,e^{-a},\sqrt n\,e^a)$, with Jacobian of absolute value one. For a fixed normalized product $1/2\le n<1$, the allowed range is $0\le a\le H$ and the median in area is $H/2$. The sum $2\sqrt N\cosh(a)$ at that median gives $S_*$. More generally the lowest fraction $t$ in logarithmic gap takes fraction $[\sinh(tH/2)/\sinh(H/2)]^2\le t^2$ of the sum span. The prime number theorem transfers this area law asymptotically on every fixed product bin of positive width as $b$ increases. Conditioning on a single exact integer product would already determine its prime pair.

## A finite population estimate at 2,048 bits

Draw two primes independently and uniformly from $[X,B]$ with $X=2^{1023}$ and $B=2X$. Keep distinct pairs with $pq\ge B^2/2$ and sort the pair. The following estimates apply to this population. Both are analytic deductions. The finite cutoff and width inequalities above are checked in Lean.

Let $\mu$ count primes and $d\nu(x)=dx/\log x$ on $[X,B]$. Split the eligible triangle $p<q$ into the region $G$ with $q^3\le B^2p$ and its complement $J$. Every horizontal and vertical section of either region is an interval, possibly empty. If the discrepancy between $\mu$ and $\nu$ on every interval is at most $\epsilon$, replacing one measure at a time bounds the count error on each region by $E=\epsilon(2\nu([X,B])+\epsilon)$. Write $D=\nu^2(G)+\nu^2(J)$. Perturbing the good and missed counts by at most $E$ each changes the fraction by at most $E/D$ when both counts exceed $E$. Its extreme values occur with errors of opposite signs.

For an unconditional bound, put $l=\log x$ and $F(x)=x/(l-1-1/l)$. Dusart's Corollary 5.3, (5.6), bounds $\pi(x)$ between $F(x)$ and $x/(l-1-1.2311/l)$ throughout this band. Since $709<l<710$, the difference of those bounds is less than $X/700000000$. Direct differentiation gives $1/l-F'(x)=(3/l+1/l^2)/(l(l-1-1/l)^2)<1/80000000000$. Subtracting the two endpoint estimates and allowing two units for open or closed endpoints gives $\epsilon<X/680000000$. Also $\nu([X,B])\le X/709$ and the sorted eligible area is $(1-\log 2)X^2$, so $D>0.306X^2/710^2$. Together these imply $E/D<0.000006834$. [Dusart, Corollary 5.3](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11139-016-9839-4)

The smooth measure differs slightly from area. At fixed $n$ its density in $a$ is proportional to $1/(c^2-a^2)$ with $c=\log\sqrt N$. Put $\lambda=H/c\le 1/2047$. The smooth central fraction is exactly $\operatorname{atanh}(\lambda/2)/\operatorname{atanh}(\lambda)$. Its deficit from $1/2$ lies between $(1-\lambda^2)\lambda^2/8$ and $\lambda^2/[8(1-\lambda^2)^2]$. For example, compare the integrals of $1/(1-\lambda^2u^2)$ over the two halves of $[0,1]$, pairing $u$ with $u+1/2$. Their integrand difference is $\lambda^2(u+1/4)$ divided by a product between $(1-\lambda^2)^2$ and 1. Thus the deficit is below $2.984\times10^{-8}$ on every fiber. Combining the estimates puts the prime-pair coverage **between 49.9993% and 50.0007% unconditionally**.

The zeta zero-location statement in the RH project supports a sharper deduction through Schoenfeld's published prime-count estimate. Under RH the endpoint error in $\pi(x)-\operatorname{li}(x)$ is at most $\sqrt{x}\log x/(8\pi)$ here. Including endpoints gives interval discrepancy below $61\sqrt B$, hence count error below $130B^{3/2}$ per region by replacing each measure once. The slab $1/2\le n\le9/16$ has area at least $B^2/64$ because $H\ge\log(4/3)>1/4$. Its smooth mass is at least $B^2/(64\cdot710^2)$ and exceeds one ninth of $D$ using total area at most $B^2/8$ and the endpoint density ratio $(1023/1024)^2$. On this slab $\lambda>1/4096$. The smooth bias therefore exceeds $8.27\times10^{-10}$ after averaging all eligible products. The prime-count error divided by $D$ is below $4\times10^{-145}$. Under RH the covered **fraction** lies strictly between $0.499999970$ and $0.4999999995$. This extra precision affects the population estimate. Computing the window for a particular input uses only integer arithmetic. [Schoenfeld, Corollary 1, (6.18)](https://doi.org/10.1090/S0025-5718-1976-0457374-X) · [Pinned zero-location statement](https://github.com/leanprover-community/riemann-hypothesis/blob/40e56bab/src/Statements.lean)

## Does searching the central window first actually help?

We drew pairs of 40-bit primes using SHAKE with seed `factor-sum-quantile-v1` and retained the first eight distinct pairs giving an 80-bit product. On every product we ran the same base-2, reverse-order, folded search three times, once on the full bit-width interval and once on each side of the cutoff. Each run allowed 20,000 table entries and 32,768 row visits. Generating the numbers and comparing answers were separate from the searches. Lean checked all eight factorizations. Certification could use trial division up to one million on prime predecessors after discovery. [Fixtures and partial results](../benchmarks/factor-sum-quantile.json) · [Draw regeneration and endpoint checks](../tests/test_factor_sum_quantile.py)

| Total over eight inputs | Search full interval | Search central first, then remaining interval on a miss |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Factors recovered | 8 pairs | 8 pairs, 4 in the central window |
| Left rows visited | 36,804 | 48,108 |
| Right entries built across the runs | 23,187 | 22,302 |

This comparison exposes the cost of starting a second search on a miss. The central window took 24.87% to 25.00% of the integer positions on the eight inputs. A shorter interval can still use the same sieve moduli as the full interval, leaving the table size unchanged. On the first 2,048-bit control, the central and full tables had the same projected size. On the second, the central cut saved about five bits of projected storage. All four projections exceeded $2^{442}$ entries for these two inputs, so the ordinary storage-capped runs stopped before building any entries. The gain established here is a population ranking of sums and a reproducible way to measure whether using the ranking saves total work. [Search comparisons](../benchmarks/factor-sum-quantile.json)
