# Where to look first for the factor sum

For two 1,024-bit primes whose product has 2,048 bits, the product tells us how to choose a central window occupying at most a quarter of the possible sum span. **About half of uniformly drawn eligible prime pairs have their sum in that window.** This predicts how often a shortened Fermat-style search will cover the answer. A particular product can miss. On our two full-size inputs the central windows alone still contained more than $2^{1015}$ integer positions. [Finite cutoff proof](../PrimeFactors/FactorSumQuantile.lean) · [Search and controls](../benchmarks/factor-sum-quantile.json)

## Computing the cutoff without the primes

Suppose $N=pq$, with $p\le q<B=2^b$, and $B^2/2\le N<B^2$. The known factor width puts the sum $S=p+q$ between $2\sqrt N$ and $B+N/B$. In this interval the central cutoff is

$$S_* = \sqrt{2N+(B+N/B)\sqrt N}.$$

Computing its floor takes two integer square roots and a division. The search needs only $N$ and $b$. The integer endpoint for the full search is $\lceil B+N/B\rceil-1$ because $q<B$. Lean proves that $S\le S_*$ for the actual factors exactly when $q^3\le B^2p$. It also proves $S_*-2\sqrt N\le (B+N/B-2\sqrt N)/4$ via the corresponding real inequality. The span statement concerns continuous lengths. Integer position counts include their endpoints. [Lean definitions and theorems](../PrimeFactors/FactorSumQuantile.lean) · [Integer evaluation](../experiments/factor_sum_quantile.py)

To see where the median comes from, write $n=N/B^2$, $a=\log(q/\sqrt N)$ and $H=\log(1/\sqrt n)$. Treat the factors momentarily as real coordinates. Then $(p/B,q/B)=(\sqrt n\,e^{-a},\sqrt n\,e^a)$, with Jacobian of absolute value one. For a fixed normalized product $1/2\le n<1$, the allowed range is $0\le a\le H$ and the median in area is $H/2$. The sum $2\sqrt N\cosh(a)$ at that median gives $S_*$. More generally the lowest fraction $t$ in logarithmic gap takes fraction $[\sinh(tH/2)/\sinh(H/2)]^2\le t^2$ of the sum span. The prime number theorem transfers this area law asymptotically on every fixed product bin of positive width as $b$ increases. Conditioning on a single exact integer product would already determine its prime pair.

## A finite population estimate at 2,048 bits

A later refinement using Axler’s bound for the Chebyshev theta function places the central fraction between **0.49999996837 and 0.49999999383 unconditionally**. The two derivations below explain the roles of the weaker Dusart estimate and the much smaller RH prime-count error. [Refined unconditional estimate and its effect on prediction](what-the-square-root-reveals-about-the-gap.md#removing-the-rh-assumption-from-the-decision)

Draw two primes independently and uniformly from $[X,B]$ with $X=2^{1023}$ and $B=2X$. Keep distinct pairs with $pq\ge B^2/2$ and sort the pair. The following estimates apply to this population. Both are analytic deductions. The finite cutoff and width inequalities above are checked in Lean.

Let $\mu$ count primes and $d\nu(x)=dx/\log x$ on $[X,B]$. Split the eligible triangle $p<q$ into the region $G$ with $q^3\le B^2p$ and its complement $J$. Every horizontal and vertical section of either region is an interval, possibly empty. If the discrepancy between $\mu$ and $\nu$ on every interval is at most $\epsilon$, replacing one measure at a time bounds the count error on each region by $E=\epsilon(2\nu([X,B])+\epsilon)$. Write $D=\nu^2(G)+\nu^2(J)$. Perturbing the good and missed counts by at most $E$ each changes the fraction by at most $E/D$ when both counts exceed $E$. Its extreme values occur with errors of opposite signs.

For an unconditional bound, put $l=\log x$ and $F(x)=x/(l-1-1/l)$. Dusart's Corollary 5.3, (5.6), bounds $\pi(x)$ between $F(x)$ and $x/(l-1-1.2311/l)$ throughout this band. Since $709<l<710$, the difference of those bounds is less than $X/700000000$. Direct differentiation gives $1/l-F'(x)=(3/l+1/l^2)/(l(l-1-1/l)^2)<1/80000000000$. Subtracting the two endpoint estimates and allowing two units for open or closed endpoints gives $\epsilon<X/680000000$. Also $\nu([X,B])\le X/709$ and the sorted eligible area is $(1-\log 2)X^2$, so $D>0.306X^2/710^2$. Together these imply $E/D<0.000006834$. [Dusart, Corollary 5.3](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11139-016-9839-4)

The smooth measure differs slightly from area. At fixed $n$ its density in $a$ is proportional to $1/(c^2-a^2)$ with $c=\log\sqrt N$. Put $\lambda=H/c\le 1/2047$. The smooth central fraction is exactly $\operatorname{atanh}(\lambda/2)/\operatorname{atanh}(\lambda)$. Its deficit from $1/2$ lies between $(1-\lambda^2)\lambda^2/8$ and $\lambda^2/[8(1-\lambda^2)^2]$. For example, compare the integrals of $1/(1-\lambda^2u^2)$ over the two halves of $[0,1]$, pairing $u$ with $u+1/2$. Their integrand difference is $\lambda^2(u+1/4)$ divided by a product between $(1-\lambda^2)^2$ and 1. Thus the deficit is below $2.984\times10^{-8}$ on every fiber. Combining the estimates puts the prime-pair coverage **between 49.9993% and 50.0007% unconditionally**.

The zeta zero-location statement in the RH project supports a sharper deduction through Schoenfeld's published prime-count estimate. Under RH the endpoint error in $\pi(x)-\operatorname{li}(x)$ is at most $\sqrt{x}\log x/(8\pi)$ here. Including endpoints gives interval discrepancy below $61\sqrt B$, hence count error below $130B^{3/2}$ per region by replacing each measure once. The slab $1/2\le n\le9/16$ has area at least $B^2/64$ because $H\ge\log(4/3)>1/4$. Its smooth mass is at least $B^2/(64\cdot710^2)$ and exceeds one ninth of $D$ using total area at most $B^2/8$ and the endpoint density ratio $(1023/1024)^2$. On this slab $\lambda>1/4096$. The smooth bias therefore exceeds $8.27\times10^{-10}$ after averaging all eligible products. The prime-count error divided by $D$ is below $4\times10^{-145}$. Under RH the covered **fraction** lies strictly between $0.499999970$ and $0.4999999995$. This extra precision affects the population estimate. Computing the window for a particular input uses only integer arithmetic. [Schoenfeld, Corollary 1, (6.18)](https://doi.org/10.1090/S0025-5718-1976-0457374-X) · [Pinned zero-location statement](https://github.com/leanprover-community/riemann-hypothesis/blob/40e56bab/src/Statements.lean)

## What if we are given leading or trailing bits of the product?

The preceding half-and-half split persists even after observing many specified product bits. Under RH, a rule given only the **first 475 bits** of the 2,048-bit product cannot correctly choose the central or the outer window more than **50.000385%** of the time in this population. Under Dirichlet GRH the corresponding bound for the **first 232 and last 232 bits together** is **50.000099%**. The rule can allocate unlimited computing time to these particular observations. A calculation using the other bits of $N$ has more input than this result covers.

For leading bits, fix any normalized bin $u\le N/B^2<u+\delta$ in $[1/2,1]$. Its width is $\delta=2^{-k}$ when $k$ leading bits are specified, including the initial 1. Write $d=1-u\ge\delta$. Both factors lie in $[uB,B]$, a band of length $dB$. Restricting the good or missed region to this bin keeps its horizontal and vertical sections intervals. The smooth mass $D$ in the bin is at least

$$\frac{B^2}{710^2}\int_u^{u+\delta}-\tfrac12\log n\,dn
\ \ge\ \frac{B^2\delta d}{4\cdot710^2}.$$

Indeed $-\log n\ge1-n$ and $d\ge\delta$. This also covers the final bin adjoining $n=1$, where the available factor range shrinks. With $k=475$, Schoenfeld's interval error $\epsilon=61\sqrt B$ is smaller than $dB/709$. The two-measure argument gives $E\le3\epsilon dB/709$, hence $E/D<2^{19}/(\delta\sqrt B)=2^{-18}$. The smooth central fraction in this bin remains between $1/2-2.984\times10^{-8}$ and $1/2$. Choosing the more likely side in *each* bin therefore has accuracy below $0.50000385$. A randomized choice is a weighted average of the two side probabilities in each bin.

For trailing bits, fix in advance $Q=2^s$ and an odd observed residue $r=N\bmod Q$. There are $f=\varphi(Q)=2^{s-1}$ unit residues. For each possible residue $z$ of $p$, the residue of $q$ must be $r/z$. Under Dirichlet GRH, the explicit arithmetic-progression estimate of Ernvall-Hytönen and Palojärvi gives interval error $\epsilon=2^{15}\sqrt B$ per unit class against $dx/(f\log x)$ on the factor band. In their Corollary 2, $x\ge Q\ge3$, both logarithms are below 710 and the displayed endpoint bound is less than $13200\sqrt B$. Two endpoints and two possible boundary atoms fit the stated interval allowance. [Ernvall-Hytönen and Palojärvi, Corollary 2](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2003.01925#page=2) · [Pinned Dirichlet zero-location statements](https://github.com/leanprover-community/riemann-hypothesis/blob/40e56bab/src/Statements.lean)

For one class pair the error in good or missed counts is at most $\epsilon[2dB/(709f)+\epsilon]$. Summing over the $f$ disjoint class pairs leaves total error at most $3\epsilon dB/709$ whenever $\epsilon\le dB/(709f)$. The smooth total is now $D/f$, since all class pairs have the same smooth measure and $z\mapsto r/z$ permutes the units. Consequently the share error is less than $2^{29} f/(\delta\sqrt B)$. For $k+s\le464$ allow the leading 1 to be known even when $k=0$, giving $f/\delta\le2^{464}$ in every case. A slightly tighter estimate handles this endpoint too. Here $\epsilon\le dB/(2\cdot709f)$, so the factor 3 in the error bound can be reduced to $5/2$. Consequently $E/(D/f)<2^{28}f/(\delta\sqrt B)\le2^{-20}$. With no suffix or just its fixed odd bit, the leading-bit bound above suffices. The possible smooth tilt adds less than $2.984\times10^{-8}$. Averaging over all observed bins and odd residues gives the stated classifier bound. It also applies if the rule decides which end to read next after seeing each bit, up to a total of 464 bits. Each path ends in a disjoint prefix-and-suffix cell. Independent random choices give mixtures of such rules. The cutoff itself makes no RH assumption.

The arithmetic-progression estimate also allows the residue of $N$ modulo a general positive integer $Q\le B/2$. All eligible primes are larger than any prime divisor of $Q$. The same upper bound applies whenever $2^k\varphi(Q)\le2^{464}$, counting the leading 1 among $k$ bits. For example, the rule may see the first **128 bits** of $N$, its residue modulo 4, and its residues at every odd prime through **251**. The number of unit classes in the combined modulus is $2\prod_{3\le p\le251}(p-1)$, with base-two logarithm about 332.56, so the inequality holds. Without any additional leading bits the same argument reaches every odd prime through 347. The conclusions also allow the next modulus to be chosen using earlier answers, if the least common multiple on every terminal path satisfies the same size and class bounds. The Chinese remainder conditions on a consistent path are exactly one residue modulo that least common multiple. This limits using those observations to predict the *location* of the true sum. They can still reject many individual sum candidates.

This includes a familiar calculation that appears to use the entire product. For odd $N$, the Jacobi symbol $(a/N)$ is multiplicative in $a$, and for each prime generator $p$ its value depends only on $N\bmod 4p$. Therefore one residue modulo $8\prod_{3\le p\le251}p$ determines the symbols for **every** nonzero integer $a$ made from these primes, 2 and a sign, with arbitrary multiplicities. The extra factor of two in the unit-class count still fits alongside 128 leading bits. Even adaptively requesting arbitrarily many of these symbols provides no better accuracy for the central-window prediction than the bound above. This result concerns symbols with the fixed smooth numerators specified here. [Mathlib's Jacobi identities](https://leanprover-community.github.io/mathlib4_docs/Mathlib/NumberTheory/LegendreSymbol/JacobiSymbol.html) · [Generator and class-count proofs](../PrimeFactors/QuantileResidueBins.lean)

The same proof limits attempts to locate the logarithmic gap more precisely. Divide $a/H$ into 256 consecutive bands of equal length. The smooth density in this coordinate is at most $1/(1-1/2047^2)$ times uniform. For a fixed boundary $t$, $a/H\le t$ means $q^{1+t}\le B^{2t}p^{1-t}$. The two monotone boundaries of any one band give interval slices in either direction. Applying the count error to the chosen band and to the entire observation cell gives success probability at most
$$\frac{1/[256(1-1/2047^2)]+2^{-20}}{1-2^{-20}}<0.003908.$$
Thus a rule observing only those end bits can name the correct band in fewer than **0.3908%** of cases. A uniform guess succeeds on 1 in 256, about 0.3906%. The result allows converting the predicted band into actual sum endpoints with the full product. This concerns the accuracy of a prediction based on the specified bits, independent of the cost of searching inside a chosen interval. Earlier work in this repository bounds selecting a sparse list of rows in a quadratic-form search from fixed high and low bits. Here the alternatives are contiguous portions of the factor-sum range. ## Does searching the central window first actually help?

We drew pairs of 40-bit primes using SHAKE with seed `factor-sum-quantile-v1` and retained the first eight distinct pairs giving an 80-bit product. On every product we ran the same base-2, reverse-order, folded search three times, once on the full bit-width interval and once on each side of the cutoff. Each run allowed 20,000 table entries and 32,768 row visits. Generating the numbers and comparing answers were separate from the searches. Lean checked all eight factorizations. Certification could use trial division up to one million on prime predecessors after discovery. [Fixtures and partial results](../benchmarks/factor-sum-quantile.json) · [Draw regeneration and endpoint checks](../tests/test_factor_sum_quantile.py)

| Total over eight inputs | Search full interval | Search central first, then remaining interval on a miss |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Factors recovered | 8 pairs | 8 pairs, 4 in the central window |
| Left rows visited | 36,804 | 48,108 |
| Right entries built across the runs | 23,187 | 22,302 |

This comparison exposes the cost of starting a second search on a miss. The central window took 24.87% to 25.00% of the integer positions on the eight inputs. A shorter interval can still use the same sieve moduli as the full interval, leaving the table size unchanged. On the first 2,048-bit control, the central and full tables had the same projected size. On the second, the central cut reduced the projected storage from about $2^{447.01}$ to $2^{442.81}$ entries. All four projections exceeded $2^{442}$ entries for these two inputs, so the ordinary storage-capped runs stopped before building any entries. The gain established here is a population ranking of sums and a reproducible way to measure whether using the ranking saves total work. [Search comparisons](../benchmarks/factor-sum-quantile.json)

## Reusing one table across the cut

A different search can build its table once for the full interval and reuse it after a missed prediction. The sieve period $M$ may now be shorter than the window. Given an integer $h\ge1$, lift each permitted right residue through $h$ copies modulo $Q=hM$, then pair each lifted index with its negative. If the unfolded right menu contains $R$ residues, the number stored is $(hR+F)/2$. Here $F$ counts zero and, for even $Q$, the midpoint $Q/2$, when they belong to the lifted menu. Each left row needs at most $\lfloor W/Q\rfloor+2$ trace lookups over a window of width $W$. This bound includes wraps at both endpoints. Lean proves that the signed positions cover exactly the original sieve constraint throughout the window. [Coverage, storage and lookup bounds](../PrimeFactors/LiftedWheelPeriod.lean)

The planner starts from a sequence of small-prime sieve moduli. It may remove up to three trailing primes, replace one of the first four moduli with a power of the same prime up to 10,000, choose either side for storage and vary $h$. It minimizes stored positions plus the upper bound on lookups among the inspected plans meeting all three budgets. Setup, modular multiplication and candidate checks have separate costs. For a fixed $M$, it suffices to inspect the smallest $h$ in each range with constant $\lfloor W/(hM)\rfloor$, since storage is nondecreasing inside that range. After fixing the planner, we tested the first four eligible 96-bit products from a separate SHAKE draw under the same caps. All four shortened-period searches recovered the factors. On draw 5, $44{,}999{,}777{,}202{,}387{,}676{,}883{,}929{,}306{,}237=209{,}966{,}509{,}290{,}409\times214{,}318{,}832{,}819{,}893$. The short period fit the **entire** search into at most 17,010 rows and 51,030 lookups. It used 14,400 entries and found the sum after 16,648 visits and 42,895 lookups. For every one of the 67 full-period choices tested, the product of local menu sizes exceeded $2\cdot20{,}000\cdot32{,}256$. No partition of those complete menus can cover the whole interval under these storage and row limits. The best attainable full-period row bound was 34,020.

Early success changes the practical comparison. The original wheel visited its 32,256 rows and missed. With the improved prime-power choice and optimal partition, the best full-period layout found the same sum after **8,143 rows and 12,449 lookups**, beating the short-period run on this input. The shorter wheel improves the proven worst-case coverage here. Its full-period competitor was the faster route to this particular answer.
