# How much can choosing a multiplier improve a norm score?

The scoring rule used in the continued-fraction experiment tests 26 odd squarefree multipliers through 63. Counting every one of the **67,108,864** possible inputs to its sign tests, the winning multiplier improves the score over $m=1$ by **0.725201 bits on average**. The rule chooses $m=1$ on 43.56% of these sign words. In a separate result, even an unlimited choice of multipliers improves the exact-logarithm score by less than **two bits on average** in this model. These are statements about how much small-prime divisibility the local model predicts after charging for the larger radicand. The empirical factor discoveries are measured separately. ## Input and costs

Let $N=pq$ for distinct odd primes. For an odd squarefree $m$ coprime to $N$, the continued fraction of $\sqrt{mN}$ generates signed norms congruent to squares modulo $N$. A collection whose signed product is a square can yield a factor by a gcd. The scale of the ordinary convergent norm bound grows as $\sqrt{mN}$, charging $\tfrac12\log m$ against the expected small-prime part of the norm. Changing $m$ can change the convergents themselves. The score cannot guarantee that a usable square relation occurs. Multiplier scoring in CFRAC and the quadratic sieve has a long history. [Pomerance and Wagstaff, *Implementation of the Continued Fraction Integer Factoring Algorithm*](https://math.dartmouth.edu/~carlp/PDF/implementation.pdf) · [FLINT's Knuth–Schroeppel selector](https://flintlib.org/doc/qsieve/qsieve_knuth_schroeppel.html)

Fix a sieve cutoff $B$. For odd primes $\ell\le B$ not dividing $N$, put $\epsilon_\ell=(N/\ell)$, $a_\ell=\ell\log\ell/(\ell^2-1)$, and $c_\ell=\log\ell/(\ell^2-1)$. Write $d(1),d(3),d(5),d(7)=(4,1,2,1)/3$ for the expected valuation at 2 indexed by the radicand modulo 8. The exact score in natural-log units is

$$S(m)=d(mN\bmod8)\log2-\tfrac12\log m
 +\sum_{3\le\ell\le B}\left(a_\ell+a_\ell(m/\ell)\epsilon_\ell-\mathbf1_{\ell\mid m}c_\ell\right),$$

where the sum is over the odd sieve primes. A split prime contributes $2a_\ell$, a nonsplit prime contributes zero, and a prime dividing $m$ contributes $a_\ell-c_\ell=\log\ell/(\ell+1)$. These weights average over primitive integer pairs modulo prime powers. A sieve prime dividing $N$ already exposes a factor, and its ramified contribution is common to both scores if the comparison continues. [Primitive-pair count](primitive-norm-counts.md)

For the means below, take $N$ uniformly among the units modulo $8\prod_{3\le\ell\le B}\ell$. CRT gives independent uniform signs and a uniform odd residue modulo eight. For fixed $B$, prime products sampled from $[X,2X)$ converge to this law as $X$ grows. The prime number theorem in the fixed progressions gives equidistribution on every scaled interval. Approximate the region $pq\ge2X^2$ by finite unions of rectangles and then shrink their mesh to retain the same law under the product-size condition. The finite 2,048-bit bound below uses an explicit prime-pair count instead of replacing that population with the limiting model.

## Exact distribution for a practical menu

The holdout score uses $w(t)=\lfloor256\log_2 t\rfloor$ and primes through 97. There are $4\cdot2^{24}$ words. The experiment splits the 24 odd-prime signs in half. For each pair of multipliers, the score difference is a left half-sum, a right half-sum and a dyadic offset. Sorting the 4,096 right sums permits an exact threshold lookup for every left sum. Bit masks intersect the winning assignments against all rivals, giving ties to the smaller multiplier as in the actual search. Counting the right-hand signs in those masks also gives the exact sum of the winning improvements. All comparisons use integers after clearing denominators.

The five most frequent winners are $1,3,5,7,11$, with counts 29,233,992, 8,816,875, 7,023,467, 4,679,846 and 3,758,296. The mean improvement, divided by 256 to express it in score bits, is exactly

$$\frac{97972717393529030549931289}{135097322164301389186990080}.$$

For comparison, a full enumeration using unrounded logarithms, the same menu and $B=31$ gives a mean in $(0.63977669598,0.63977669600)$ bits and a maximum in $(3.97637184460,3.97637184462)$ bits. Rational intervals certify every comparison. The smaller cutoff has fewer scored primes, so these numbers measure a different score. Reproduce the practical calculation with `python3 -m experiments.multiplier_score --quantized`, and the unrounded calculation with `python3 -m experiments.multiplier_score`. ## A ceiling on the mean benefit of any menu

The following bound holds for every $B$ using exact logarithms. Let $A=\sum a_\ell+(2/3)\log2$, the mean score of $m=1$. For fixed $m$, the centered dyadic term $Z=(d(mN)-2/3)\log2$ satisfies $\mathbb E e^{3Z}=3/2$. The odd signs give an independent product of hyperbolic cosines. Using $\cosh z\le e^{z^2/2}$ and dropping the nonpositive ramification correction yields
$$\mathbb E e^{3(S(m)-A)}\le\tfrac32 m^{-3/2}\exp\!\left(\tfrac92\sum_{\ell\ge3}a_\ell^2\right).$$
A choice of multiplier that reads the whole sign word is covered by summing the exponentials over the menu. The sum over every positive odd $m$ of $m^{-3/2}$ is less than $1+4/(3\sqrt3)<1.8$ by the integral test. Jensen's inequality for the logarithm now gives
$$
\mathbb E\,[\max_m S(m)-S(1)]
\le\tfrac13\left(\log(2.7)+\tfrac92\sum_{\ell\ge3}a_\ell^2\right)<2\log2.
$$
For a finite $B$ a maximizing multiplier exists because the local contributions are bounded and the penalty grows without bound. This proof allows every odd squarefree multiplier to compete. It does not require the scores of different multipliers to be independent. Lean checks the finite exponential-moment and adaptive-selection inequalities. The mean, infinite tail and Jensen steps are paper deductions. [Lean proof](../PrimeFactors/MultiplierScore.lean)

The variance constant has an independent rational upper bound below $0.683$. Sum upper bounds for $a_\ell^2$ through $K=8192$. Bound the remaining sum by summing over all integers past $K$, then integrating the decreasing function $(\log x/x)^2$. The tail is at most
$$(1-K^{-2})^{-2}\big((\log K)^2+2\log K+2\big)/K.$$
The script encloses each logarithm by range reduction to $[1,2]$ and 18 terms of the series for $2\operatorname{atanh}((x-1)/(x+1))$, with a geometric bound on the remainder. The resulting upper bound on the mean is below 1.954 score bits. It weights each candidate by its size through $m^{-3/2}$. Simply multiplying a single-multiplier tail by the menu size would discard that advantage.

## The price of forcing every sign

Suppose all the first $k$ odd primes through $y$ must split. A candidate $m$ coprime to their product specifies exactly one allowable sign word for $N$. Candidates divisible by one of those primes cannot force a nonzero square there. Thus $H$ candidates cover at most $H/2^k$ of uniform sign words, even when the input selects the multiplier. In particular the median size of the first all-split multiplier is at least $2^{k-1}$ in this model. At cutoff $B=y$ the most one can gain before the size charge is $2\sum_{\ell\le y}a_\ell+\log2=(2+o(1))\log y$ by the prime number theorem and partial summation. For typical words, the cost of matching every sign therefore grows much faster than the entire available reward. Picking a moderate multiplier that improves a few heavily weighted primes avoids this requirement.

There are nevertheless explicit ways to enforce those signs. Given $N$, the least positive odd representative of its class modulo $P=\prod\ell$ is below $2P$. Its squarefree part has the same signs. This is an existence bound; extracting the squarefree part can take additional work. Under Dirichlet GRH, apply the least-prime-in-a-coset theorem at modulus $2P$ to the common kernel of the $k$ characters, of index $h=2^k$. For $2P\ge20000$ and $h>1$, the least prime in the prescribed coset is at most $\max\!\left\{10^9,\;\left[(h-1)\log(2P)+3(h+1)+\tfrac52\bigl(\log\log(2P)\bigr)^2\right]^2\right\}$. A gcd checks whether it reveals a factor of $N$ directly. Enumerating candidates up to this bound charges for the search as well as the increased norm size. [Lamzouri, Li and Soundararajan, Theorem 1.4](https://arxiv.org/html/1309.3595v3#S1.SS2)

**Finite 2,048-bit population.** Draw distinct 1,024-bit primes with 2,048-bit product as in the earlier counts. For the cutoff 727, there are 128 odd primes. The rational estimates give $2\sum a_\ell/\log2<14.5$. As the dyadic contribution can improve by at most one bit, a multiplier tying or beating $m=1$ must be smaller than $2^{31}$. At most $2^{30}$ positive odd multipliers qualify. For a fixed $p$ and a fixed full sign word, CRT allows exactly $\varphi(P)/2^{128}$ unit classes for its partner $q$. In $[X,2X)$ this gives at most $(X+P)\varphi(P)/(P2^{128})$ integer choices, with $X=2^{1023}$. Multiplying by the candidate count and the number of first primes, and dividing by the eligible ordered prime pairs, bounds the chance that *any* all-split multiplier ties or beats $m=1$ by
$$\frac{1.511\cdot710}{0.22^2}\left(1+\frac{P}{X}\right)\frac{\varphi(P)}P\frac{2^{30}}{2^{128}}<2^{-86}.$$
This conclusion is unconditional and has no multiplier-size assumption. The score itself imposes the size cap. [Prime-count constants](stride-budget.md#an-explicit-bound-at-exactly-2048-bits) · [Exact arithmetic check](../experiments/multiplier_score.py)

## A worst-input cutoff from Dirichlet GRH

Even if the sieve bound $B$ is arbitrarily large, an odd squarefree multiplier coprime to a large odd semiprime $N$ and scoring at least as well as $m=1$ must satisfy $m\le(\log N)^{8+o(1)}$ under Dirichlet GRH. This bounds the size of the menu one would need to scan for the exact score. Evaluating it still requires the selected sieve primes and their symbols. An explicit practical threshold of this strength at 2,048 bits has not been obtained here.

For any squarefree $d>1$, the signs $(d/\ell)$ at odd primes come from a nonprincipal quadratic character of conductor $Q\le4d$. Uniformly in $B$, its signed score component differs from $\sum_{\ell\le B}\chi(\ell)\log\ell/\ell$ by a bounded absolute amount, since $a_\ell-\log\ell/\ell=O(\log\ell/\ell^3)$. Ramification corrections are also absolutely summable. For a nonprincipal character the GRH estimate $\psi(x,\chi)\ll\sqrt x\log(2x)\log(2Qx)$ bounds the corresponding prime sum. Prime powers of exponent at least two give a bounded term after reciprocal weighting. Set $z=(\log Q)^2(\log\log Q)^4$ for large $Q$. Up to $\min(z,B)$ the unsigned contribution is at most $(1+o(1))\log z$ by the prime number theorem. Beyond $z$, partial summation up to any $B$ costs $O(\log(2z)\log(2Qz)/\sqrt z)=O(1)$. Thus each character sum is at most $(2+o(1))\log\log Q$ in absolute value. Comparing the characters of $mN$ and $N$ and the bounded dyadic terms gives $\tfrac12\log m\le(4+o(1))\log\log(4mN)$. First this forces $\log m=O(\log\log N)$, and substitution gives the stated exponent. This application uses the Dirichlet result for nonprincipal characters. [Montgomery and Vaughan, Theorem 13.7](https://personal.science.psu.edu/rcv4/personal/Publications/MNTI/17.0_pp_419_451_Conditional_estimates.pdf) · [Dirichlet verification record](https://math.mit.edu/~drew/grh/)
