# What combining modular orders can reveal

Several small bases can give more information than one. If one base gives a congruence for the factor sum modulo 9 and another gives one modulo 25, together they give a congruence modulo 225. Once their orders have been combined this way, even knowing the exact size of the subgroup they generate cannot add another factor to that modulus. The *values* of the powers can still reveal a prime by a gcd. The example below separates these two uses. [Lean proof](../PrimeFactors/OrderTorsionCeiling.lean)

## An exact limit from the product

Let $N=pq$ for distinct primes, let $S=p+q$, and let $\lambda=\operatorname{lcm}(p-1,q-1)$. Suppose we have certified that $m\mid\lambda$. Earlier work gives $S\equiv N+1\pmod{L_N(m)}$, where $L_N(m)=m\gcd(m,N-1)$. We can compute this modulus from $N$ and $m$. Obtaining $m$ is separate work. [Earlier congruence](../PrimeFactors/PartialOrderLift.lean)

Exactly $L_N(m)$ units modulo $N$ satisfy $x^m=1$. Indeed the Chinese remainder theorem gives $\gcd(m,p-1)\gcd(m,q-1)$ such units. For a prime with valuations $t,a,b$ in $m,p-1,q-1$, respectively, $t\le\max(a,b)$. Its exponent in this product is $\min(t,a)+\min(t,b)=t+\min(t,a,b)$. Finally $\gcd(\lambda,N-1)=\gcd(p-1,q-1)$ and $m\mid\lambda$. Thus the root count depends only on the public data. [Cyclic root count and CRT](../PrimeFactors/SingleBaseRepeat.lean) · [Public cardinality](../PrimeFactors/OrderTorsionCeiling.lean)

Take any subgroup $H$ generated by the bases, and let $m$ be its exponent, the least common multiple of their exact orders. The group $H$ sits inside that power kernel, so $|H|\mid L_N(m)$. This bound is attained by the entire kernel, which has exponent $m$ since the unit group contains an element of order $\lambda$. Also $L_N(\operatorname{lcm}(a,b))=\operatorname{lcm}(L_N(a),L_N(b))$. Thus the congruence from the combined orders is exactly the conjunction of the individual congruences. If only divisors of the orders have been certified, their lcm still gives a valid sum congruence. The subgroup-cardinality bound applies to the elements annihilated by that lcm. [Subgroup divisibility and lcm theorem](../PrimeFactors/OrderTorsionCeiling.lean)

For $k$ bases, map integer exponent vectors in $\mathbb Z^k$ to their products of powers. The index of the **full** lattice of relations is $|H|$. Even a certificate of that exact index can therefore supply only a divisor of the known modulus through Lagrange's theorem. If only $k$ linearly independent relations have been found, the absolute value of their determinant is a *multiple* of $|H|$, and can grow by multiplying a relation by an arbitrary integer. The lattice interpretation follows from the first isomorphism theorem. The Lean result establishes the subgroup and cardinality claims directly.

## A factor from two residues

For $N=12{,}403$, start with bases 3 and 11 and multiply successively modulo $N$ until each first returns to 1. Starting at 1 takes 78 multiplications for 3 and 39 for 11. The exact orders can also be checked with one annihilating power and one nonidentity test per distinct prime divisor of each proposed order. These are four powers for 3 and three for 11. All five prime-division tests have gcd 1 with $N$ after subtracting 1. The small example was selected to exhibit this behavior. [Exact orders and checked factorization](../PrimeFactors/OrderTorsionCeiling.lean)

Two of the residues from those tests are $u=3^{26}\bmod N=6{,}738$ and $v=11^{13}\bmod N=10{,}688$. Both have order three. Comparing them gives $\gcd(v-u,N)=79$. Division gives the other prime, 157. A second comparison gives $\gcd(v-(u^2\bmod N),N)=157$ directly. At one prime $v=u$; at the other $v=u^2$. Finding such a disagreement among powers of two bases is classical. Źrałek's Theorem 4.2 and Corollary 4.3 address the more general problem of factoring from a noncyclic subgroup with known factored generator orders. [Źrałek, §4](https://arxiv.org/pdf/0707.4102#page=5)

There is a cheaper sum search on this same input. The order of 3 alone gives $L_N(78)=6{,}084$. In the balanced interval from 223 through 236, only 236 is congruent to $N+1$ modulo 6,084. One square test gives $236^2-4N=78^2$, and hence both primes. For a typical 2,048-bit product we have no efficient general way here to obtain generator orders large enough for such a one-test recovery. Certifying an order proposed by another calculation is much cheaper than finding it from scratch.
