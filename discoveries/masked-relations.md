# When two small remainders guarantee a factor

Two square roots can each come from the first step of a continued fraction and already determine a factor. For $N=247$, the integer parts of $\sqrt{3N}$ and $\sqrt{7N}$ are 27 and 41. Their squares fall short by 12 and 48. Since $48=4\cdot12$, the numbers $2\cdot27=54$ and 41 have the same square modulo 247. Their difference is 13 and their sum is 95. Taking gcds with 247 gives both primes, 13 and 19. We can test for this kind of overlap using only the input. Its frequency for typical large products remains open. ## A height bound for guaranteed splits

Choose positive public multipliers $m,k\le K$. Put $a=\lfloor\sqrt{mN}\rfloor$ and $b=\lfloor\sqrt{kN}\rfloor$. Suppose that dividing their two deficits by squares $s^2,t^2$, with $1\le s,t\le S$, gives the same integer $c$. The two identities are

$$
a^2+c s^2=mN,\qquad b^2+c t^2=kN.
$$

Then $(at-bs)(at+bs)=(mt^2-ks^2)N$. Check that $mt^2\ne ks^2$. For distinct squarefree multipliers this holds automatically. If $4KS^2<N$, then $at+bs\le2S\sqrt{KN}<N$ and $0<|at-bs|<N$. Hence $\gcd(at+bs,N)$ is a proper divisor. Indeed, if the sum were coprime to $N$, the other nonzero factor would be a multiple of $N$ of absolute value less than $N$. The same proof works whenever $at+bs<N$ holds directly. The common quotient $c$ can be composite. [Theorems `maskedRelations_short` and `maskedRelations_bounded_overlap`](../PrimeFactors/MaskedRelations.lean)

To search, take $M$ distinct squarefree multipliers at most $K$. Compute one integer square root per multiplier. For each $s\le S$ whose square divides the deficit, store the quotient as a dictionary key. A repeated key from two multipliers supplies all inputs to the gcd. This takes $M$ square roots, at most $MS$ divisibility tests, at most $MS$ expected constant-time dictionary operations, and at most $MS$ stored entries. Integer operations still depend on operand size. A zero deficit means $mN$ is itself a square and can be handled separately. The bound guarantees every qualifying collision gives a divisor. It leaves the number of multipliers needed to find the first collision unresolved. [Earlier evidence on pooling continued-fraction walks](pooled-relations.md)

There are products of unbounded size that pass this test with $m=3$, $k=7$, $s=1$ and $t=2$. Start with $(h,b)=(9,41)$, for which $b^2+20=21h^2$. The update $(h,b)\mapsto(55h+12b,252h+55b)$ preserves that equation and increases both coordinates. Put $N=3h^2+4$. Then $3N=(3h)^2+12$ and $7N=b^2+48$. At the initial pair and every later pair, these deficits are smaller than the gaps to the next integer squares. The product-only search therefore recovers a proper divisor using the same two square roots and one gcd at every size in this sequence. The example at $h=9$ is the semiprime 247. Primality of the resulting divisors at later steps requires separate verification. [Recurrence and first-row certificates](../PrimeFactors/MaskedRelations.lean)

## Which square masks preserve the factor information?

For a square dependency $X^2\equiv Y^2\pmod N$, multiplying both roots by any public unit $u$ preserves $\gcd(X-Y,N)$ exactly. The sum gcd follows by replacing $Y$ with $-Y$. This holds for any modulus. If every input row has its own nonzero integer mask $u_i$ coprime to $N$, its signed norm $R_i$ becomes $u_i^2R_i$ and its left root $A_i$ becomes $u_i A_i$. Each subset keeps the same parity of every integer prime exponent. The magnitude of its candidate square root acquires $|\prod u_i|$, and both modular roots acquire the same unit up to global sign. Thus the unordered pair of gcds stays fixed, even for masks picked adaptively. A mask sharing a proper gcd with $N$ already gives a divisor. [Theorems `maskedRelations_unit_gcd` and `maskedRelations_dependency_gcd`](../PrimeFactors/MaskedRelations.lean)

An independently obtained row can change the result. Given $a^2\equiv ds^2$ and $b^2\equiv dt^2\pmod N$, first check the known cofactors for proper gcds. For unit rows and odd $N$, the comparison splits exactly when $at\not\equiv\pm bs\pmod N$. Equivalently the normalized roots $a/s$ and $b/t$ occupy different classes under a global change of sign. A bucket of $L$ unit roots of one residue needs at most $L-1$ comparisons against one anchor to detect any such pair. Multiplying by previously known roots of one gives the same criterion. A root different from $\pm1$ already splits an odd product by a gcd, while any product of global signs is still a global sign. ## When a random mask gives a new sign

Let $N=pq$ for distinct odd primes, start from a known unit $x$, and sample a uniform unit $t$. Give only $w=(xt)^2\bmod N$ to an independent procedure that tries to construct a root $y$ of $w$. Its acceptance decision and root may depend on $w$, public data fixed before sampling, and its own independent random coins. For each accepted $w$, fix those coins. The four possible $t$ give the four possible values of $xt/y$, one for each pair of CRT signs. Exactly two values give a proper gcd. For example, with $N=77$, $x=15$ and independently supplied $y=19$, the four masks reaching $19^2\bmod77$ are $2,9,68,75$. The difference gcds are $11,1,77,7$, respectively. [Exact four-mask identity and checked enumeration](../PrimeFactors/MaskedRelations.lean) · [Menezes, van Oorschot and Vanstone, §3.5, Facts 3.46–3.47](https://cacr.uwaterloo.ca/hac/about/chap3.pdf#page=17)

If the procedure holds one root for each of $T$ distinct target residues, a fresh unit mask hits the table with probability $4T/\varphi(N)$ and returns a factor through this comparison with probability $2T/\varphi(N)$. A rejection rule depending only on the target can select small or smooth values and retain the conditional half-success probability whenever an independent root is available. The cost includes every rejected mask and the work to produce that root. Carrying the original root through the known mask preserves the original sign. An independent way to obtain roots on a substantial fraction of the targets would change the cost directly.
