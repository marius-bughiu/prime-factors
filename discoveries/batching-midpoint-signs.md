# Testing a whole block of midpoints with one Jacobi seed

Fix a product $N$, a first midpoint $h$, and $H\ge1$ consecutive parity-compatible candidates $h+2j$, $0\le j<H$, with positive discriminants $D_j=(h+2j)^2-N$. For any single seed $a\ge1$, there is a randomized test for whether **at least one of the $H$ midpoints passes** its stripped Jacobi check. One trial costs $\widetilde O(\sqrt H)$ operations on integers of polynomially many bits in $\log(2+a+N+h+H)$. It never reports a pass on an empty block. On every nonempty block it reports a pass with probability at least $1/4$, including when only one candidate passes. Repeating $k$ times bounds the chance of overlooking a pass by $(3/4)^k$. This is a paper algorithm with a kernel-checked masking bound. [Single-candidate definition](random-midpoint-checks.md) · [Lean masking and pattern proofs](../PrimeFactors/MidpointBlocks.lean)

## From parity to existence

Choose a power of two $B$ with $\sqrt H\le B<2\sqrt H$ (take $B=1$ at $H=1$). Write each index as $iB+j$, with $0\le j<B$, in at most $B$ rows. Let $A_{ij}$ be the bit indicating that the candidate passes, padding the final partial row with zeros. Choose independent fair row and column bits $u_i,v_j$. The trial computes
$$
       u^\mathsf{T} A v
         =\sum_{i,j}u_i A_{ij}v_j\pmod2 .
$$
If this is one, at least one candidate passes. Given a nonzero entry $A_{ij}$, fix every mask bit except $u_i,v_j$. Across their four assignments the four results sum to $A_{ij}$ in $\mathbb F_2$. At least one is one. A matrix containing exactly one passing entry meets the $1/4$ bound. Lean verifies the four-assignment identity and the count over all masks. A successful mask also permits locating a passing position: halve its selected rows or columns, retain a half with odd parity, and continue. The extra factor in work is logarithmic. [Lean finite count](../PrimeFactors/MidpointBlocks.lean)

It suffices to compute the ordinary product of the signs on the selected rows and columns. If there are $s$ selected real positions, multiplying that product by $(-1)^s$ gives $(-1)^{u^\mathsf{T}Av}$. The count $s$ comes from the two masks and the last row. Taking all bits equal to one gives the unmasked block sign product deterministically. An even passing count alone includes both zero and positive even counts.

For $N=1900014700027$, $h=1378412$, $H=16$ and $a=600600$, the four rows of pass bits are $1011,1111,0001,1011$. Of all $16\cdot16$ mask pairs, 112 give odd parity. The bounded experiment reproduces all 256 masks and tests composite seeds. It uses schoolbook polynomial products and individual Horner evaluations, with separate counters for this implementation’s work. [Experiment](../experiments/midpoint_blocks.py) · [Reproduction tests](../tests/test_midpoint_blocks.py)

The reported bit answers a question about one seed. A passing candidate still needs the other tests and the exact discriminant-square check.

## Computing the selected product without factoring the seed

The stripped sign is multiplicative in the seed. Each odd prime power $p^e\parallel a$ contributes $(D_j/p)^e$ if $p\nmid D_j$ and one otherwise. The prime 2 always contributes one. The following calculation obtains every contribution even when a different factor is stripped at each position.

1. **Small prime divisors.** Trial-divide the seed through $B$, removing each full prime power. A found divisor is prime because all smaller prime factors have already been removed. For each resulting odd $p$ of odd multiplicity, tabulate its stripped sign for each index residue modulo $p$. Form two residue histograms over $\mathbb F_2$, counting the selected full-row offsets $Bi\bmod p$ and selected column offsets $j\bmod p$. Their cyclic convolution, obtained by polynomial multiplication modulo $X^p-1$, gives the parity of every index residue among selected positions. Combine these parities with the $p$ tabulated signs. Handle the final partial row directly. This costs $\widetilde O(B)$ operations per found prime, with at most $\log_2 a$ such primes.
2. **The remaining modulus.** Write $A_0$ for the residual seed and omit this stage when it equals one. Every prime divisor of $A_0$ is odd and exceeds $B$, after separately removing 2 if $B=1$. For each such $p$ the quadratic congruence $D_j=0\bmod p$ has at most two solutions per period $p$. Thus at most $E\le 2(1+H/B)\log_2 a$ positions have $\gcd(D_j,A_0)>1$, even before masking. Their locations and the prime factorization of $A_0$ are unnecessary.
3. **Polynomial products over $\mathbb Z/A_0\mathbb Z$.** For a dyadic slice $J$ of the $B$ columns form
$$
   Q_J(X)=\prod_{\substack{j\in J\\v_j=1}}\big((X+2j)^2-N\big).
$$
At first evaluate the full-column polynomial at all selected full-row starts $X=h+2Bi$. When an evaluation is coprime to $A_0$, its Jacobi symbol gives the product of the corresponding stripped signs. When the gcd exceeds one, bisect the column slice for that row. At each depth, batch all evaluations of the *same* $Q_J$ by a monic subproduct and remainder tree. The sum of degrees across slices at that depth is at most $2B$. The first depth has at most $B$ row starts, and every later depth has at most $2E$ evaluations: each bisected parent contains a distinct exceptional position at that depth. Product trees build the polynomials in quasilinear work per depth. Monic division permits multipoint evaluation over the composite modulus. At exceptional singletons compute the original stripped reading directly, and scan any final partial row of size less than $B$. Over $1+\log_2 B$ depths this gives the claimed bound, including gcds, mask generation and signs. The masked calculation uses the classical polynomial blocking technique for products along arithmetic progressions. [Bostan, Gaudry and Schost](https://doi.org/10.1137/S0097539704443793) · [Monic multipoint evaluation over a commutative ring](https://flintlib.org/doc/algorithms/polynomials/subproduct-tree.html)

## How many consecutive sign patterns are possible?

Fix an odd prime $\ell\nmid N$, a length $1\le t<\ell$ and any $t$ distinct shifts $c_j$ modulo $\ell$. For a prescribed sign string, count the starts $x\bmod\ell$ at which all $(x+c_j)^2-N$ are nonzero and have those Legendre signs. Calling the count $C$, one has
$$
   |C-\ell/2^t|
       \le (t-1+2^{-t})\sqrt\ell+t .
$$
In particular an all-negative run, an all-positive run and every mixture occur whenever $\ell/2^t>t\sqrt\ell+t$. This applies to $c_j=2j$ and to any parity-compatible starting midpoint covering a full period.

To prove it, take $r^2=N$ in a splitting field. For every nonempty subset $S$ of the shifts, some $c\in S$ satisfies $c+2r\notin S$: otherwise the orbit of $c$ under addition of $2r$ would place $\ell$ distinct elements in $S$. At $x=-c-r$, the factor for $c$ has a simple root, and the only possible other factor vanishing there has shift $c+2r$. The selected product therefore has a simple root, even when some of its other roots coincide. Weil's quadratic character bound for a subset of size $s$ is at most $(2s-1)\sqrt\ell$. Equivalently, take its squarefree part and allow an error of at most one per discarded root of even multiplicity. Expand $2^{-t}\prod_j(1+\epsilon_j\chi((x+c_j)^2-N))$ and sum over $x$. Averaging the nonempty-subset error gives the stated coefficient of $\sqrt\ell$. At the at most $2t$ roots of any factor the weighted indicator is at most $1/2$, costing at most $t$. [Lean proof of the unique root](../PrimeFactors/MidpointBlocks.lean) · [Weil](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.34.5.204) · [Wright, character expansion for prescribed residue patterns](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jnt.2013.01.004)

## Why separate block checks do not locate a common survivor

Consider a procedure that can observe each length-$t$ pass vector only through one-bit answers depending on that vector, public randomness and earlier answers. For a single vector, a deterministic algorithm relying on subset parities needs at least $t$ queries on its all-zero path when every pattern is possible: fewer than $t$ linear equations admit a nonzero vector with the same answers. The random masks above give a bounded-error test for this case.

For **two** vectors, deciding whether some coordinate passes both is two-party set disjointness with the output complemented. The parties can simulate this procedure: whichever party owns the queried vector sends its one answer bit. Thus even adaptive randomized queries, allowing any separate one-bit function, require $\Omega(t)$ answers in the worst case at constant error below one half. This lower bound addresses an interface that reveals the vectors through separate replies. An algorithm exploiting the public numerical start, the product, or arithmetic joint across the seeds has more information. [Kalyanasundaram and Schnitger](https://doi.org/10.1137/0405044) · [Razborov's distributional proof](https://people.cs.uchicago.edu/~razborov/files/dis.pdf)

These pairs of sign patterns really occur together for prime seeds. For example, for any product in the paper's 2048-bit, balanced, parity-filtered interval, take one prime in $(2^{499},2^{500})$ and another in $(2^{500},2^{501})$. Bertrand's theorem supplies both, and neither can divide the product of two 1024-bit primes. At $t=240$ both primes meet the strict pattern bound. Choose a starting residue producing the desired string at each prime. CRT gives a common start, and stepping between aligned, disjoint blocks by $2t$ visits every residue modulo the product of the two primes. The interval has more than $2^{1018}$ parity-compatible candidates, giving more than $2^{1001}$ full blocks, enough for that period. This is a worst-case construction for the separate-answer model. The distribution of two independent 2,202-bit seeds and the cost of general arithmetic on midpoints are different questions. [Midpoint interval and seed law](random-midpoint-checks.md)

## A common pass when the earlier tests have a small period

For earlier seeds $a_1,\ldots,a_r$, put $M=\operatorname{lcm}(a_1,\ldots,a_r)$ and $L=\min(H,M)$. Test indices $0\le j<L$ and retain the set $R$ passing all $r$ seeds. These readings repeat every $M$ indices: each odd prime power in a seed contributes a sign depending only on $D_j$ modulo its prime, including the value one at a zero. Hence every candidate passing the earlier tests occurs in exactly one progression $j+Mt$, $j\in R$, of length $n_j=1+\lfloor(H-1-j)/M\rfloor$.

Let $b$ be an unrestricted last seed. Divide $b$ repeatedly by its current gcd with $2M$ until the remainder $b_0$ is coprime to $2M$. Write $g=b/b_0$. On the progression starting at $j$, the sign from $g$ is constant, $c_j=F_g(D_j)$, because the discriminant stays fixed modulo every prime of $g$. The candidate passes the last test exactly when $F_{b_0}(D_{j+Mt})=c_j$. Either desired sign uses the same masked parity algorithm: multiply each residual sign by $c_j$ before interpreting a positive entry as a pass. Replace the midpoint stride $2$ by $2M$ in the polynomial and residue histograms. Every prime of $b_0$ is coprime to the stride, so its quadratic has at most two roots per period as before.

With $k$ trials on each progression, the work is $\widetilde O(rL+k\sum_{j\in R}\sqrt{n_j})$, bounded by $\widetilde O(rL+k|R|\sqrt{\lceil H/M\rceil})$ in the same bit model. An odd result identifies a progression containing a common pass, which the bisection above can locate. If any common pass exists, missing all of them has probability at most $(3/4)^k$ by considering one fixed nonempty progression. For reporting *all* nonempty progressions the union bound gives $|R|(3/4)^k$. The table cost becomes a full scan at $M\ge H$. Arbitrary collections of large-period tests still lack a corresponding sublinear joint algorithm.

For $N=1900014700027$, $h=1378412$ and $H=315$, seeds $15,7$ leave 21 classes modulo 105. Last seed $33$ splits as $g=3$, $b_0=11$. On class 14 the constant is $-1$ and the residual signs at indices $14,119,224$ are $+1,+1,-1$. Only 224 passes there. Direct enumeration gives 30 common passes across the block.
