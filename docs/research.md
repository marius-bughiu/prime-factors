# What the Riemann hypothesis gives a factor search

The upper-end factor-sum test and the half Gauss factorial calculation use elementary congruences and quadratic reciprocity. The public upper-end success range follows from integer inequalities. The modular factorial approach would benefit from a fast approximation to a particular residue modulo the input. The released zero-location statements give estimates about the distribution of primes and do not supply this approximation. [Upper-end decoder](../discoveries/when-the-product-is-near-the-largest-square.md) · [Factorial residue and known approximation theorem](../discoveries/half-gauss-product.md) · [Verified statements](https://math.mit.edu/~drew/grh/)

Assuming RH, one square test at multiplier six splits more than $2^{1509}$ distinct 2,048-bit products. Their larger prime is exceptionally close to three halves of their smaller prime. The proof uses a published bound on the *average* error in counting primes in short intervals. This is strong enough to bound the number of smaller primes with few suitable partners. A separate unconditional estimate gives the same one-test existence for almost all eligible smaller primes as the bit width grows, with no explicit threshold for 2,048 bits. [Finite RH estimate and proof assumptions](../discoveries/MeanSquarePrimePartners.md) · [Unconditional asymptotic result](../discoveries/PrimeGapPopulation.md)

The released Dirichlet zero-location statements also prompted a direct test of what a finite character sum can reveal. For the principal character modulo a product of two equal-width odd primes, truncating at the square root removes exactly the term indexed by the smaller prime from an ordinary geometric sum. At 1,024 specified frequencies this difference encodes all its bits. The complete Ramanujan sum equals 1 at each frequency. Efficiently evaluating the truncated sum with absolute error below one half remains open here. The finite identities and error bound have unconditional Lean proofs. [Missing-term identity and precision](../discoveries/reading-the-missing-term.md) · [Released statements](https://github.com/leanprover-community/riemann-hypothesis/blob/40e56bab/src/Statements.lean)

The same published prime-gap estimate gives every smaller prime in the stated range a partner for which two equal-budget searches disagree. Together with two classical bounds on the number of smaller primes, this yields more than $2^{1009}$ additional 2,048-bit products at a budget of $2^{21}$ checks. Unconditional certificates already give two examples of a strict gain at specific budgets. [Equal-budget comparison and analytical premises](../discoveries/factoring-more-products-at-the-same-budget.md)

Under the prime-gap estimate of Carneiro, Milinovich and Soundararajan, each smaller prime in a specified 1,024-bit range has at least one larger partner that a product-only search can recover in at most 16,598 square tests. Lean assumes the published one-sided interval estimate and checks the remaining steps, including the factor and the search budget. [Partner theorem and what the quantifiers mean](../discoveries/prime-partners-near-three-halves.md) · [Theorem 5 of the paper](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1708.04122#page=5)

For full-width products in a fixed public bin, the zeta zero-location statement and Schoenfeld's explicit prime-count error give lower and upper bounds on four sets of candidate primes. With those interval inequalities as premises, Lean proves that switching to multiplier six after a long ordinary Fermat prefix reaches over 80% more *additional* prime pairs in that bin than continuing the ordinary search. The same program found a deliberately constructed 2,048-bit pair in 15,264 odd traces. Both comparison products remained unresolved. [Search rule, conditional comparison and proof boundary](../discoveries/switching-after-a-long-fermat-search.md) · [Schoenfeld, Corollary 1](https://www.ams.org/mcom/1976-30-134/S0025-5718-1976-0457374-X/S0025-5718-1976-0457374-X.pdf)

The shared-multiplier search raises two counting questions. Ramanujan's work on superior highly composite numbers gives the multiplier that maximizes the number of weights relative to their fourth-root width penalty. For this exponent the maximum follows by finite prime-power comparisons. To bound the fraction of 2,048-bit products actually split by a fixed catalogue, we use integer partner intervals and the project's unconditional prime-population bound. The pinned zeta and Dirichlet statements provide no choice of a multiplier tailored to the hidden primes. For any target product, a selector $d$ with Jacobi symbol $(d/N)=-1$ puts its two primes in opposite groups. The released Dirichlet zero-location statements, together with Lamzouri, Li and Soundararajan's Theorem 1.1(2), bound the least positive prime selector below 2,016,400. The unconditional finite arithmetic then proves either group's product returns a factor by a gcd, and that each full group at this selector size still contains at least $2^{1000}$ odd integers. The analytic implication is written separately from the Lean theorem about a supplied least-prime bound. In a cubic number field, an element can look like a square at every prime in any fixed finite list and still have no square root in the field. Lean constructs such an element for each list using Dirichlet's theorem on primes in arithmetic progressions and quadratic reciprocity. Those results are unconditional. The released Dirichlet zero-location statements concern characters of the rational integers. We use exact algebra to decide whether each surviving cubic element is a square. Two explicit constructions guarantee a pair of unequal square remainders that reveal a factor on more than 28% of eligible 2,048-bit products. An unconditional prime-count bound proves that lower bound. Using the released Dirichlet hypothesis gives the share covered by those constructions to within $2^{-464}$ of $9/32$. The work of locating the two remainders is measured separately. The Dirichlet result supplies a finite deterministic choice of a base for the collision-gap search. The bases that satisfy the Fermat equality at both primes form a proper subgroup. For two 1,024-bit primes there are no prime divisors below $(\log N)^2$, so Theorem 1.1(2) of Lamzouri, Li and Soundararajan gives a prime outside the subgroup below 2,016,400. A single such prime suffices for a completed search. Its modular test and the subsequent decoding are elementary, and random selection needs at most two unit draws in expectation without GRH. This use of the analytic theorem is a written deduction. Our Lean theorem takes the finite least-prime statement as a hypothesis. When the product chooses the first primes at which it is a square, the choice itself affects how small a remainder we can expect. Averaging Dirichlet character sums over both hidden primes bounds the chance that the selection takes unusually long and sharpens the asymptotic estimate. The finite 2,048-bit estimate uses integer counts alone. [Adaptive prime selection](../discoveries/crt-screen-population.md)

We also asked how large a multiplier could be worth trying when a continued fraction starts from the square root of the multiplied input. For the score with exact logarithms, a Dirichlet GRH estimate bounds the size of a winning multiplier in terms of the input, even as more primes are added to the score. This narrows a theoretical search over multipliers. Recovering factors still requires finding a usable relation in the resulting continued fraction. [Derivation and its assumptions](../discoveries/multiplier-score.md#a-worst-input-cutoff-from-dirichlet-grh)

A character can also select one of the two unknown primes inside a divisor sum. For 667, a power-series calculation using only the product recovered the last three binary digits of 29. It took 222,111 convolution terms. This example uses the classical Rogers–Ramanujan identity and needs no Riemann hypothesis. Computing the corresponding coefficient cheaply at 2,048 bits remains open. [Derivation and examples](../discoveries/theta-residues.md)

Two later counting results use the Dirichlet theorems in different ways. Miller's reduction uses small character witnesses to turn a totient count into deterministic factorization, including for products with repeated factors. A prime at which the product is a quadratic nonresidue makes the two unknown primes distinct modulo that auxiliary prime. This permits a smaller weighted readout on the original multiplication network. [Totient count](../discoveries/counting-modular-inverses.md) · [Choice of a separating prime](../discoveries/exponential-readout.md)

In the signed short-period search, the public Jacobi symbol chooses between $N-1$ and $N+1$. Dirichlet GRH and a published coset bound guarantee a small prime discriminant realizing each pair of hidden signs. The quadratic-group arithmetic and a numerical example are checked in Lean. [Signed exponents and the character bound](../discoveries/jacobi-separation.md)

The same Dirichlet hypothesis bounds the work of finding a quadratic nonresidue at each auxiliary prime in a Fermat search. Testing all primes in a wide band doubles its limiting population coverage. A mean-value theorem also bounds the total work unconditionally. [Proof and explicit limiting fraction](../discoveries/gap-search-frequency.md#testing-every-auxiliary-prime)

A product residue can also choose the entire list of moduli. The coverage bound averaged over products is unconditional. For a specified residue, Dirichlet character orthogonality and the released GRH statements give a uniform estimate for how many prime pairs produce it. That estimate yields a bound conditional on the actual observed residue. [Derivation and range of moduli](../discoveries/conditional-strides.md#conditioning-on-the-actual-observed-word)

The class-number route uses the released Dirichlet statements to bound how far a search for suitable auxiliary primes must go, and to study approximations to a Dirichlet L-value. An unconditional follow-up shares the same class-number interval across successive searches. On 32 small products this reduced the number of form compositions from 86,408 to 44,221. On the 2,048-bit comparison input, two public remainders also guarantee a known divisor of the class number, reducing the projected table size by 3.2%. Computing a sufficiently narrow interval cheaply remains open. The polynomial remainder search uses integer arithmetic alone. In the continued-fraction search, the same theorem on the least prime in a coset bounds the auxiliary primes that must be considered. For a 2,048-bit product it gives a cutoff of one billion. Trying all partners of one fixed eligible prime suffices for the two-prime multiplier case. The bound leaves the work of each continued-fraction walk open. ## A bounded witness search for structured semiprimes

Suppose the input is $N=pq$ for distinct primes $p<q$, with $p-1$ dividing $q-1$. The factors can be far apart. If $p<q$ are primes with the same bit length of at least three, $p-1<q-1<2(p-1)$, so this hypothesis fails throughout that family. [Equal-width obstruction](../PrimeFactors/Subgroup.lean) The search takes only $N$. For each integer base $a$ starting at 2, it first takes $\gcd(a,N)$. If that is 1, it computes $r=a^{N-1}\bmod N$ by modular exponentiation and takes $\gcd(r-1,N)$. Any gcd strictly between 1 and $N$ certifies a split.

For this family and $N\ge 3000$, it suffices to search through $(\ln N)^2$. Here is the argument.

1. A prime divisor of $N$ at or below the bound is found by the first gcd. Now suppose every prime at or below the bound is coprime to $N$.
2. Modulo $p-1$, both $p$ and $q$ are 1. Hence $p-1\mid N-1$. Fermat’s little theorem gives $a^{N-1}=1\pmod p$ for every unit $a$.
3. Modulo $q-1$, the remainder of $N-1$ is $p-1$, strictly between 0 and $q-1$. Since the units modulo $q$ are cyclic, some unit has $(N-1)$st power different from 1. The Chinese remainder theorem lifts it to a unit modulo $N$. Thus the units whose $(N-1)$st power equals 1 modulo $q$ form a **proper subgroup** $H$ of the units modulo $N$.
4. Under Dirichlet GRH, Theorem 1.1(2) of Lamzouri, Li and Soundararajan gives a prime base $a\le (\ln N)^2$ outside $H$. At that base $p$ divides $r-1$ and $q$ does not, so the second gcd equals $p$. The base is a modular exponentiation witness. It can be much smaller than either factor. [Theorem 1.1, p. 2](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1309.3595#page=2) · [Dirichlet theorems in the release](https://github.com/leanprover-community/riemann-hypothesis/blob/40e56bab/src/Main.lean)

The executable uses $b^2$ as an integer upper bound, where $b$ is the binary length of $N$. This covers the analytic window because $\ln N<b\ln 2<b$. Scanning all integer bases includes every prime in the bound and requires constant-many working integers. For a composite outside this family, or with a user-supplied smaller cap, exhaustion means only that the tested bases produced no proper gcd. Miller’s primality test also uses small witnesses under Dirichlet GRH. A witness that proves compositeness alone can leave the gcd equal to 1. [Miller’s original paper](https://www.cs.cmu.edu/~glmiller/Publications/Papers/Mi76.pdf)

Lean checks that the subgroup is proper and that its small outside prime yields the stated gcd. The formal theorem takes the finite conclusion `SmallPrimeOutside N bound` as an explicit hypothesis. Proving this hypothesis from the released Dirichlet statements by formalizing Theorem 1.1(2) is the open analytic step. Individual certificates for complete prime factorizations already stand independently of the search bound. [Subgroup and gcd theorems](../PrimeFactors/Subgroup.lean) · [Search implementation](../experiments/subgroup_split.py) · [Factorization certificates](../PrimeFactors/Certificate.lean)

## Divisor sums and exact recovery

Sutherland combined the RH release with the independent Robin1984 project at `acab1a3`. The resulting verified inequality is $\sigma(N)<e^\gamma N\log\log N$ for $N>5040$, where $\sigma$ is the sum of all positive divisors. Robin1984’s original code is available under Apache-2.0, subject to its file-specific notices. [Combined theorem](https://math.mit.edu/~drew/grh/ArithSolution.lean) · [Verification evidence](https://math.mit.edu/~drew/grh/) · [Robin1984 licensing](https://github.com/kimihiro64/Robin1984/blob/main/LICENSING.md)

For distinct primes $p,q$, multiplicativity gives $S=\sigma(N)-N-1=p+q$. With **exact** $\sigma(N)$, the discriminant $S^2-4N$ is $(q-p)^2$ and the roots $(S\pm\sqrt{S^2-4N})/2$ are the two factors. For example, $N=10403$ has $\sigma(N)=10608$, giving $S=204$, discriminant 4 and factors 101 and 103. A fast way to obtain that exact divisor sum from $N$ would itself solve the factorization problem for this family. [Mathlib’s divisor-sum results](https://leanprover-community.github.io/mathlib4_docs/Mathlib/NumberTheory/ArithmeticFunction/Misc.html)

Substituting Robin’s upper bound gives $S<e^\gamma N\log\log N-N-1$. Even at the lower endpoint $N=5041$, the right side exceeds $2N$. The elementary inequality $(p-2)(q-2)\ge 0$ already gives $S\le N/2+2$. The Robin consequence therefore yields no improvement to this search window throughout its applicable range. An input-specific upper bound for $\sigma(N)$ would have to beat the elementary estimate before it could prune additional midpoints.
